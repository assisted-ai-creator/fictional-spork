# Kripa, Drona and Karna

Meanwhile Kripa, best of those who bear weapons, of great valour and courage,
great of spirit, came there, the great chariot fighter, eager for battle, to
fight Arjuna. Those two chariots, bright as the sun, ready to fight, mighty,
stood shining like two autumn clouds.

Partha drew the Gandiva, the highest weapon, famous in the world, and loosed
many iron arrows that pierce the vitals. Before they reached him, Kripa cut
Partha's blood-drinking iron arrows with sharp arrows, by the hundred and the
thousand. Then Partha in his anger, showing wonderful skills, covered the
directions and the quarters with arrows. The great chariot fighter, boundless in
spirit, made the sky as if one shadow everywhere and covered Kripa with hundreds
of arrows. Pierced by those sharp arrows like tongues of fire, Kripa in his
anger quickly pierced the great Partha, of matchless energy, with a thousand
arrows, and roared in the battle.

Then the hero Arjuna, quickly, with four fine sharp arrows with golden feathers
and smooth joints, loosed from the Gandiva, pierced his four horses. Pierced by
those sharp arrows, like blazing snakes, the horses all reared up suddenly, and
Kripa fell from his place. When the joy of the Kurus saw Gautama fallen from his
place, that killer of enemy heroes did not pierce him, respecting his dignity.

Gautama got back to his place and quickly pierced the Left-handed Archer with
ten arrows with heron feathers. Then Partha cut his bow with a single sharp
broad arrow, and took the bow out of his hand. Then with sharp arrows that
pierce the vitals he blew away his armour, but Partha did not hurt his body. As
he came out of his armour, his body shone like a snake's when it sheds its skin
in season. When his bow had been cut by Partha, Gautama took another bow and
strung it, and that was a wonder. But the son of Kunti cut that too with a
smooth-jointed arrow. So, with a sure hand, the Pandava, killer of enemy heroes,
cut many other bows of Sharadvat's son.

With his bow cut, the mighty one took up a spear and hurled it at the son of
Pandu like a blazing thunderbolt. As that spear adorned with gold came through
the sky like a great meteor, Arjuna cut it with ten arrows. Cut into ten pieces
by wise Partha, it fell to the ground. Then Kripa, bow in hand, quickly pierced
Partha with ten sharp broad arrows. Then Partha, full of energy, in his anger,
loosed thirteen arrows sharpened on stone, like fire. With one he cut his yoke,
with four his four horses, and with the sixth he took the driver's head from his
body. With three he cut the three bamboo poles of the chariot, with two the
axles, and with the twelfth, a broad arrow, he cut down his banner. Then, as if
laughing, Phalguna, equal to Indra, struck Kripa in the chest with the
thirteenth, like a thunderbolt.

His bow cut, his chariot gone, his horses and driver killed, Kripa jumped down
with a mace in his hand and quickly hurled it. But that heavy, well-made mace,
thrown by Kripa, was driven back by Arjuna's arrows along the way it had come.
Then warriors, wanting to rescue the unforgiving son of Sharadvat, covered
Partha from every side in the battle with showers of arrows. Then Virata's son
turned the horses to the left, made a double circle, and held those warriors
off. Then those bulls among men took Kripa, who had lost his chariot, and
carried him away at great speed from Dhananjaya, son of Kunti.

---

"Where that golden altar stands raised on a golden staff, bright as a blazing
flame and adorned with flags," said Arjuna, "take me there, bless you, sir, to
Drona's army. His great red horses shine there, fine to drive, like smooth
coral, with copper-coloured mouths, handsome, trained in every skill, yoked to
his fine chariot. Long-armed, full of energy, with strength and beauty, famous
in all the worlds, is the mighty son of Bharadvaja. In understanding he is equal
to Ushanas and in policy to Brihaspati. The four Vedas are established in him,
and celibacy, and all the heavenly weapons with the means of calling them back,
sir, and the whole science of the bow. Patience, self-control, truth, kindness
and honesty, these and many other virtues are in that best of brahmins. I want
to fight that fortunate man in battle. So bring me to the teacher, Uttara, and
drive fast."

When Arjuna said this, Virata's son urged the horses adorned with gold towards
the chariot of Bharadvaja's son. As the Pandava, best of chariot fighters, came
at him fast, Drona went out to meet Partha like a rutting elephant going to meet
another. Then he had his conch blown, with the sound of a hundred kettledrums,
and the whole army was stirred up like the sea. When they saw the fine red
horses mixed in battle with horses the colour of swans, as swift as thought, the
people in the battle were amazed. When they saw those two chariots full of
valour at the head of the battle, teacher and pupil, unconquered, masters of
learning, high-minded, Drona and Partha, the mighty, locked together, the great
army of the Bharatas shook again and again.

Then the mighty Partha, full of joy, the great chariot fighter, as if laughing,
brought his chariot up to Drona's chariot. The strong-armed son of Kunti, killer
of enemy heroes, greeted him and spoke gently, in a smooth voice. "We have lived
out our time in the forest, and we want to pay back what was done to us. You
should not be angry with us, you who are always hard to beat in battle. I shall
strike you only when you have struck me first, sinless one. That is my
intention. Please do so."

Then Drona sent more than twenty arrows at him, and Partha cut them before they
reached him, with a sure hand. Then mighty Drona covered Partha's chariot with a
thousand arrows, quickly showing his weapons. So the battle between Bharadvaja's
son and the diadem-wearer began, both loosing blazing arrows equally in the
fight. Both famous for their deeds, both as swift as the wind, both knowing the
heavenly weapons, both of the highest energy, they threw nets of arrows and
bewildered the kings. All the warriors who had gathered there were amazed and
praised them, saying, "Well done, well done!" as they loosed their arrows
quickly. "Who but Phalguna could fight Drona in battle? This dharma of
kshatriyas is a terrible thing, that he should fight his teacher." So said the
people standing at the head of the battle.

Those two heroes, enraged, close to each other, great chariot fighters,
unconquered, covered each other with masses of arrows. Drawing his great
gold-backed bow, hard to face, Bharadvaja's son fought Phalguna in his fury.
With nets of shining arrows washed on stone he hid the light of the sun from
Arjuna's chariot. The strong-armed great chariot fighter pierced Partha with
sharp, swift arrows, as a cloud pierces a mountain with rain. In the same way
the Pandava, bold, took up the heavenly Gandiva, the best bow, killer of
enemies, swift, able to bear any strain, and loosed many bright arrows worked
with gold. The mighty one destroyed Bharadvaja's showers of arrows with arrows
loosed quickly from his bow, and that was a wonder. Moving on his chariot,
Partha Dhananjaya, a sight to see, showed all his weapons in every direction at
once. He made the sky one shadow all round with his arrows, and Drona could not
be seen, as if covered with mist. Covered with the finest arrows, he looked like
a mountain blazing on every side.

When Drona saw his own chariot covered with Partha's arrows in the battle, he
drew his bright bow, with a sound like thunder. Drawing that terrible, highest
weapon, like a wheel of fire, Drona, glory of the assembly, scattered those
arrows, and there was a great noise, like bamboos burning. With the golden
feathers of his arrows flying from his fine bright bow, he of boundless spirit
covered the directions and the light of the sun. Then flights of arrows with
golden feathers and smooth joints could be seen in great numbers in the sky.
Drona's arrows, fixed feather to point as they came from his bow, looked in the
sky like a single long arrow joined together. So those two heroes, loosing great
arrows worked with gold, covered the sky as if with meteors. The arrows of the
two, fitted with heron and peacock feathers, shone like lines of swans moving in
the sky in autumn.

The fight between those two great men there was furious and terrible, like that
of Vritra and Vasava. Like two elephants meeting with the points of their tusks,
they struck each other with arrows loosed from bows drawn full. Those two
heroes, enraged, glories of the battlefield, fought on, using the heavenly
weapons in turn. Then Arjuna, best of victors, held off with sharp arrows the
stone-whetted arrows the chief of teachers loosed. Showing himself fierce, of
fierce valour, Indra's son quickly covered the sky with many arrows.

As Arjuna, tiger among men, of piercing energy, tried to kill him, Drona, chief
of teachers, best of those who bear weapons, played with Arjuna in the battle
with smooth-jointed arrows. As Bharadvaja's son loosed heavenly weapons in the
great battle, Phalguna held off weapons with weapons and fought him. Between
those two angry lions among men, unforgiving towards each other, there was a
clash like that of gods and danavas. The weapons of Indra, of Vayu and of Agni,
each time Drona loosed them, the Pandava swallowed again and again with weapons
of his own. So those two heroes, great archers, loosing sharp arrows, made the
sky one shadow with their showers of arrows.

Then the sound of the arrows Arjuna loosed, falling on bodies, was heard like
thunderbolts falling on mountains. Elephants, chariots and horsemen could be
seen smeared with blood, lord of the people, like flame-of-the-forest trees in
flower. With arms fallen, still in their armlets, with fine great chariots, with
armour bright with gold and with banners brought down, and with warriors killed
there, crushed by Partha's arrows, the army was thrown into confusion at the
meeting of Drona and Arjuna.

Those two heroes, shaking their bows that could bear any strain, covered each
other with arrows, each trying to outlast the other. Then a cry rose in the sky
from those praising Drona. "Drona has done a hard thing in fighting Arjuna, that
crusher, of great valour, of firm grip, hard to face, the conqueror of gods,
daityas and snakes, the great chariot fighter." Seeing Partha's tirelessness,
his training, his lightness of hand and the distance of his shots in battle,
Drona was filled with wonder.

Then the unforgiving Partha raised the heavenly Gandiva and drew it with both
arms in the battle, bull of the Bharatas. His rain of arrows spread like
locusts, and not even the wind could pass between his arrows. As he fitted and
loosed his arrows without pause, no gap could be seen, even as he took them. So,
as the terrible battle of swift weapons went on, Partha loosed other arrows,
faster and faster. Then hundreds of thousands of smooth-jointed arrows fell at
once on Drona's chariot. As Drona was covered with arrows by the archer of the
Gandiva, a great cry of dismay rose from the armies, bull of the Bharatas.
Maghavan praised the Pandava's swiftness with weapons, and so did the gandharvas
and apsarases who had gathered there.

Then the teacher's son, leader of a host of chariots, suddenly held the Pandava
back with a great crowd of chariots. Ashvatthaman in his heart honoured that
deed of the great Partha's, and was also filled with great anger at him. Seized
by rage, he ran at Partha in the battle, scattering thousands of arrows like a
cloud pouring rain. The strong-armed Partha turned his horses towards Drona's
son and gave Drona room to get away. Drona took the chance and quickly went away
with his swift horses, his armour and banner cut, the hero wounded by the finest
arrows.

---

Partha met him as he came on like a storm of wind, pouring a great net of arrows
like a raining cloud. The clash between those two, throwing nets of arrows, was
great, like that of the gods and asuras, like Vritra and Vasava. The sun did not
shine and the wind did not blow, when the sky was thick with arrows and made one
shadow everywhere. There was a great crackling as the two warriors struck each
other, like bamboos burning, conqueror of enemy cities.

Arjuna brought all his horses near to death, and Ashvatthaman, dazed, king,
could not tell one direction from another. Then Drona's son, of great valour,
seeing a tiny gap as Partha moved about, cut his bowstring with a razor arrow.
The gods praised him when they saw that superhuman deed. Then Drona's son,
drawing back eight bow lengths from that bull among men, struck Partha again in
the heart with heron-feathered arrows. Then strong-armed Partha, laughing aloud,
strung the Gandiva with a new string by force. Then Partha turned his chariot in
a half moon and met him, like a rutting elephant, leader of a herd, meeting
another rutting elephant.

Then a great battle began between those two heroes of the earth, in the middle
of the field, only the two of them, making the hair stand on end. All the Kurus
watched those two heroes with wonder, the great men fighting like two leaders of
herds that have met. Those two heroes, bulls among men, struck each other with
arrows like venomous snakes, like blazing serpents. The great Pandava had two
heavenly quivers that never ran out, and so the hero Partha stood in the battle
like a mountain that does not move. But Ashvatthaman's arrows, as he shot them
quickly in the battle, soon ran out, and so Arjuna had the better of him.

Then Karna, drawing his great bow with greater fury, twanged it, and there was a
great cry of dismay. Partha turned his eyes to where the bow was being drawn and
saw the son of Radha there, and his anger grew very great. Seized by rage and
wanting to kill him, the bull of the Kurus looked at Karna alone with rolling
eyes. As Partha turned away like this, men quickly brought Drona's son arrows by
the thousand, king. Leaving Drona's son, strong-armed Dhananjaya, conqueror of
rivals, suddenly ran at Karna alone. Running at him, the son of Kunti, his eyes
red with anger, wanting single combat, said this.

---

"Karna, the time has come for all the boasting you did in the middle of the
hall," said Arjuna, "that no one was equal to you in battle. You spoke harsh
words, abandoning dharma altogether. But I think what you want to do now will be
hard to do. What you said before, without ever meeting me, do it today, son of
Radha, with me in the middle of the Kurus. You watched Panchali being tormented
in the hall by wicked men. Get the full fruit of that today. What I bore before,
bound by the noose of dharma, son of Radha, now see the victory of that anger of
mine in battle. Come, Karna, take up the fight with me. Let all the Kurus with
their soldiers be the spectators."

"Do in deeds what you say in words, Partha," said Karna. "It is famous on earth
that deeds outrun words. What you bore before, you bore because you had no power
to do otherwise. That is what I take it to be, Partha, seeing you so lacking in
valour. If you bore it before because you were bound by the noose of dharma, you
think yourself free now, bound as you still are. If you have really lived in the
forest as was agreed, then, knowing dharma and profit as you do, you are worn
out and want to break the agreement. If Shakra himself fought for you, Partha, I
would feel no fear in showing my valour. This wish of yours has come true before
long, son of Kunti. You will fight with me, and today you will see my strength."

"Just now you ran away from me in battle," said Arjuna, "and that is why you are
alive, son of Radha, though your younger brother was killed. Having had his
brother killed and left the head of the battle, what man but you would speak
like this among good men?"

Saying this to Karna, the unconquered Bibhatsu went at him, loosing arrows that
pierce armour. Karna met those arrows like tongues of fire with a great shower
of arrows, like a raining cloud. Terrible nets of arrows flew up on every side.
He pierced Partha's horses, his arms and his hand guard, one by one. Partha, not
bearing it, cut the strap of Karna's quiver with a sharp-pointed, smooth-jointed
arrow. Karna took other arrows from his quiver and pierced the Pandava in the
hand, and his fist loosened. Then strong-armed Partha cut Karna's bow. Karna
hurled a spear at him, and Partha shattered it with arrows.

Then many of the son of Radha's followers fell on him, and he sent them to
Yama's house with arrows loosed from the Gandiva. Then Bibhatsu struck his
horses with sharp arrows able to bear any strain, loosed from bows drawn to the
ear, and they fell dead on the ground. Then the mighty, strong-armed son of
Kunti pierced Karna in the chest with another sharp, blazing arrow. It split his
armour and went into his body. Then darkness came over him and he knew nothing.
In great pain he left the battle and went away towards the north. Then Arjuna
shouted, and so did Uttara, the great chariot fighter.
