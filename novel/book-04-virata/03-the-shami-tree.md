# The Shami Tree

The heroes, with swords bound on, carrying their weapons and quivers, their
lizard-skin finger guards tied on, went along the Kalindi. They followed its
southern bank on foot, living in mountain strongholds and forest strongholds,
the archers, shooting all kinds of deer, the great archers of great strength.
North of the Dasharnas and south of the Panchalas, between the Yakrilomas and
the Shurasenas, the Pandavas came out of the forest into the country of the
Matsyas, calling themselves hunters.

When they reached the settled country, Krishna said to the king, "Look,
footpaths can be seen, and fields of many kinds. Virata's capital must still be
far away. Let us spend the rest of the night here. I am very tired."

"Dhananjaya," said Yudhishthira, "lift up Panchali and carry her, Bharata. We
shall stay in the capital, and be free of the forest."

Arjuna took Draupadi up quickly, like a king of elephants, and when he came near
the city he set her down. When he reached the capital, the son of Kunti said to
Arjuna, "Where shall we hang our weapons before we go into the city? If we go
into the city with our weapons, my son, we shall alarm these people, no doubt of
it. And then we must go into the forest again for twelve years. For so we
promised, even if one of us is recognised."

"Here on this height, lord of men, is a great, dense shami tree," said Arjuna,
"with fearsome branches, hard to climb, near the cremation ground. No one comes
here, king. It grows off the road in the forest, haunted by deer and beasts of
prey. Let us hang our weapons in it and go to the city. Then we can live here as
we please, Bharata."

When he had said this to King Yudhishthira, whose self was dharma, he set about
putting away the weapons, bull of the Bharatas. Partha unstrung Gandiva, the
generous bow with the great voice, which destroys hosts of enemies and takes
away fear. With it the joy of the Kurus had beaten gods, men and snakes with a
single chariot, and conquered other rich countries.

Yudhishthira, the heroic scorcher of enemies, took the unfailing string from the
bow with which he had guarded Kurukshetra.

Bhimasena took the noose of the string from his bow. With it the lord had beaten
the Panchalas in battle, and held back many enemies alone in his conquest of the
quarters. At its twang enemies fled in battle, as at the splitting of a mountain
or the crash of a thunderbolt. With it, sinless one, he had laid hands on the
king of Sindhu.

The Pandava who had conquered the western quarter with his bow, the hero who
roars in battle, drew off its string.

Sahadeva, the hero of courteous conduct, unstrung the weapon with which the lord
had conquered the southern quarter.

With the bows they put away their long, yellow swords, their costly quivers, and
their arrows with calf-tooth heads and razor edges. Nakula climbed the tree and
put away the bows himself, in the places he thought were strong. Where he saw
that the rain would not fall on them, he bound them fast with strong cords.

The Pandavas tied a single corpse there too. "People will keep far away from
this shami when they smell the stench and think, 'A corpse is tied here.'" To
the herdsmen and shepherds they told the same, the scorchers of enemies: "This
is our mother, a hundred and eighty years old. It is the custom of our family,
followed by our forefathers, to hang the dead in a tree." So the Parthas,
destroyers of enemies, came near the city.

Yudhishthira gave them secret names: Jaya, Jayanta, Vijaya, Jayatsena and
Jayadbala. Then, in the disguises they had promised, they went into the great
city, to live the thirteenth year in that kingdom unrecognised.

**Divergences.**

* **The hymn to Durga.** A rejected appendix passage (@4) has Yudhishthira praise the goddess Durga in his mind as he goes into Virata's city, and the goddess appear and promise them victory. It is not in the CE, and not narrated. See L-163.
* **The virtues of the shami.** A rejected appendix passage (@2) praises the shami as a tree that removes sin and brings victory. Not narrated.
* **Dhaumya and Indrasena.** Rejected passages (\*139, \*140) say where Dhaumya and the charioteers went. Not narrated here; the CE gives Yudhishthira's arrangements at 4.4.2–4.
* **Nakula's name.** Rejected lines (\*167–\*168) explain Nakula's name ("none in the family is his equal in beauty"). Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*130–\*138, \*141–\*166, \*169–\*176, @3) add descriptions and speeches. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 4.5.1 *kālindīm*: "the Kalindi", the Yamuna.
* 4.5.4 *yakṛllomān*: "the Yakrilomas", a people. *lubdhā bruvāṇāḥ*: "calling themselves hunters".
* 4.5.5 *ekapadyaḥ*: "footpaths".
* 4.5.12 *kūṭe*: "on this height".
* 4.5.18–23: each brother unstrings his own bow; the text names Yudhishthira, Bhima and Sahadeva, and describes the fourth as the Pandava who conquered the west. In the Sabha Parva that was Nakula (2.29.1–2). The novel keeps the text's description.
* 4.5.21 *saindhavaṃ yena rājānaṃ parāmṛṣata*: "with which he had laid hands on the king of Sindhu", Jayadratha (3.256).
* 4.5.26: "Where he saw that the rain would not fall on them". The sense is uncertain.
* 4.5.28 *aśītiśatavarṣeyam*: "a hundred and eighty years old".
* 4.5.30: the secret names are given without saying which brother has which.
