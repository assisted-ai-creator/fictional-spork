# Virata's Dice

Having won back the wealth, Virata, lord of the army, entered the city gladly
with the four Pandavas. Having beaten the Trigartas in battle and brought back
all the cattle, the great king shone with the Parthas, surrounded with glory. As
the hero sat on his throne, raising the joy of his friends, all his subjects
came to him with the brahmins. Honoured by them with his army, the king of the
Matsyas thanked them, and then sent the brahmins and the subjects away.

Then Virata, king of the Matsyas, lord of the army, asked after Uttara. "Where
has he gone?" he said. The women and girls in the house and those who lived in
the women's quarters told him gladly, "The Kurus have taken the cattle.
Bhuminjaya, furious, has gone out alone, with too much daring, to win them back,
with Brihannada to help him. He has gone against six great chariot fighters who
have come, Drona, Shantanu's son, Kripa, Karna, Duryodhana and Drona's son."

When he heard that his son had gone on a single chariot, with Brihannada, the
pride of the assembly, as his charioteer, King Virata was deeply troubled. He
said to all his chief counsellors, "When the Kurus and the other kings of the
earth hear that the Trigartas have been beaten, they will not stand their ground
at all. So let my warriors who were not wounded by the Trigartas go with a great
army to rescue Uttara." He quickly sent out horses, elephants and chariots,
companies of foot soldiers and the bravest heroes, equipped with bright weapons
and ornaments, for his son's sake. So Virata, king of the Matsyas, lord of an
army, quickly ordered out that army of four parts. "Find out quickly whether the
prince is alive or not. I think a man whose driver is a eunuch cannot be alive."

The Dharma King laughed and said to Virata, who was distressed and tormented by
the Kurus, "If Brihannada is his charioteer, lord of men, the enemy will not
carry off your cattle today. With that charioteer to drive him well, your son
can conquer all the kings together, and the Kurus, and gods, asuras, yakshas and
snakes too, in battle."

Then the swift messengers Uttara had sent reached Virata's city and told of the
victory. A minister told the king of that great victory, of the defeat of the
Kurus and that Uttara was on his way. "All the cattle have been won back and the
Kurus are beaten. Uttara is safe, with his driver, scorcher of enemies."

"How lucky that your cattle have been won back and the Kurus beaten," said
Kanka. "How lucky that your son is alive, as we hear, bull among kings. But I do
not think it any wonder that your son has beaten the Kurus. Victory is certain
for anyone whose driver is Brihannada."

Then King Virata, his hair standing on end with joy at hearing of the victory of
the prince of boundless energy, gave the messengers clothes and gave orders to
his ministers. "Let the royal roads be decorated with flags for me, and let all
the gods be worshipped with offerings of flowers. Let the princes and the chief
warriors, the courtesans in their finery, and all the music go out to meet my
son. Let a man with a bell and drum mount a rutting elephant quickly and
announce my victory at every crossroads. And let Uttara, with many girls around
her, in bright clothes and ornaments, go out to meet Brihannada."

When they heard the king's words, all the people, with auspicious signs in their
hands, with kettledrums, trumpets and conches, and lovely women in the finest
clothes, sutas with magadhas, drummers and players of instruments, went out of
the city of mighty Virata to meet his son of endless valour.

---

When he had sent out the army, the girls and the courtesans in their finery, the
wise king of the Matsyas said in his joy, "Bring the dice, sairandhri. Kanka,
let us play."

When he heard him say this, the Pandava answered, "We have heard that one should
not play with a gambler who is excited. I dare not play with you today, when you
are so full of joy. But I want to do what pleases you. Let it go ahead, if you
think so."

"Women, cattle, gold and any other wealth I have," said Virata, "none of it is
safe from you, even without playing."

"What do you want with dice, king of kings, giver of honour, with all their
faults?" said Kanka. "There are many faults in playing, so one should avoid it.
You must have heard of, or seen, Yudhishthira the Pandava. He lost his great,
rich kingdom and his godlike brothers, everything, at dice. So I do not like
dice. Or yet, if you think so, king, let us play, if you like."

As the game went on, the Matsya said to the Pandava, "Look, such Kurus as those
have been beaten in battle by my son."

Then Yudhishthira, son of Dharma, said to the king of the Matsyas, "How could
anyone whose driver is Brihannada not win?"

At this the king, the Matsya, angry, said to the Pandava, "You wretch of a
brahmin, do you praise a eunuch equally with my son? You do not know what should
and should not be said. You must be despising me. Why should he not beat all of
them, with Bhishma and Drona at their head? Because you are my friend, brahmin,
I forgive you this offence. But you must never speak like this again, if you
want to live."

"Where Drona is, and Bhishma, and Drona's son, Vaikartana, Kripa, King
Duryodhana, and other great chariot fighters," said Yudhishthira, "or Shatakratu
himself surrounded by the hosts of the Maruts, who but Brihannada could fight
them all together?"

"You have been told again and again," said Virata, "and still you do not hold
your tongue. If there were no one to punish, no one would follow dharma."

Then the king, in great anger, struck Yudhishthira hard in the face with a die
and scolded him in his fury. "Not like that!" Struck hard, he bled from the
nose. Before it could reach the ground, Partha caught it in his hands. And the
righteous one looked at Draupadi, who stood beside him. She knew what he meant,
for she followed her husband's thoughts. The faultless woman filled a bowl of
bronze and gold and caught in it the blood that flowed from the Pandava.

---

Then Uttara, sprinkled with sweet scents and garlands of many kinds, came
happily into the city at his ease. Honoured by the townspeople, the women and
the country folk, he reached the door of the palace and sent word in to his
father. Then the doorkeeper went in and said to Virata, "Your son Uttara is
standing at the door with Brihannada."

The king of the Matsyas, delighted, said to the steward, "Bring them both in
quickly. I want to see them."

But the king of the Kurus whispered softly in the steward's ear, "Let Uttara
come in alone. Brihannada must not be brought in. For that strong-armed one has
taken a vow that anyone who wounds my body, or even draws my blood, except in
battle, shall not live. No doubt of it. In his great anger he would not bear it
if he saw me bleeding. He would kill Virata here, with his counsellors, his army
and his mounts."

Then the king's eldest son, Prithivinjaya, came in. He greeted his father's feet
and saw the Dharma King, bleeding, faultless, his mind elsewhere, sitting on the
ground apart, with the sairandhri waiting on him. Then Uttara asked his father
in haste, "Who struck him, king? Who did this wicked thing?"

"I struck this crooked man," said Virata, "and he deserves worse than that. When
you, a hero, were being praised, he praised the eunuch."

"You have done a wrong thing, king," said Uttara. "Win his favour quickly, or
the terrible poison of a brahmin will burn you up, root and all."

When he heard his son's words, Virata, increaser of the kingdom, begged the
pardon of the son of Kunti, who was like a fire covered with ashes. The Pandava
answered the king as he begged pardon. "I forgave it long ago, king. There is no
anger in me. If this blood from my nose had fallen on the ground, you and your
kingdom would have been destroyed, great king, no doubt of it. I do not blame
you, king. But a man who strikes one who does no wrong, great king, soon meets a
strong and terrible fate."

When the bleeding had stopped, Brihannada came in. He greeted Virata and Kanka
and stood waiting.

When he had begged the Kauravya's pardon, the Matsya praised Uttara, back from
the battle, while the Left-handed Archer listened. "Through you I have an heir,
joy of Kaikeyi. There has never been, and never will be, a son like you for me.
How did you meet with Karna, dear son, who could walk a thousand steps without
missing one? How did you meet with Bhishma, dear son, who has no equal in the
whole world of men, unshakeable as the sea, unbearable as the fire at the end of
time? How did you meet with Drona, dear son, the brahmin who was teacher of the
Vrishni heroes and the Pandavas, the teacher of all the kshatriyas, best of all
who bear weapons? How did you meet with the teacher's son, the hero called
Ashvatthama, among all who bear weapons? How did you meet with Kripa, dear son,
at the sight of whom men sink in battle like merchants robbed of their wealth?
How did you meet with Duryodhana, dear son, the prince who could pierce a
mountain with his great arrows?"

"I did not win back the cattle," said Uttara, "and I did not beat the enemy.
That whole deed was done by some son of a god. When I was frightened and running
away, that god's son stopped me. He stood on the chariot, a young man like the
one with the thunderbolt in his hand. He won back the cattle. He beat the Kurus.
That deed was that hero's, father. I did not do it. He turned back Sharadvat's
son, Drona and Drona's son, the suta's son and Bhishma with his arrows, mighty
as he was. And to Duryodhana in the battle, broken like the leader of a herd of
elephants, frightened, the mighty prince, he said, 'I see no refuge for you in
Hastinapura. Save your life by fighting, son of the Kauravas. You will not get
away by running, king. Set your mind on battle. If you win, you will enjoy the
earth. If you are killed, you will reach heaven.' That tiger among men turned
back, loosing arrows like thunderbolts, the king surrounded by his counsellors,
hissing on his chariot like a snake. Then my hair stood on end and my thighs
went stiff, sir, when he scattered with his arrows that army like a mass of
clouds. When he had driven off that army of chariots, the mighty young man, with
the build of a lion, laughed and took the Kurus' clothes, king. Six chariot
fighters were surrounded by that one hero, like deer that graze on grass in the
forest by a rutting tiger."

"Where is that strong-armed hero, the famous son of a god, who won back my
wealth in battle when the Kurus had taken it?" said Virata. "I want to see him
and honour that mighty son of a god, who saved you and the cattle for me."

"The mighty son of a god has vanished, father," said Uttara. "But I think he
will appear tomorrow or the day after."

So, even as he was being described, Virata did not recognise Partha Arjuna, the
Pandava, living there hidden in his disguise. Then Partha, with the great
Virata's leave, himself gave the cloths to Virata's daughter. The lovely Uttara
took those fine, costly cloths of many kinds and was delighted. Then the son of
Kunti talked in secret with Uttara about everything that was to be done about
King Yudhishthira. And the bull of the Bharatas, delighted, did it all properly
with the son of the Matsya, bull among men.
