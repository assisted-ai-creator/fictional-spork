# The Trigartas' Raid

Then Susharma, king of the Trigartas, leader of a host of chariots, spoke up in
great haste with words that suited the time. He had been harmed many times
before by the Matsyas and the Salveyas, and again and again by the Matsya's
suta, Kichaka, who had oppressed him and his kin by force, lord. Glancing at
Karna, he said to Duryodhana.

"My kingdom has been attacked many times by the king of the Matsyas with all his
power. His commander was mighty Kichaka, cruel, hot-tempered, wicked, famous on
earth for his valour. That evil-doer, that brute, has been killed there by
gandharvas. Now that he is dead, king, I think Virata will lose his pride. He
will have no one to lean on, and no spirit left. I think we should march there,
if it pleases you, sinless one, and all the Kauravas, and great-souled Karna. I
think this is the thing to do now, pressing and useful. Let us attack his
kingdom quickly, full of grain. Let us take his jewels and riches of many kinds.
Or we shall take his villages and lands and share them out. Or else we shall
squeeze his city by force and carry off many thousands of his fine cattle of
every kind. Let us join with the Kauravas and the Trigartas, lord of the people,
and take his cattle quickly, all of us, closely united. Or we can make a treaty
with him and tie down his manhood. Or we can kill his whole army and bring him
under our power. When we have brought him under our power as is right, we shall
live in comfort. And your strength will grow, no doubt of it."

When he heard these words, Karna said to the king, "Susharma has spoken well,
words that suit the time and do us good. So let us march out quickly, harnessing
the army and dividing its divisions, or as you think best, sinless one. Our wise
grandfather, the elder of the Kurus, and our teacher Drona, and Kripa son of
Sharadvat, let the march be arranged as they all think fit. Let us consult them
and go quickly to win the king's goal. What have we to do with the Pandavas, who
have lost their wealth, strength and manhood? Either they are lost for good, or
they have gone to Yama's house. Let us go without fear, king, to Virata's
country. We shall take his cattle and riches of many kinds."

King Duryodhana took up those words of Vaikartana Karna's, and at once himself
gave the order to Duhshasana, next to him in age, always ready to carry out his
commands. "Consult the elders and harness the army quickly. We shall all go
together with all the Kauravas to the place we have chosen. Let the great
chariot fighter Susharma go to the place agreed, the king with the Trigartas,
with all his strength and his mounts. Let him go first, well armoured, to the
Matsya's country. We shall follow a day behind, closely united, into the Matsya
king's rich country. Let them go there fast, towards Virata's city, fall on the
herdsmen quickly and take the great wealth, hundreds of thousands of fine
cattle. We too will divide our army in two and seize them."

So King Susharma went in the direction agreed, the direction of Fire, and took
the cattle on the seventh day of the fortnight. On the next day, the eighth,
king, all the Kauravas together seized the herds, thousands of them.

---

By then the time had passed completely for the great Pandavas, of boundless
energy, who had come in under false marks and were living in that fine city,
doing King Virata's work. At the end of that thirteenth year, Bharata, Susharma
had seized a great wealth of cattle by force.

Then a herdsman came to the city at great speed. He jumped down from his
chariot, wearing earrings, and saw the king of the Matsyas surrounded by brave
warriors wearing earrings and armlets, with his good counsellors, and with the
Pandavas, bulls among men. He came up to the great king, the increaser of the
kingdom, as he sat in the hall, bowed and said to Virata, "The Trigartas have
beaten us in battle, and insulted us and our kin. They are driving off your
cattle, hundreds of thousands of them. Get them back, lord of men, so that your
herds are not lost."

When he heard this, the king harnessed the army of the Matsyas, crowded with
chariots, elephants and horses, thronged with foot soldiers and banners. The
kings and princes put on armour there, shining, bright-coloured and well made,
each his own. Virata's dear brother Shatanika put on a coat of armour of
burnished gold with steel and iron inside. Madirashva, Shatanika's younger
brother, put on a strong mail of pure iron, with a fine covering. The king of
the Matsyas put on an armour marked with a hundred suns, a hundred circles, a
hundred dots and a hundred eyes, as good as unbreakable. Suryadatta put on a
gold-backed coat, bright as the sun, with a hundred lotuses and fragrant water
lilies raised on it. Virata's eldest son, the hero Shankha, put on a strong
white mail with iron inside, with a hundred eyes. So in their hundreds the great
chariot fighters, godlike fighters, armed themselves for battle, each in his own
armour. The great chariot fighters yoked horses in golden harness to their fine,
bright, great chariots, each to his own. Then the Matsya's great golden banner,
like the sun or the moon, was raised on his heavenly chariot. And the brave
kshatriyas set up on their chariots other banners of many shapes, adorned with
gold, each his own.

Then the king of the Matsyas said to Shatanika, his younger brother, "Kanka,
Ballava, the cowherd and valiant Damagranthi would fight, I think. I have no
doubt of it. Let them too be given chariots with banners and flags. Let them put
on bright-coloured armour, strong and supple, and let them be given weapons.
These men have the limbs and looks of heroes, like the trunks of king elephants.
My mind tells me they cannot fail to fight."

When he heard the king's words, Shatanika quickly ordered chariots for the
Parthas, king, for Sahadeva, for the king, for Bhima and for Nakula. The
charioteers, glad and full of loyalty to the king, quickly harnessed the
chariots the lord of men had named. Virata gave them bright-coloured armour,
strong and supple, and those tireless scorchers of enemies put it on and armed
themselves.

Swift, disguised, all skilled in war, the bulls of the Kurus followed behind
Virata together, the four brave brothers, Pandavas of true valour. Terrible
rutting elephants followed the king, their temples broken open, streaming like
clouds, with fine tusks, sixty years old, well mounted by skilled and trained
elephant riders, like moving mountains. Eight thousand chariots, a thousand
elephants and sixty thousand horses of the Matsyas marched out, skilled,
obedient, glad followers. That army of Virata's shone as it marched, bull of the
Bharatas, great king, going to follow the tracks of the cattle. That fine force
of Virata's shone as it set out, crowded with men with strong weapons, with
elephants, horses and chariots.

---

The Matsya warriors marched out of the city in battle order and reached the
Trigartas when the sun was low. Trigartas and Matsyas, enraged, mad for battle,
mighty men greedy for the cattle, roared at each other. Terrible rutting
elephants, urged on by lances and goads, were ridden by skilled elephant riders,
chiefs of villages. Their clash was terrible and tumultuous, king, making the
hair stand on end, like the battle of the gods and asuras, as the sun hung low.

Dust rose from the ground, so that nothing could be seen. Birds fell to earth,
covered by the dust of the armies. The sun vanished behind the crossing arrows,
and the sky shone as if full of fireflies. The gold-backed bows of the archers,
heroes of the world who fell as they shot to right and left, became tangled
together. Chariot met chariot, foot soldier met foot soldier, horseman met
horseman, and great elephant met elephant. With swords, axes, spears, darts and
lances they struck each other in their fury in battle, king. Heroes with arms
like iron bars killed each other in battle, but in their fury they could not
make the brave turn back.

A head could be seen cut off, with trimmed moustache and fine nose, the hair
trimmed, adorned, with its earrings, fouled with dust. There in that great
battle the limbs of kshatriyas could be seen, cut to pieces by arrows, like the
trunks of sal trees. The ground there was strewn with arms like the coils of
snakes, smeared with sandal, and with heads still wearing their earrings. The
dust of the earth settled as the blood flowed. A terrible stupor came over them,
and the fighting went on without any limit.

Shatanika killed a hundred and Vishalaksha four hundred. Those two great chariot
fighters went into the great army of the Trigartas and came to grips in their
fury, hair to hair and nail to nail. Seeing that they had gone into the
Trigartas' mass of chariots, Suryadatta and Madirashva went in behind them.
There in the battle, Virata killed five hundred chariot fighters, and hundreds
of horses, and five great chariot fighters. That leader of a host of chariots,
moving along many tracks among the chariots, came up against Susharma of the
Trigartas, with his golden chariot, in the battle. Those two great-souled,
mighty ones fought there, roaring at each other like two bulls in a cow pen.
Then the two chariot fighters wheeled round each other in their chariots and
quickly loosed arrows like clouds loosing rain. They moved about in great fury,
unforgiving, masters of weapons, carrying swords, darts and maces, with sharp
arrows. Then the king pierced Susharma with ten arrows, and each of his four
horses with five. Susharma, mad for battle, a master of the highest weapons,
pierced the king of the Matsyas with fifty sharp arrows in the same way. Then,
as the armies closed round the king of the Matsyas and Susharma, in the dust of
evening, they could no longer make each other out.

---

When the world was flooded with darkness and dust, Bharata, the warriors stood
still for a while in their battle lines. Then the moon rose, driving away the
darkness, making the night clear, and gladdening the kshatriyas in the battle.
Then, when the light had come, the fighting began again, terrible to see, and
they had no eyes for anything else.

Then Susharma the Trigarta, with his younger brother, charged the king of the
Matsyas with his whole mass of chariots. The two brothers, bulls among
kshatriyas, leapt down from their chariots and ran in great fury, maces in hand,
at his horses. Their forces too ran at each other in anger, with maces and
swords and axes, and with spears with sharp points and well-tempered edges.
Susharma, king of the Trigartas, crushed and beat the whole army of the Matsya
with his army, and, overpowering the Matsya, ran at mighty Virata. The two of
them killed each of his two horses and the two drivers at the side. They took
the king of the Matsyas alive, without his chariot. Susharma carried him off
like a weeping bride, put him on his own chariot and drove off fast.

When Virata, the stronger, had been taken without his chariot, the Matsyas ran
in fear, hard pressed by the Trigartas. As they were being put to flight,
Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, spoke to strong-armed Bhimasena, tamer of enemies.
"The king of the Matsyas has been seized by Susharma the Trigarta. Set him free,
strong-armed one. He must not fall into the power of his enemies. We have all
lived here in comfort, honoured with everything we could wish. Bhimasena, you
must repay that stay."

"I shall rescue him at your command, king," said Bhimasena. "See the great deed
I shall do, fighting with our enemies. Relying on the strength of your own arms,
stand aside with our brothers, king, and see my valour today. This great tree
with its fine trunk stands here like a club. I shall pull it up and put our
enemies to flight with it."

As he looked at the tree like a rutting elephant, the Dharma King Yudhishthira
said to his brave brother, "Do not do anything rash, Bhima. Let the tree stand.
People must not see you doing superhuman deeds with a tree and realise, 'This is
Bhima,' Bharata. Take some other weapon, a human one, a bow, or a spear, or a
sword, or an axe. Take whatever weapon is human and would not be noticed by
others, Bhima, and set the king free quickly. The mighty twins will guard your
wheels, dear brother, in the battle, while you try to rescue the king of the
Matsyas."

Then all of them together urged on their horses. In their anger at the Trigartas
they made use of divine weapons. When it saw the Pandavas turn their chariots,
Virata's great army, in the greatest anger, fought wonderfully. Yudhishthira,
son of Kunti, killed a thousand there. Bhima showed seven hundred warriors the
other world. Nakula too sent seven hundred with his arrows. At Yudhishthira's
order, mighty Sahadeva, bull among men, killed three hundred heroes, breaking
through that great army of the Trigartas, bull among men.

Then King Yudhishthira, the great chariot fighter, ran at Susharma in haste and
wounded him hard with arrows. Susharma too, in a great rage, quickly pierced
Yudhishthira with nine arrows and his four horses with four. Then Vrikodara, son
of Kunti, quick in action, came up to Susharma and smashed his horses, king. He
killed the two who guarded his back with fine arrows, and then in his anger he
struck the driver off the seat of the chariot. The guard of his wheel, a brave
man named Shonashva, saw the single combat and left the Trigarta in fear.

Then Virata leapt down from Susharma's chariot, took hold of Susharma's mace and
struck him with it, mighty as he was. He moved about, mace in hand, old as he
was, like a young man. Bhima, terrible to see, wearing earrings, leapt down from
his chariot and seized the king of the Trigartas, as a lion seizes a small deer.

When that great chariot fighter of the Trigartas had been taken without his
chariot, the whole army of the Trigartas broke, stricken with fear. Then the
mighty sons of Pandu turned back all the cattle. Having beaten Susharma and
taken all the riches, strong in the strength of their own arms, restrained by
modesty, keeping their vows, they spent that night happily in the middle of the
battlefield.

---

Then Virata honoured the great chariot fighters, the sons of Kunti, whose valour
was more than human, with wealth and respect. "My jewels are as much yours as
mine," said Virata. "All of you, do with them as you please, as you like. I
shall give you girls, finely adorned, and riches of many kinds, and whatever
your hearts desire, slayers of enemies. By your valour I am free today and safe
here. So you are all lords of the Matsyas."

When the Matsya spoke like this, the Kauravas, with Yudhishthira at their head,
each joined his hands and said, "We welcome all you say, lord of the people. We
are content with this alone, that you have been freed from your enemies today."

Then the king of the Matsyas, strong-armed Virata, best of kings, spoke again to
Yudhishthira in his joy. "Come, I shall consecrate you. Be king of the Matsyas
for us. Whatever your heart desires, slayer of enemies, I shall give you all of
it. You deserve everything from us. Jewels, cattle, gold, gems and pearls.
Vaiyaghrapadya, best of brahmins, homage to you in every way. Because of you I
see my kingdom today, and my own life, and the enemy who started this has been
brought into my power."

Then Yudhishthira spoke to the Matsya again. "I welcome your words. You speak
pleasantly, Matsya. May you always be devoted to kindness, and always happy. Let
messengers go quickly to your city, king, to tell your friends the good news and
proclaim your victory."

At his word the king of the Matsyas gave orders to messengers. "Go to the city
and announce my victory in battle. Let girls and boys, finely dressed, come out
of my city to meet me, with all the music, and the courtesans in their finery."

The messengers went through the whole night, and towards sunrise they proclaimed
the victory near Virata's city.

**Divergences.**

* **Susharma made to call himself a slave.** In a rejected appendix passage (@32), Bhima binds Susharma, drags him to Yudhishthira and makes him say "I am a slave" in every assembly before he lets him live, and Yudhishthira then frees him. In the CE Bhima seizes him, the Trigarta army breaks, and nothing more is said of him here (4.32.32–35). See L-167.
* **The fight with Susharma.** Rejected lines (\*602–\*606) have Bhima seize Susharma by the hair, grind him on the ground and kick him on the head. Not narrated.
* **Bhima's bow.** A rejected appendix passage (@31) has Bhima take up a bow and shower arrows. In the CE the brothers "made use of divine weapons" (4.32.22) with no more said.
* **The tree.** Here, in the CE, Bhima does look at a tree to use as a club, and Yudhishthira forbids it, so that no one will know him (4.32.16–20). The same scene in Virata's hall, against Kichaka, is only in rejected passages. See L-164.
* Other rejected lines (\*545–\*601, \*607–\*617, @28–@30, @33) add details. Not narrated.
