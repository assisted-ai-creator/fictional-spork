# The Stupefying Weapon

When he had beaten Vaikartana, Partha said to Virata's son, "Take me to that
division where the golden palm tree stands. There, on his chariot, stands
Bhishma, Shantanu's son, our grandfather, godlike to see, wanting to fight me. I
shall take his bowstring from him in battle today. Watch me today as I loose a
bright heavenly weapon, like lightning coming from a thundercloud in the sky.
The Kurus will see my gold-backed Gandiva. All my enemies together will wonder,
'Is he shooting with his right hand or his left?' I shall make a river flow,
with blood for its water, chariots for its whirlpools and elephants for its
crocodiles, hard to cross, flowing to the other world.

"With smooth-jointed broad arrows I shall cut down the forest of the Kurus,
thick with hands, feet, heads, backs and arms for branches. As I alone, one
archer, conquer the Kaurava army, a hundred paths will open, like the paths of a
fire in a forest. You will see their army whirled round by me like a wheel.
Stand on the chariot without fear, on even ground and uneven. I could split with
my arrows a mountain that stood blocking the sky. At Indra's word I once killed
hundreds and thousands of the Paulomas and the Kalakhanjas in battle. From Indra
I have a firm grip, from Brahma a sure hand, and from Prajapati a way of
fighting that is deep, tumultuous, bright and piercing. Across the sea I broke
Hiranyapura, beating sixty thousand chariot fighters with fierce bows. The Kurus
are a forest, with banners for its trees and foot soldiers for its grass, full
of lions among chariot fighters. I shall set fire to it with the heat of my
weapons. With my smooth-jointed arrows I alone shall drive them from their
chariot seats, as the wielder of the thunderbolt drove the asuras. I got the
fierce weapon from Rudra, the Varuna weapon from Varuna, the Agni weapon from
Agni and the Vayu weapon from the Wind, and the thunderbolt and other weapons
from Shakra. I shall root up the terrible forest of the sons of Dhritarashtra,
guarded by lions among men. Let your fear go, son of Virata."

Reassured like this by the Left-handed Archer, Virata's son plunged into the
terrible army of chariots of wise Bhishma. Bhishma, cruel in action, calmly held
back strong-armed Dhananjaya as he came on, wanting to conquer his enemies in
battle. Then, wearing bright garlands and ornaments, skilled and high-minded,
Duhshasana, Vikarna, Duhsaha and Vivimshati came against Bibhatsu of the
terrible bow, their bowstrings in their hands, and surrounded him.

Duhshasana pierced Uttara, son of Virata, with a broad arrow, and with a second
the hero pierced Arjuna in the chest. Jishnu turned and cut his bow, adorned
with gold, with a broad-bladed arrow with vulture feathers. Then he pierced him
in the chest with five, and Duhshasana, stricken by Partha's arrows, left the
battle and went away. Vikarna, son of Dhritarashtra, pierced Arjuna, killer of
enemy heroes, with sharp, straight arrows with vulture feathers. The son of
Kunti quickly struck him in the forehead with a smooth-jointed arrow, and
wounded, he fell from his chariot. Then Duhsaha and Vivimshati ran at Partha and
covered him with sharp arrows, trying to save their brother in the battle.
Dhananjaya, calm, pierced both of them at once with two sharp arrows with
vulture feathers, and killed their horses. Their horses killed, their limbs
wounded, those two sons of Dhritarashtra were carried off by their followers on
other chariots. And unconquered Bibhatsu, the mighty son of Kunti in his diadem
and garland, hitting every mark, fell on every quarter.

---

Then all the great chariot fighters of the Kauravas came together and fought
Arjuna together, on their guard, Bharata. He of boundless spirit covered those
great chariot fighters on every side with nets of arrows, as mist covers
mountains. With the trumpeting of great elephants, the neighing of horses and
the sound of drums and conches, the noise was tumultuous. Partha's nets of
arrows split the bodies of men and horses and their iron armour, and flew out by
the thousand. Loosing arrows fast, the Pandava shone in the battle like the
blazing sun at midday in autumn.

Terrified, the chariot fighters leapt from their chariots, the horsemen from
their horses' backs, and the foot soldiers fell to the ground. As the armour of
the great men was struck by arrows, copper, silver and iron, there was a great
noise. The whole field was covered with the bodies of the dead, riders of
elephants and horses whose lives had been taken by sharp arrows. The earth was
strewn with men who had fallen from their chariot seats. Dhananjaya, bow in
hand, seemed to dance in the battle. Hearing the twang of the Gandiva, like the
crash of a thunderbolt, all creatures fled from the great battle in terror.
Heads could be seen fallen at the front of the battle, still wearing earrings
and turbans and golden garlands. The earth shone, covered with limbs torn by
arrows, with arms still holding bows, and with others still wearing hand
ornaments. As heads were struck off by sharp arrows between them, it was like a
shower of stones from the sky, bull of the Bharatas.

The Pandava Partha had been held back for ten years and three. Showing himself
in that fierce form, with the valour of Rudra, he let loose the terrible fire of
his anger on the sons of Dhritarashtra. When they saw his valour as he burned up
the army, all the warriors lost heart while Dhritarashtra's son watched. Having
terrified that army and put the great chariot fighters to flight, Arjuna, best
of victors, moved about the field, Bharata. He set flowing a terrible river with
waves of floods of blood, choked with bones for its moss, made by Time at the
end of an age. Partha made a great river, running with blood. Bows and arrows
were its rafts, flesh and blood its mud, great chariots its islands, and conches
and drums its roar. As he took arrows, fitted them, loosed them and drew the
Gandiva, no gap could be seen at all.

---

Then Duryodhana, Karna, Duhshasana, Vivimshati, Drona with his son and Kripa,
the great chariot fighter, came back in great fury. They wanted to kill
Dhananjaya, and they drew their mighty, strong bows. He of the monkey banner
went out to meet them all together on his chariot bright as the sun, its flags
flying, great king. Then Kripa, Karna and Drona, best of chariot fighters,
surrounded mighty Dhananjaya with great weapons. Loosing floods of arrows well,
like rain clouds, they poured showers of arrows on the diadem-wearer as he came
on. From close by, they eagerly filled him quickly in the battle with many
feathered arrows. Covered like this on every side with their heavenly weapons,
not even two finger-breadths of him could be seen uncovered.

Then, laughing, the great chariot fighter Bibhatsu fitted the heavenly Indra
weapon, bright as the sun, to the Gandiva. Like the sun with its rays, the
mighty son of Kunti in his diadem and garland, scorching in the battle, covered
all the Kurus. As lightning in a cloud, or fire on a mountain, so the Gandiva
was, stretched like Indra's bow, the rainbow. As lightning shines in the sky
when the rain-god pours, so the Gandiva, as it fell, covered all ten directions.
All the chariot fighters there were terrified on every side. They all lost heart
and could not collect their minds. All the warriors turned from the battle, out
of their wits. So all the armies were broken, bull of the Bharatas, and fled in
every direction, without hope of life.

---

Then, as the warriors were being killed, Bhishma, Shantanu's son, hard to
overcome, mighty, ran at Dhananjaya. He took his fine bow adorned with gold, and
sharp-pointed arrows that pierce the vitals and crush. With a white umbrella
held over his head, that tiger among men shone like a mountain at sunrise. The
son of Ganga blew his conch, delighting the sons of Dhritarashtra, came round
keeping him on the right, and held Bibhatsu back.

When he saw him coming like that, the son of Kunti, killer of enemy heroes,
received him gladly, as a mountain receives a rain cloud. Then mighty Bhishma
sank eight swift arrows, hissing like snakes, into Partha's banner. Those
blazing arrows reached the Pandava's banner and struck the monkey and the
creatures that lived at the top of the banner. Then the Pandava cut Bhishma's
umbrella with a great broad-bladed arrow, and it quickly fell to the ground.
Swift in action, the son of Kunti struck his banner hard with arrows, and his
chariot horses, and the two drivers at his sides.

The fight between Bhishma and Partha was tumultuous, making the hair stand on
end, like that of Bali and Vasava. As broad arrows met broad arrows in the fight
between Bhishma and the Pandava, they shone in the sky like fireflies in the
rains. As Partha loosed arrows to left and right, the Gandiva became like a
whirling wheel of fire, king. He covered Bhishma with hundreds of sharp arrows,
as a cloud covers a mountain with streams of rain. Bhishma scattered that shower
of arrows with his own, as it rose like a tide, and held Arjuna back. Then those
nets of arrows, cut to pieces, were scattered in the battle towards Phalguna's
chariot. Then the Pandava's shower of arrows with golden feathers rose quickly
from his chariot, spreading like locusts, and Bhishma scattered it again with
hundreds of sharp arrows.

Then all the Kurus said, "Well done, well done! Bhishma has done a hard thing in
fighting Arjuna. The Pandava is strong, young, skilful and quick in action. Who
else could bear Partha's force in battle, except Bhishma, Shantanu's son, or
Krishna, son of Devaki, or the great teacher, mighty Bharadvaja's son?" Holding
off weapons with weapons, those two bulls among men played, bewildering the eyes
of all creatures, mighty as they were. Using the weapons of Prajapati, of Indra,
of Agni, the most terrible, of Kubera, of Varuna, of Yama and of Vayu, the two
great men ranged about in the battle. All creatures were amazed as they watched
them in the fight, and said, "Well done, strong-armed Partha! Well done,
Bhishma! This great use of great weapons that we see in the battle between
Bhishma and Partha is not right among men."

So the battle of weapons went on between those two who knew all weapons. Then
Jishnu turned and cut Bhishma's bow, adorned with gold, with a broad-bladed
arrow. In the space of a blink, strong-armed Bhishma took another bow in the
battle and strung it, mighty as he was, and in his anger quickly loosed very
many arrows at Dhananjaya. Arjuna too, of great energy, loosed many sharp,
bright arrows at Bhishma, and Bhishma at the Pandava. As those two great men,
who knew the heavenly weapons, loosed arrows without pause, no difference could
be seen between them, king. The great chariot fighter, the son of Kunti in his
diadem and garland, covered the ten directions with arrows, and so did the hero,
Shantanu's son. The Pandava outdid Bhishma, and Bhishma outdid the Pandava, in
that battle, king, and it was a wonder in the world.

The heroes who guarded Bhishma's chariot, killed by the Pandava, lay all round
the son of Kunti's chariot, king. Then the arrows of Shvetavahana, loosed from
the Gandiva, came on, fixed feather to point, wanting to clear away the enemy.
Flying from his chariot, bright, clad in gold, they could be seen in the sky
like lines of swans. All the gods with Vasava, standing in the sky, watched that
deep, bright, heavenly weapon of his as he shot. Seeing that bright wonder, the
mighty gandharva Chitrasena, delighted, praised it to the king of the gods.
"Look at these arrows of Jishnu's, splitting enemies, going as if joined
together. This heavenly weapon he is loosing is wonderful. Men would not believe
it, for there is nothing like it among them. This is a wonderful meeting of
great ancient weapons. The armies cannot look at the Pandava, like the sun
blazing at midday in the sky. Both are famous for their deeds, both skilled in
war, both equal in deeds, both hard to face in battle." When he said this, the
king of the gods honoured the meeting of Partha and Bhishma with a heavenly rain
of flowers, Bharata.

Then Bhishma, Shantanu's son, aimed at an opening as the Left-handed Archer
shot, and struck him in the left side. Then, laughing, Bibhatsu cut the bow of
Bhishma, of boundless energy, with a broad-bladed arrow with vulture feathers.
Then Dhananjaya, son of Kunti, pierced him in the chest with ten arrows, as he
struggled and strove. In pain, the strong-armed son of Ganga, hard to overcome
in battle, held on to the pole of his chariot and stood there a long time,
stricken. His driver, remembering his teaching, carried the great chariot
fighter away, senseless, to protect him.

---

Bhishma had left the head of the battle and was fleeing. Then the great-souled
son of Dhritarashtra raised his banner, took his bow himself and came at Arjuna,
roaring. With a broad arrow loosed from a bow drawn to the ear, he pierced
Dhananjaya of the terrible bow, of high valour, ranging among the enemy hosts,
in the middle of the forehead. With that well-sharpened arrow, bright as gold,
fixed there, the one of honoured deeds shone, king, like a lovely mountain with
a single peak. From the wound hot blood poured out without stopping, and it
shone on him like a garland bright with golden flowers.

Struck by Duryodhana's arrow, the swift one's anger rose, and with his spirit
undaunted he took arrows like poison and fire and pierced the king. Duryodhana,
of fierce energy, and Partha, the one hero, those two heroes among men of
Ajamidha's line, struck each other equally in the battle.

Then Vikarna, on a great elephant in rut, like a mountain, with four chariots
guarding the elephant's feet, ran again at Jishnu, son of Kunti. As that king of
elephants came on fast, Dhananjaya pierced it between the temples with a very
swift arrow of strong iron, loosed from a bow drawn to the ear. The arrow with
vulture feathers, loosed by Partha, went into the elephant up to the feathers,
splitting it, huge as a great mountain, as a thunderbolt thrown by Indra splits
a mountain. Burning from the arrow, that king of elephants, its limbs trembling
and its heart in pain, sank and fell to the ground, like a mountain peak struck
by a thunderbolt. When that best of elephants had fallen to the ground, Vikarna
in terror jumped down suddenly, quickly went eight hundred paces and climbed
into Vivimshati's chariot.

With that arrow like a thunderbolt he had killed the elephant, like a great
mountain or cloud. With another arrow like it, Partha split Duryodhana's chest.
Then, with the elephant and the king struck, and Vikarna broken with the guards
of his feet, the chief warriors, driven by the arrows loosed from the Gandiva,
suddenly left. The hero of the Kurus saw the elephant killed by the arrow and
heard that all the warriors were running. He turned his chariot round and fled
from the battle to where Partha was not.

As Duryodhana fled fast, terrible to see, wounded by the arrow and vomiting
blood, the diadem-wearer, conqueror of enemies, with his quiver, eager to fight,
shouted at him. "Leaving behind your great fame and glory, why do you turn and
run from the battle? Today the drums are not beaten for you as they were when
you went into battle. I am the one who does Yudhishthira's bidding, the third
Partha, and I stand firm in battle. Turn your face back for that reason, and
remember the conduct of kings, son of Dhritarashtra. The name you were given
long ago on earth, Duryodhana, is empty. There is nothing of Duryodhana, the
hard fighter, in you, when you leave the battle and run. I see no one to protect
you, Duryodhana, in front or behind. Get away from the fight, hero of the Kurus,
and save your dear life from the Pandava today."

---

Called out like this in battle, the high-minded son of Dhritarashtra turned back
at the goad of his words, like a rutting elephant turned by the goad. Unable to
bear it, touched by the words of the great chariot fighter, the swift and mighty
hero turned his chariot round, like a snake trodden on by the sole of a foot.
Seeing him turning, Karna, hero among men, with his golden garland, stopped his
own flight, steadied himself, wounded as he was, and came to Duryodhana's right
side against Partha. Then strong-armed Bhishma, Shantanu's son, turned back,
urging on his horses with their golden girths, his bow strung, and guarded
Duryodhana from behind against Partha. Drona and Kripa, Vivimshati and
Duhshasana too turned quickly and all came in front, bows and arrows stretched,
hurrying for Duryodhana's sake.

When he saw those divisions turning back like rising floods, Partha Dhananjaya,
swift, fell on them like a swan on a cloud coming on. They surrounded Partha on
every side, took up heavenly weapons, came on and rained arrows all round him,
as clouds rain on a mountain with the force of their water. Then the archer of
the Gandiva, Indra's son, conqueror of enemies, held off the weapons of those
bulls of the Kurus with a weapon. Then he brought out another weapon, the
stupefying one, which cannot be resisted. He covered the directions and the
between-directions with sharp, well-edged, well-feathered arrows, and with the
twang of the Gandiva the mighty one shook their minds. Then Partha, killer of
enemies, took his great conch in both hands, with its terrible, loud sound, and
blew it, filling the directions and quarters, the sky and the earth. Stupefied
by the sound of the conch that Partha blew, the heroes of the Kurus all dropped
their bows, so hard to face, and fell into stillness.

With his enemies senseless like this, Partha remembered what the princess Uttara
had said. He said to the son of the Matsya, "Go into the middle of them while
the Kurus are senseless. Take the white cloths of the teacher and Sharadvat's
son, Karna's fine yellow one, and the blue ones of Drona's son and the king,
hero among men. But I think Bhishma has not lost his senses. He knows the
counter to my weapon. Keep his horses on your left, for that is how to go past
those who have not lost their senses."

Then Virata's great-souled son dropped the reins, jumped down from the chariot,
took the cloths of the great chariot fighters and quickly got back on his own
chariot. Then Virata's son urged on the four fine horses with their golden
girths. The white horses carried Arjuna out of the middle of the battle, past
the army with its banners. As that hero among men went off like this, mighty
Bhishma struck at him with arrows. Arjuna killed Bhishma's horses and pierced
him in the side with ten arrows. Then Arjuna, with his unfailing bow, left
Bhishma in the battle, pierced his driver, and stood free of the mass of
chariots, like the sun when it has split Rahu.

The hero of the Kurus came to himself and saw Partha, like great Indra, standing
alone, freed from the battle. The son of Dhritarashtra said quickly, "How did
this man get away from you? Bind him so that he cannot get free."

Shantanu's son laughed and said to him, "Where had your understanding gone,
where was your valour, when you were standing there in stillness, having dropped
your arrows and your bright bow? Bibhatsu is not the man to do a cruel deed. His
mind is not set on evil. He would not give up his own dharma for the sake of the
three worlds. That is why we were not all killed in this battle. Go quickly to
the Kurus, hero of the Kurus. Let Partha win the cattle and go back."

When he heard his grandfather's words, which were for his own good, the king,
who had given up his hopes of the battle, unforgiving as he was, sighed and fell
silent. Seeing that Bhishma's words were good, and that the fire of Dhananjaya
was growing, they all set their minds on going back, guarding Duryodhana.

---

When he saw those heroes of the Kurus going, Partha Dhananjaya, glad at heart,
followed them for a while, speaking to them and honouring his elders there. He
honoured the old grandfather, Shantanu's son, and the teacher Drona, by bowing
his head, and greeted Drona's son, Kripa and all his elders with bright arrows.
With an arrow Partha cut Duryodhana's crown, bright with the finest jewels. He
took leave of the heroes worthy of honour, filling the worlds with the twang of
the Gandiva. He suddenly blew Devadatta, splitting the hearts of his enemies.
The hero, shining with his net of gold, overcame all his enemies with his
banner.

When he saw the Kurus going, the diadem-wearer, delighted, said to the son of
the Matsya, "Turn the horses round. Your cattle are won. The enemy have gone. Go
back to the city, glad."

Having beaten the Kurus in battle, the one with the eyes of a bull brought back
Virata's great wealth. When the sons of Dhritarashtra had all gone, broken, many
Kuru soldiers came out of the thick forest. They gathered from here and there,
their minds full of fear, and stood with their hair loose and their hands
joined. Worn out by hunger and thirst, in a strange land, out of their wits,
they bowed and said, bewildered, "Partha, what shall we do for you?"

"Go in peace, bless you," said Arjuna. "You have nothing at all to fear. I do
not want to kill men in distress. I give you my full assurance."

When they heard those words of safety, the soldiers who had gathered there
blessed him with wishes of long life, fame and glory.

When the Kurus had turned back, broken and in his power, Phalguna took the road
and said, "Prince, look over all the herds that have been brought back,
strong-armed hero, with the herdsmen. We shall go towards Virata's city in the
afternoon, when we have rested the horses, watered them and bathed them. Let
herdsmen go quickly, sent by you, to tell the good news in the city and announce
your victory."

Then Uttara quickly gave orders to messengers, at Arjuna's own word. "Announce
my victory."

**Divergences.**

* **Stripped and shaved.** A rejected line (\*1037) speaks of the beaten warriors with their heads and beards shaved. In the CE nobody is shaved or stripped. Uttara takes only the cloths (*vastra*) of five leaders, for the princess's dolls (4.61.12–15). See L-169.
* **Whom Arjuna spares.** A rejected appendix passage (@55) has Arjuna list those he will not kill: the frightened, the naked, the chariotless, one who seeks refuge. In the CE he spares the frightened soldiers after the battle (4.62.5).
* **More weapons.** Rejected lines (\*985–\*988) add the weapons of the Sun, Yama, Garuda, Pashupati and Vasuki to Arjuna's list. Not narrated.
* **The stupefying weapon's name.** A rejected appendix passage (@56) calls it the sleep weapon (*svāpana*). In the CE it is *saṃmohana*, "the stupefying weapon" (4.61.8).
* **After the battle.** Rejected appendix passages (@52, @53) add Arjuna's orders on the road home. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines in 4.56–62 (\*977–\*984, \*989–\*1036, \*1038–\*1051, @49–@51, @54) add details. Not narrated.
