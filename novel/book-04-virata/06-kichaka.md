# Kichaka

While the Parthas lived in the city of the Matsyas, the great chariot fighters
in hiding, ten months passed. Yajnaseni, who deserved to be served herself,
lived in great sorrow waiting on Sudeshna, lord of the people, Janamejaya.

As Panchali went about like this in Sudeshna's house, Virata's commander saw
her, lotus-faced. When he saw her going about like a goddess, like a child of
the gods, Kichaka wanted her, pierced by the arrows of love. Burning with the
fire of desire, the commander went to Sudeshna and said, as if laughing.

"I have never seen this lovely woman here in King Virata's house before. She
maddens me with her beauty, as a new wine maddens with its scent. Who is she,
lovely lady, like a goddess, who goes straight to the heart? Tell me who she is
and where she comes from, this beauty. She churns my mind and puts me in her
power, and I see no other remedy for it today. Ah, this fine maid of yours seems
fresh to me. She does work that does not suit her. Let her rule me and whatever
I have. Let her grace my great house, rich in elephants, horses and chariots,
rich in wealth, with plenty of food and drink, lovely, adorned with gold."

Then Kichaka, having spoken to Sudeshna, went to the king's daughter, Krishna,
and spoke to her soothingly, like a jackal to a lion's daughter in the forest.

"This beauty of yours, and your early youth, are wasted, lovely one. Like a fine
garland that no one wears, you do not shine, lovely as you are. I will leave my
old wives. Let them be your slaves, you of the lovely smile. And I, lovely one,
will stand before you like a slave, always in your power, fair-faced one."

"You think me desirable, son of a suta," said Draupadi, "a sairandhri of low
birth, a dresser of hair, not fit to be asked. I am another man's wife, bless
you. This is not right of you. Wives are dear to living beings. Think of dharma.
You must never set your mind on another man's wife in any way. To keep away from
what should not be done is the vow of good men. A wicked man who lusts wrongly,
in his folly, will meet terrible disgrace, and very great danger. Do not
rejoice, son of a suta. Do not throw away your life today by thinking of me, who
am hard to win and guarded by heroes. You cannot have me. My husbands are
gandharvas. In their anger they would kill you. Enough. Do not destroy yourself.
You want to go by a road that men like you cannot travel. As a foolish, witless
child standing on one bank wants to cross to the far bank, so is what you want
to do. Even if you go down into the earth, or fly up into the sky, or run to the
far shore of the ocean, you will not escape them. For my husbands are the sons
of gods, and they crush their enemies. Why do you press me today, Kichaka, as a
sick man presses the night of doom? Why do you think of me like a child lying in
his mother's lap who wants to grasp the moon?"

---

Refused by the princess, Kichaka, flooded with a terrible desire that knew no
bounds, said to Sudeshna, "Kaikeyi, see to it that I can be with the sairandhri.
Find a way to win her for me, Sudeshna, so that I do not lose my life."

When she had heard him lamenting like this again and again, the high-minded
queen, Virata's wife, took pity on him. Seeing her own advantage, thinking of
his, and of Krishna's distress, Sudeshna said to the suta, "Have wine and food
made ready, on the pretext of a festival. I will send her to you there to fetch
the wine. When she has been sent there, soothe her as you please in private,
where no one will stop you. Perhaps, when she is soothed, she will take pleasure
in you."

At his sister's word Kichaka went home and had wine brought, fit for a king,
well strained. He had skilled men prepare fine food and drink, much meat of
goats and sheep, and many kinds of deer. When this was done, Queen Sudeshna,
invited by Kichaka, sent the sairandhri to Kichaka's house.

"Get up, sairandhri," said Sudeshna. "Go to Kichaka's house. Bring me something
to drink, lovely one. Thirst is troubling me."

"I will not go to his house, princess," said Draupadi. "You yourself know how
shameless he is, queen. I will not become a woman who goes where she likes in
your house, lovely one, faithless to my husbands. And you know, queen, the
agreement that was made when I came into your house. Kichaka, fool that he is,
is proud with lust. When he sees me, he will dishonour me. I will not go there,
lovely one. You have many maids in your power, princess. Send another, bless
you. For he will dishonour me."

"He would never hurt you," said Sudeshna, "when I have sent you from here."

With these words she gave her a golden vessel with a lid. Afraid, weeping,
putting her trust in fate, she set out for Kichaka's house to fetch the wine.

"As I know no man but the Pandavas," said Draupadi, "by that truth let Kichaka
not bring me into his power when I come there."

Then the helpless woman stood for a moment before the Sun, and the Sun
understood everything about the slender-waisted woman. He ordered an invisible
rakshasa to guard her, and it did not leave the faultless woman there in any
state. When the suta saw Krishna come near him, frightened like a doe, he rose
in joy, like a man crossing a river who has found a boat.

---

"Welcome, lovely-haired one," said Kichaka. "My night has dawned well. You have
come as my mistress. Do what pleases me. Let them bring gold garlands, shell
bracelets, earrings of pure gold, silk clothes and skins. There is a fine bed
made ready for you. Come there with me and drink the honeyed wine."

"The princess sent me to you to fetch wine," said Draupadi. "She said to me,
'Bring me something to drink quickly. I am thirsty.'"

"Others will take the princess her wine, good woman," said Kichaka.

With that the suta's son seized her by the right hand. Seized, she shook herself
free, threw Kichaka to the ground, and ran for shelter to the hall where King
Yudhishthira was. As she ran, Kichaka caught her by the hair, and before the
king's eyes he threw her down and kicked her.

Then the rakshasa whom the Sun had set to guard her carried Kichaka away with
the force of the wind, Bharata. Struck by the rakshasa's strength, he fell to
the ground, reeling and motionless, like a tree cut at the root.

Bhimasena and Yudhishthira, sitting there, saw her, and could not bear Kichaka's
kicking Krishna. Great-minded Bhima, wanting to kill the wicked Kichaka, ground
his teeth in his rage. Then the Dharma King pressed Bhima's big toe with his own
big toe, king, and held him back, for fear they would be recognised.

She came to the door of the hall, weeping, and spoke to the king of the Matsyas,
looking at her husbands in their wretchedness, the lovely-hipped one. Guarding
their disguise and the promise joined to dharma, burning, Drupada's daughter
spoke with a fierce look.

"They whose enemy does not sleep while he treads the earth: their proud wife the
suta's son has kicked. They who give and do not beg, kind to brahmins, speakers
of truth: their proud wife the suta's son has kicked. They whose drums sound,
and whose bowstrings are heard without end: their proud wife the suta's son has
kicked. They who are full of energy, restrained, strong and proud: their proud
wife the suta's son has kicked. They who could kill this whole world, but are
bound by the noose of dharma: their proud wife the suta's son has kicked.

"Where are those great chariot fighters today, who give shelter to those who
come seeking it, who go about the world in hiding? How can they bear it, like
eunuchs, mighty and boundless in their energy as they are, while their dear,
faithful wife is kicked by a suta's son? Where is their anger now, their courage
and their energy, if they do not try to save their wife when a wicked man
strikes her? What can I do here against Virata, this spoiler of dharma, who
watches me, blameless as I am, being struck, and bears it?

"You do nothing like a king to Kichaka, king. Your dharma is like a robber's. It
does not shine in the assembly. Kichaka does not keep to his own dharma, nor
does the king of the Matsyas in any way. And the courtiers who sit round him do
not know dharma either. I do not reproach you, King Virata, in this gathering of
people. I am not fit to be struck like this near you, king of the Matsyas. Let
the courtiers see Kichaka's offence."

"I do not know the quarrel between you two," said Virata. "It happened out of my
sight. How can I act rightly without knowing the truth of the matter?"

Then the courtiers, understanding, honoured Krishna again. They said, "Well
done! Well done!" and blamed Kichaka. "The man who had this woman, lovely in
every limb, with her long eyes, for his wife would have the greatest gain. He
would never grieve."

As the courtiers praised Krishna like this, sweat broke out on Yudhishthira's
forehead in his anger. Then the Kauravya said to the princess, his beloved
queen, "Go, sairandhri. Do not stay here. Go to Sudeshna's house. Heroes' wives
suffer as they follow their husbands. By suffering in their service they win
their husbands' worlds. I think your husbands do not see the time for anger.
That is why the gandharvas, bright as the sun, do not run to you. You do not
know the right time, sairandhri. You run about like an actress. You are
disturbing the Matsyas at their dice in the king's hall. Go, sairandhri. The
gandharvas will do what pleases you."

"I keep dharma for their sake, and they are too kind," said Draupadi. "Those
whose eldest is a dicer, anyone at all may strike them."

When she had said this, Krishna ran to Sudeshna's house, her hair loose, her
eyes red with anger. Her face, as she stopped weeping, shone like the orb of the
moon in the sky freed from a streak of cloud.

"Who struck you, lovely one?" said Sudeshna. "Why are you weeping, beautiful
one? Who will have no happiness today, good woman? Who has done you wrong?"

"Kichaka struck me when I went to fetch your wine," said Draupadi, "in the hall,
before the king's eyes, as if we were alone."

"I will have Kichaka killed, lovely-haired one, if you wish," said Sudeshna,
"since he is mad with lust and thinks of you, who are hard to win."

"Others will kill him, those he has wronged," said Draupadi. "I think it is
plain that he will go to the other world this very day."

**Divergences.**

* **Bhima and the tree.** Rejected passages (\*315, \*317) have Bhima look at a great tree, meaning to tear it up and attack Kichaka. In the CE Bhima grinds his teeth, and Yudhishthira holds him back by pressing his big toe (4.15.11–12). See L-164.
* **Kichaka's victory.** Rejected lines (\*263–\*264) say Kichaka had just come back from a victory at the border. Not narrated.
* **A hymn to Shiva.** A rejected appendix passage (@13) has Draupadi pray to Shiva. In the CE she prays to be kept true to the Pandavas and stands before the Sun (4.14.18–19).
* **Yudhishthira's sermon.** A rejected appendix passage (@17) gives Yudhishthira a long teaching on the dharma of wives. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*265–\*314, \*316, \*318–\*342, @12, @14–@16, @18, @19) add speeches, including more of Draupadi's reproach and Sudeshna's offer. Not narrated.
