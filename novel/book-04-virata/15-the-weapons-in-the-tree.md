# The Weapons in the Tree

When he came to the shami tree, Partha said to Virata's son, the delicate
prince, well known to be no great expert in battle, "At my command, Uttara,
bring down the bows quickly. These of yours will not be able to bear my
strength, or carry a heavy load, or crush an elephant, or stand the swing of my
arms as I conquer our enemies here. So climb this leafy shami, Bhuminjaya. The
bows of the sons of Pandu are laid away in it, Yudhishthira's, Bhima's,
Bibhatsu's and the twins'. The banners and arrows of those heroes are here, and
their heavenly armour. Here too is Partha's bow of great power, the Gandiva,
which by itself is equal to a hundred thousand others, and increases a kingdom.
It can bear any strain, great, like a palm tree, the greatest of all weapons,
the oppressor of enemies. It is heavenly, worked with gold, smooth, long,
without a flaw, fit to bear a heavy load, fearful and beautiful to see. All the
others are like it, mighty and strong."

"We have heard that a body is tied in this tree," said Uttara. "How can I touch
it with my hand, a king's son? It is not right for someone like me, born of
kshatriyas, a great prince who knows the mantras and the sacrifices, to touch
such a thing. If I touched a corpse I would be unclean, like a bearer of the
dead. How could you make me fit to deal with again, Brihannada?"

"You will be fit to deal with, great king, and you will be clean," said
Brihannada. "These are bows. Do not be afraid. There is no body here. How could
I make you do anything shameful, the heir of the king of the Matsyas, born in
his family, a high-minded prince?"

When Partha said this, Virata's son in his earrings jumped down from the chariot
and climbed the shami tree, against his will. Standing in the chariot,
Dhananjaya, killer of enemies, gave him his orders. "Get the wrappings off them
quickly." So he loosened all their bindings, and there he saw the Gandiva with
four others. As those bows, bright as the sun, were unwrapped, a heavenly light
came out of them, like the planets rising. When he saw them, like snakes
stretching, his hair stood on end and he was seized with fear for a moment.

Touching those great, shining bows, Virata's son said this to Arjuna, king.
"Whose is this fine bow, with a hundred drops of gold set on it, and a thousand
points, all gold? Whose is this fine bow, on whose back golden elephants shine,
well fixed, with fine sides and a good grip? Whose is this fine bow, with sixty
ladybirds of pure gold spread out shining on its back? Whose is this fine bow,
on which three golden suns shine, well fixed, blazing with light? Whose is this
fine bow, with golden moths worked in pure gold, bright with gold and gems? And
whose are these thousand iron arrows, feathered, with gold tips all round, in a
golden quiver? Whose are these broad, long arrows with vulture feathers,
sharpened on stone, the colour of turmeric, well pointed, yellow, all of iron?
Whose is this black quiver, marked with five tigers, mixed with boars' ears,
holding ten arrows? Whose are these broad, long arrows, all of iron, seven
hundred of them, drinkers of blood? Whose are these, well dressed with feathers
like parrots' wings in front and with yellow iron behind, with golden feathers,
sharpened on stone? Whose is this long sword, stone-backed and stone-pointed,
laid in a tiger-skin sheath, with a great hilt worked with gold? Whose is this
heavenly sword, with a fine blade and a bright sheath, a great sword hung with
bells, with a golden hilt, without a flaw? Whose is this clean sword set in a
sheath of cowhide, with a golden hilt, not to be withstood, from Nishadha, fit
for heavy work? Whose is this blade in a sheath of five-clawed hide, with a
golden guard, of fine size and shape, yellow, like the sky? Whose is this heavy,
yellow sword in a sheath of gold, well heated, bright as fire, with a hand
guard, without a flaw? Tell me truly what I ask, Brihannada. I am full of wonder
to see all these great things."

"What you asked me about first," said Brihannada, "is Partha's bow, the Gandiva,
famous in the worlds, the destroyer of enemy armies. The greatest of all
weapons, adorned with gold, this was Arjuna's Gandiva, the highest weapon, which
by itself is equal to a hundred thousand others and increases a kingdom, with
which Partha overcomes gods and men in battle. It has been honoured by gods,
danavas and gandharvas for endless years. Brahma held it first, for a thousand
years. After him Prajapati held it, for five hundred and three years, Shakra for
eighty-five, King Soma for five hundred, and Varuna for a hundred. Partha of the
white horses has held it for sixty-five years. This is that great, heavenly bow
of great power, the finest of bows, honoured among gods and men, and it has the
highest beauty.

"This one with fine sides and a golden grip is Bhimasena's bow, with which
Partha, scorcher of enemies, conquered the whole eastern quarter. This fine bow,
with its lovely shape, bright with ladybirds, is King Yudhishthira's, son of
Virata. The one on which golden suns shine, blazing with light, is Nakula's
weapon. The one with golden moths worked in pure gold is the bow of Madri's son
Sahadeva.

"These thousand arrows like razors, feathered, like snakes' poison, are
Arjuna's, son of Virata. Blazing in battle with energy, flying fast, they never
run out for the hero as he breaks through enemies in battle. These broad, long
ones that look like half-moons are Bhima's sharp arrows, which destroy enemies.
This quiver marked with five tigers, holding the turmeric-coloured arrows with
golden feathers, sharpened on stone, is Nakula's. It was the quiver of that wise
son of Madri with which he conquered the whole western quarter in battle. These
arrows shaped like the sun, all of iron, bright and well made, are wise
Sahadeva's. These sharp, yellow, broad, long-feathered ones, with golden
feathers and three joints, are the king's great arrows.

"This long sword, stone-backed and stone-pointed, is Arjuna's, strong and able
to bear heavy work in battle. The great sword in the tiger-skin sheath is
Bhimasena's, heavenly, able to bear heavy work, the terror of enemies. The one
with the fine blade and the bright sheath, with the golden hilt, the best of
them, is the sword of the wise Kauravya, the Dharma King. The one laid in a
sheath of five-clawed hide, with bright stitching, is Nakula's sword, strong and
able to bear heavy work. And this clean sword set in a sheath of cowhide, know
that it is Sahadeva's, strong, able to bear any work."

---

"These weapons worked with gold, belonging to the great-souled, swift-acting
Parthas, shine out beautifully," said Uttara. "But where is Arjuna Partha, or
Yudhishthira the Kauravya, Nakula and Sahadeva, and Bhimasena the Pandava? All
those great men, the destroyers of all their enemies, lost their kingdom at
dice, and nothing has been heard of them since. And where is Draupadi Panchali,
famous as a jewel among women? When they were beaten at dice, Krishna followed
them into the forest."

"I am Arjuna Partha," said Arjuna. "The courtier is Yudhishthira, and Ballava,
your father's cook, is Bhimasena. The horse-keeper is Nakula, and Sahadeva is
among the cows. Know that the sairandhri is Draupadi, for whose sake the
Kichakas were killed."

"If you can tell me the ten names of Partha that I have heard before," said
Uttara, "I shall believe all of it."

"Very well, I shall tell you my ten names," said Arjuna. "Arjuna, Phalguna,
Jishnu, Kiritin, Shvetavahana, Bibhatsu, Vijaya, Krishna, Savyasachin and
Dhananjaya."

"Why are you called Vijaya?" said Uttara. "Why are you Shvetavahana? Why are you
called Kiritin, and how are you Savyasachin? Why are you Arjuna, Phalguna,
Jishnu, Krishna, Bibhatsu and Dhananjaya? Tell me truly. I have heard the
reasons for that hero's names."

"When I had conquered all the countries and taken all their wealth," said
Arjuna, "I stood in the middle of the treasure. That is why they call me
Dhananjaya. When I go out to battle against men mad for war, I never come back
without winning. That is why they know me as Vijaya. White horses in golden
harness are yoked to my chariot when I fight in battle. That is why I am
Shvetavahana. I was born by day on the slope of the Himalaya, under the two
stars Uttara Phalguni and Purva Phalguni. That is why they know me as Phalguna.
Long ago, when I fought the bulls of the danavas, Shakra put a diadem bright as
the sun on my head. That is why they call me Kiritin. I would never do a hideous
deed when I fight. That is why gods and men know me as Bibhatsu. Both my hands
are right hands for drawing the Gandiva. That is why gods and men know me as
Savyasachin. My colour is rare, and has no equal on the four-cornered earth, and
I do pure deeds. That is why they know me as Arjuna. I am hard to reach, hard to
beat, a tamer, the son of the Punisher of Paka. That is why I am known among
gods and men by the name Jishnu. My father gave me the tenth name, Krishna, out
of love for his dark, bright boy."

Then Virata's son came close and greeted Partha. "I am named Bhuminjaya, and by
name I am also Uttara. How lucky that I see you, Partha. Welcome, Dhananjaya.
Red-eyed, strong-armed one, like the trunk of a king elephant, forgive what I
said to you in ignorance. Since you have done wonderful, hard deeds before, my
fear has gone, and I feel the greatest affection for you."

---

"Mounting this great chariot, hero, with me as your charioteer," said Uttara,
"which division will you go to? I shall drive where you tell me."

"I am pleased with you, tiger among men," said Arjuna. "You have nothing to
fear. I shall drive off all your enemies in battle, skilled in battle as you
are. Be at ease, wise one, and watch me fighting the enemy in this clash, doing
great and terrible deeds. Quickly tie all these quivers to my chariot, and bring
this sword adorned with gold. I shall fight the Kurus and win back your cattle.
The seat of this chariot, guarded by me, will be your city. My resolve will be
its flanks, my arms its walls and gates, its three poles and quivers its
defences, its many banners its crowds. The slap of the bowstring will be its
challenge, made in anger, and the rumble of the wheel rims its drums. A chariot
mounted by me in battle, archer of the Gandiva, cannot be beaten by enemy
armies. Let your fear go, son of Virata."

"I am not afraid of them," said Uttara. "I know you are steady in battle, equal
to Keshava himself, or to Indra, in war. But when I think of this, I am utterly
confused, and slow as I am, I cannot come to any answer. How has this state of a
eunuch come to one with such heroic limbs and looks, with such marks, by the
ripening of what deed? I think you are the trident-bearer going about in a
eunuch's dress, or the equal of the king of the gandharvas, or Shatakratu the
god."

"At the command of my eldest brother I am keeping this vow of celibacy for a
year," said Arjuna. "I am telling you the truth. I am no eunuch, strong-armed
one. I am under another's command and bound by dharma. Know, prince, that I have
finished my vow and come through it."

"You have shown me the greatest favour today," said Uttara. "My guess was not
wrong. The best of men do not look like eunuchs, as you do. I have a helper in
battle. I could fight the immortals. My fear is gone. What shall I do? Tell me.
I shall hold your horses, which break enemy chariots. I was trained in driving
by a good teacher, bull among men. Know that I am trained in driving as Daruka
is for Vasudeva, or Matali for Shakra, best of men.

"The horse yoked on the right, whose hoofs no one can see touch the ground as he
goes, is like Sugriva. This fine one, best of the yoke horses, who draws the
left of the pole, I think is like Meghapushpa in speed. This handsome one in
golden harness who draws the rear on the left I think is stronger than Sainya in
speed. And the one who draws your rear on the right, arched and eager, is
thought more powerful in speed than Balahaka. This chariot is fit to carry you,
the archer, in battle, and you are fit to fight from it, I think."

Then that mighty man took the bangles off his arms and put on his fine, bright
leather guards that sound like a drum. He tied up his dark, curling hair with a
white cloth. He strung the Gandiva quickly and drew it. As the bow was drawn
there was a great sound, as when a great mountain strikes another mountain. The
earth rumbled, a strong wind blew in every direction, the sky was full of birds
flying about, and the great trees shook. The Kurus knew that sound, like the
crash of a thunderbolt, to be Arjuna drawing that best of bows with his arms on
the chariot.

---

With Uttara as his charioteer, Dhananjaya walked round the shami tree with it on
his right, took all the weapons and set out. The great chariot fighter took the
lion banner off the chariot and laid it at the foot of the shami, and set out
with Uttara driving. He fixed on the chariot the divine illusion made by
Vishvakarman, the golden banner with the lion's tail and the mark of the monkey.
He thought in his mind of Agni's favour. Agni, knowing what he thought, urged
the creatures into the banner.

The great chariot fighter Bibhatsu, son of Kunti, of the white horses, mounted
the chariot with its flag, its bright parts and its quivers. With his sword tied
on and his armour on and his bow in his hand, he set out towards the north, with
the great monkey on his banner. That mighty destroyer of enemies, summoning his
strength, blew his great, loud conch, which made his enemies' hair stand on end.
Then those swift horses sank to the ground on their knees, and Uttara,
terrified, sat down on the floor of the chariot.

The son of Kunti steadied the horses, pulling them up with the reins, and Arjuna
embraced Uttara and comforted him. "Do not be afraid, best of princes. You are a
kshatriya, scorcher of enemies. How can you lose heart in the middle of the
enemy, tiger among men? You have heard the sounds of conches and many
kettledrums, and of elephants trumpeting as they stood in the battle lines. So
how are you so frightened by this conch here, downcast and terrified, like some
common man?"

"I have heard the sounds of conches and many kettledrums," said Uttara, "and the
trumpeting of elephants standing in the battle lines. But I have never heard a
conch like this before. I have never seen a banner like this, and I have never
heard such a twang of a bow. At the sound of this conch, and the twang of the
bow, and the roar of the chariot, my mind is utterly confused. All the
directions are whirling, and my heart seems to ache. All the quarters are hidden
by the banner, and I cannot make them out. And my ears have been deafened by the
sound of the Gandiva."

"Stand firm on the chariot," said Arjuna, "and press down with your feet. Hold
the reins tight. I am going to blow my conch again."

At the sound of that conch, the roar of the chariot wheels and the twang of the
Gandiva, the earth shook.

"From the roar of the chariot," said Drona, "from the way the conch is blown and
the earth trembles, this is no one but the Left-handed Archer. Our weapons do
not shine. The horses are not glad. The fires do not blaze, though they are
kindled. That is not good. All the beasts cry out terribly towards the sun.
Crows settle on our banners. That is not good. And the birds on our left tell of
great danger. This jackal runs howling through the middle of the army. It has
gone away unharmed, and that tells of great danger. I see your hair standing on
end. Your army is beaten already, and no one wants to fight. Nearly all the
warriors are pale-faced and out of their wits. Let us send the cattle on and
stand ready to strike, in battle order."

**Divergences.**

* **Urvashi's curse.** Rejected lines (\*757–\*758) have Arjuna explain his state as the result of Urvashi's curse. In the CE he says he is under a vow of celibacy for a year at his brother's command, and is no eunuch (4.40.12–13). See L-25.
* **The weapons' history.** Rejected lines (\*711–\*712) tell how the Gandiva passed from Varuna to Agni and so to Arjuna. The CE lists only who held it and for how long (4.38.39–41). Not narrated.
* **Uttara as Arjuna's slave.** A rejected line (\*748) has Uttara say he will be Arjuna's slave. Not narrated.
* **Kunti's name.** A rejected appendix passage (@37) adds an eleventh name, Partha, from his mother Pritha. Not narrated.
