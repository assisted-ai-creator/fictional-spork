# Entering Virata's Court

Then King Yudhishthira came first to Virata as he sat in his hall. He had tied
the golden dice, with their beryl colours, in his cloth and held them under his
arm. The lord of men, glorious, the increaser of the Kuru line, of great power,
honoured by the kings of men, as hard to approach as a snake of sharp venom,
came to the glorious ruler of the kingdom. The great bull among men, in plain
dress, was like an immortal in a form of flame, like the sun covered by great
banks of cloud, like a mighty fire covered with ash.

Virata the king saw the Pandava coming, like the moon wrapped in clouds. He
asked his counsellors and brahmins, his sutas and the people, and all who sat in
the assembly, "Who is this who has come here, who looks at my hall like a king?
This best of men cannot be a brahmin. He is a lord of the earth, I think. He has
no slave, no chariot, no earrings near him, and yet he shines like Indra. The
marks of his body show him to be a consecrated king, so my mind tells me. And he
comes near me untroubled, like a rutting elephant coming to a lotus pool."

As Virata was wondering, Yudhishthira, the bull among men, came to him and said,
"Emperor, know that a brahmin has come here who has lost everything, looking for
a living. I want to live here near you, sinless one, and come and go as I
please, lord."

"Welcome," the king said at once, in delight, "and be received. I ask you with
goodwill, my friend: from what king's country have you come here? Tell me truly
your family and your name, and what craft you have learned."

"I was once Yudhishthira's friend," said Yudhishthira. "I am a brahmin of the
Vaiyaghrapadya clan. I am skilled at throwing dice, and a player. My name is
Kanka, Virata. That is how I am known."

"I will give you whatever boon you wish," said Virata. "Rule the Matsyas, for I
am in your power. For dice players are always dear to me, and you, who are like
a god, deserve a kingdom."

"Let this be my one condition, lord of the people," said Yudhishthira. "Let
there be no dispute with the low, king of the Matsyas. And let no one I have
beaten keep the stake. Let that be my boon, by your grace."

"If anyone displeases you," said Virata, "I will kill him even if he should not
be killed, and banish him from the kingdom even if he is a brahmin. Let the
people gathered here hear me: Kanka is lord in this kingdom as I am. You will be
my friend, and ride in the same carriage as I do. You will have clothes in
plenty and much food and drink. You will look after my affairs within the palace
and outside it always. I have opened the door to you. Whoever comes to you, worn
thin by want, speak for them to me always, and I shall give them all they need,
no doubt of it. You need fear nothing near me."

So the bull among men won his boon and his place with King Virata, and the hero
lived there happily, greatly honoured. No one saw through what he was doing.

---

Then another came, of terrible strength, blazing with splendour, walking with
the grace of a lion. He held a ladle and a spoon in his hand, and a black sword,
unsheathed and unscratched. In the form of a cook, with the highest splendour,
like the sun lighting up this world, wearing black clothes, with the strength of
the king of mountains, he came up to the king of the Matsyas and stood there.

When the king saw him come asking for work, he said to the people gathered
there, "Who is this young bull among men, very handsome, his shoulders as high
as a lion's? I have never seen him before. He is like the sun. I think and
think, and I cannot find out what he is. And thinking about his mind too, I
cannot get to the truth of this bull among men."

Then the great-minded Pandava came to Virata, looking very humble, and said, "I
am a cook, lord of men, named Ballava. Take me on, for I am a fine maker of
sauces."

"I do not believe you are a cook, giver of honour," said Virata. "You look like
the thousand-eyed god. In splendour, beauty and valour you shine, my friend,
second to none among men here."

"I am your cook and servant, lord of men," said Bhima. "I know the finest
sauces, which King Yudhishthira himself used to taste, all of them, king. And no
one is my equal in strength. I have always been given to wrestling, lord of the
earth. I have fought elephants and lions. I will always do what pleases you,
sinless one."

"I give you your wish, the kitchen," said Virata. "And do it well, since you say
you are skilled. But I do not think this work is fit for you. You deserve the
earth with the ocean for its rim. But as you wish, so be it. Be the head of my
kitchen. I make you master of the men who have served me there before."

So Bhima was set in the kitchen, and he became dear to King Virata. He lived
there, king, and neither the common people nor any of the servants knew him.

---

Then Krishna gathered up her soft, faultless hair, curling at the ends, and hid
it on her right side, the dark-eyed woman. She put on a single dress, black and
very dirty, and in the dress of a sairandhri Krishna went about as if in
distress. Men and women came running when they saw her wandering, and asked,
"Who are you? What do you want?"

She told them, king of kings, "I am a sairandhri, and I have come here. I want
to work for anyone who will keep me." But because of her beauty, her dress and
her gentle voice, they did not believe she was a servant who had come for her
food.

Now Virata's wife, a princess of the Kekayas, greatly honoured, was looking out
from the palace and saw the daughter of Drupada. When she saw her like that,
without a protector, in a single garment, she called her and said, "Good woman,
who are you? What do you want?"

She told her, king of kings, "I am a sairandhri, and I have come here. I want to
work for anyone who will keep me."

"Women who look like you are not what you say, lovely one," said Sudeshna.
"Rather, they send out many slave women and slave men of that kind. Your ankles
are hidden, your thighs are close together, you are deep in three places and
high in six, red in the five that should be red, and your voice is as soft as a
goose's. Your hair is lovely, your breasts are lovely, you are dark, with full
hips and breasts. You have all the marks, like a Kashmiri mare. Your eyelashes
curl, your lips are like the bimba fruit, your waist is slender, your neck is
like a conch, your veins are hidden, and your face is like the full moon. Tell
me truly who you are, good woman. You are no slave in any way. Are you a yakshi,
a goddess, a gandharvi or an apsaras? Are you Alambusha, Mishrakeshi, Pundarika
or Malini, Indrani or Varuni, or the wife of Tvashtri, of Dhatri or of
Prajapati? Which of the goddesses famous among the gods are you, lovely one?"

"I am not a goddess," said Draupadi, "nor a gandharvi, nor an asuri, nor a
rakshasi. I am a sairandhri, a maid who serves. I tell you the truth. I know how
to dress hair, I grind ointments well, and I shall weave lovely, many-coloured
garlands. I served Satyabhama, Krishna's beloved queen, and Krishna, the wife of
the Pandavas, the one beauty of the Kurus. I go from place to place like this,
getting fine food, and as long as I get clothes, I am content. The queen herself
gave me my name, Malini. I have come to your house, Queen Sudeshna."

"I would keep you on my head, I have no doubt of it," said Sudeshna, "if only
the king would not go after you with all his heart. Look at the women in the
royal household here in my house. They gaze at you, fascinated. What man would
you not bewitch? Look at the trees standing in my house. Even they seem to bow
to you. What man would you not bewitch? When King Virata sees your more than
human body, lovely-hipped one, he will leave me and go after you with all his
heart. Any man you looked at with attention, faultless, long-eyed one, would
fall into the power of love. Any man who saw you always, you of the lovely
smile, would fall into the power of the bodiless god. As the female crab takes
in its young to its own death, so I think your living here would be for me,
bright-smiling one."

"I cannot be had by Virata or by anyone else," said Draupadi. "My husbands are
five young gandharvas, lady, the sons of a certain gandharva king of great
power. They always protect me, and I live this hard life. My gandharva husbands
would be pleased with a place where I do not have to take anyone's leftovers or
wash anyone's feet. Any man who lusted after me as he would after other common
women would enter another body that very night. And no one can move me, lady.
For my gandharvas are hard-tempered, and they are very strong."

"Then I will keep you as you wish, happy one," said Sudeshna. "You shall never
touch feet or leftovers."

So Krishna was reassured by Virata's wife, and no one else there knew who she
really was, Janamejaya.

---

Sahadeva too put on the fine dress of a cowherd, took up their way of speaking,
and came to Virata. When the king saw that bull among men coming, shining, he
went up to the joy of the Kurus and asked him, "Whose man are you, where have
you come from, and what do you want, my friend? I have never seen you before.
Tell me the truth, bull among men."

When he had come to the king, the scorcher of enemies said, in a voice like the
roar of a great mass of cloud, "I am a vaishya named Arishtanemi. I was the
counter of cattle for the bulls of the Kurus. I want to live with you, best of
the people, for I do not know where those lions among kings, the Parthas, are. I
cannot live by any other work, and no king but you pleases me."

"You are a brahmin, or else a kshatriya," said Virata. "You look like the lord
of the earth with the ocean for its rim. Tell me the truth, tormentor of
enemies. Vaishya work does not suit you. From what king's country have you come,
and what craft have you learned? How will you live with us always, and what will
your wages be?"

"Yudhishthira is the eldest of the five sons of Pandu," said Sahadeva. "He had
eight hundred thousand herds of cattle, a hundred in each, and another ten
thousand, and twice as many again. I was the counter of his cattle, and they
called me Tantipala. Past, present and future, anything that can be counted
anywhere for ten yojanas around, there is nothing I do not know. That great man
knew my skills well, and Yudhishthira, the king of the Kurus, was pleased with
me. With me the cows quickly grow many, and no sickness comes among them. I have
learned this by many means, and these crafts are in me. And I know the bulls,
king, with their honoured marks, whose urine, when it is smelt, makes even a
barren cow conceive."

"I have a hundred thousand beasts brought together," said Virata, "with the
qualities of each kind set apart. I give them to you, with their keepers. Let my
beasts be in your care here."

So the lord of men lived there happily, unknown to the king, lord of the people.
No others knew him either, and the king gave him the wages he wished.

---

Then another was seen, rich in beauty, a big man wearing women's ornaments, with
earrings hanging, and long, lovely shell bracelets overlaid with gold, by the
rampart of the wall. Strong-armed, walking like a rutting elephant, scattering
his long, thick hair, making the ground shake with his step, he came to Virata
near the hall. The king saw him come into the hall, the crusher of enemies in
disguise, shining with the highest splendour, the son of great Indra with the
gait of a king of elephants. He asked all those near him, "Where does this man
come from? I have never heard of him before." When the men could not tell him
who it was, the king said in wonder.

"You are a man with every gift, handsome, dark and young, like the leader of a
herd of elephants. Take off your lovely gilded shell bracelets, take off your
braid and your earrings, wear your hair in a crest, dress differently, and be an
archer in armour with arrows. Mount a chariot and be one of those who ride out.
Be equal to my sons, or be my equal. For I am old and want to lay down my
burden. Protect all the Matsyas by your strength. Men like you are never eunuchs
in any way. That is what my mind tells me."

"I sing, I dance and I play music," said Arjuna. "I am good at dancing and
skilled at song. Give me to the princess Uttara yourself, and I shall be the
queen's dancer, lord of men. As for this form of mine, what use is it to tell of
it? It only adds to my grief. Know me, lord of men, as Brihannada, a son or a
daughter without father or mother."

"I give you your wish, Brihannada," said Virata. "Teach my daughter to dance,
and girls like her. But I do not think this work fit for you. You deserve the
earth with the ocean for its rim."

The king of the Matsyas tried Brihannada in the arts, in dance and in music, and
when he had heard that his manhood was surely gone, he sent him to the maidens'
quarters. There lordly Dhananjaya taught Virata's daughter song and music, and
her friends and her maids, and the Pandava became dear to them. So Dhananjaya
lived there in disguise, master of himself, doing what pleased them, and no one
knew him, neither the people outside nor those within.

---

Then another Pandava was seen, a lord, while King Virata was looking at his
horses. People saw him coming like the orb of the sun freed from a cloud. He
looked at the horses here and there, and the king of the Matsyas saw him looking
at them. Then the slayer of enemies said to his followers, "Where has this man
come from, who shines like an immortal? He is looking closely at my horses. He
must surely be skilled with horses, and wise. Bring him to me quickly. The hero
shines like an immortal."

The slayer of enemies came up to the king and said, "Victory to you, lord of the
earth, and good fortune. I have always been thought well of in handling horses,
king. I would be your skilled horse-master."

"I will give you carriages, wealth and a house," said Virata. "You deserve to be
my horse-master. But where are you from? Whose man are you? How have you come
here? Tell me what craft you have learned."

"Yudhishthira is the eldest of the five sons of Pandu," said Nakula. "He set me
over his horses before, slayer of enemies. I know the nature of horses and all
their training, how to deal with vicious ones, and all their treatment. No horse
of mine was ever timid. No mare of mine is vicious, let alone a stallion. People
call me, and so did the Pandava Yudhishthira, Granthika by name."

"Whatever horses and draught animals I have," said Virata, "let them all be in
your charge from today. And let all the men who yoke my horses, and my drivers,
be under you. If this pleases you, godlike one, tell me what wages you have in
mind. Work with horses does not suit you. You shine like a king, and I think
well of you. For the sight of you is to me like the sight of Yudhishthira. How
does the blameless Pandava live and find joy in the forest without his
servants?"

So the young man, like the best of the gandharvas, was honoured by King Virata
with delight, and no one else knew him either, as he went about among them,
pleasant and dear.

So the Pandavas lived among the Matsyas, as they had promised, their sight never
failing. They kept their life in hiding with care, the lords of the earth with
its rim of ocean, in the greatest distress.

* 4.6.10 *vaiyāghrapadyaḥ*: "of the Vaiyaghrapadya clan".

* 4.7.1 *khajaṃ ca darvīṃ ca*: "a ladle and a spoon".
* 4.8.1: Draupadi hides her hair on her right side.
* 4.8.6 *kaikeyī*: "a princess of the Kekayas", Sudeshna.
* 4.8.10–12: Sudeshna's list of the marks of beauty is kept. "Deep in three places, high in six, red in five" are traditional marks; the text does not list them.
* 4.8.19 *mālinī*: "Malini", the name Draupadi gives for herself. "The queen" who named her is the queen she served.

* 4.9.4 *vaiśyo 'smi nāmnāham ariṣṭanemiḥ*: "I am a vaishya named Arishtanemi". Two verses later he says he was called Tantipala (4.9.9). Both are kept.
* 4.9.8–9: the numbers of Yudhishthira's herds are the text's.
* 4.10.1 *prākāravapre*: "by the rampart of the wall".
* 4.10.8 *uttarāyāḥ*: "the princess Uttara", Virata's daughter. Her brother, Prince Uttara, has the same name in English; the novel always says which is meant.
