# Dhaumya's Counsel

"You have named the work you will each do," said Yudhishthira, "and you will do
it. I too have decided what seems best to me. Let our household priest guard our
sacred fires in Drupada's house, with the cooks and stewards. Let Indrasena and
the others take the empty chariots and go quickly to Dvaravati. That is my
thought. And let these women, all Draupadi's maids, go to the Panchalas with the
cooks and stewards. All of them must say, 'We do not know where the Pandavas
are. They all left us at Dvaitavana and went away.'"

"Even when a thing is known," said Dhaumya, "friends should speak of it out of
affection. So I too shall say something. Hear only the reasons. Listen, princes.
I shall tell you about life in a king's household, so that a servant who goes
about in a royal house may come to no harm.

"It is hard, Kauravyas, even for one who knows, to live a year in a king's house
unhonoured and unknown, when you deserve honour. Let a man enter by the door he
is shown, and not trust kings. Let him look for a seat where no other will lay a
claim against him. He who does not mount the king's carriage, couch or stool,
his elephant or his chariot, thinking, 'I am in favour,' may live in a king's
house. He who never sits where wrongdoers might suspect him may live in a king's
house.

"Let him never instruct the king when the king has not asked him. Let him sit by
him in silence, and honour him at the right time. For kings resent men who speak
falsely, and they despise a counsellor who lies. A wise man should never make
friends with the king's wives, or with those who go in and out of the women's
quarters, or with those the king hates or who wish him ill. Let him do even the
smallest tasks with the king's knowledge. A man who goes about in this way with
a king comes to no harm.

"Let him wait on the king with care, as on fire, as on a god. For if he serves
him with falsehood, the king will surely strike him down. Let him follow
whatever his master sets him to, and avoid carelessness, disrespect and anger.
In all counsel let him speak of what is good and what is pleasing, but let him
say what is good rather than what is only pleasing. Let him agree with the king
in all matters and all talk, and never praise to him what is displeasing and
harmful. A wise man should serve thinking, 'I am not dear to him,' careful and
watchful, doing what is good and what is pleasing. Let him not court what the
king dislikes, nor keep company with those who wish him ill, nor waver from his
own place. Such a man may live in a king's house.

"A wise man should sit on the king's right or left side. For the place behind is
set for the armed guards, and a high seat in front is always forbidden. When he
is in the king's sight, let him not whisper anything, however important. For
even the poor take that as the worst of offences. Let him not reveal to others a
falsehood the king has spoken, nor speak of a man the king resents. Let him not
be proud, thinking, 'I am brave,' or, 'I am wise.' By doing what pleases the
king, a man becomes dear to him and prospers.

"When he has won a lordship hard to win, and the king's favour, let him be
careful in what is dear and good to the king. Whose anger is a great harm, and
whose favour bears great fruit: what man thought wise would wish him harm, even
in his mind? Let him never twist his lips, and never cut short the king's words.
Let him always sneeze, break wind and spit quietly. When something is said in
jest, let him not laugh too much, nor laugh like a madman. Nor should he be too
grave, for then he will seem heavy. Let him show a gentle smile, born of good
humour. He who is not elated by gain, not hurt by slights, and never confused,
may live in a king's house.

"A wise counsellor who always gives way to the king or the king's son keeps his
fortune long. A counsellor who is taken up and then held back for some reason,
and does not press the king, is taken up again. The man who lives by the king or
in his lands should be wise, and speak well of him both to his face and behind
his back. For the counsellor who asks the king to let him take by force will not
keep his place long, and will come into danger of his life.

"Always seeing his own advantage, let him not speak against the king before
others, and never outdo the king in the training grounds. He who is not weary,
who is strong and brave, never leaving him like a shadow, truthful, gentle and
self-controlled, may live in a king's house. He who, when another is being sent,
springs forward and says, 'What shall I do?' may live in a king's house. He who
does not hesitate when he is commanded, in heat or cold, by night or by day, may
live in a king's house. He who, living away from home, does not keep remembering
those he loves, and looks for happiness through hardship, may live in a king's
house.

"Let him not dress like the king, nor laugh too loudly near him, nor let his
counsel out in many places. So he becomes dear to the king. When he is set to a
task, let him not touch any wealth. For a man who takes what is not his meets
with bonds or death. Whatever the king gives him, a carriage, clothes, ornaments
or anything else, let him always wear that. So he becomes dearer to him.

"Keep to such conduct this year, my sons, if you want to prosper. Then, when you
have come back to your own land, you may live as you please."

"You have taught us well, bless you," said Yudhishthira. "No one else would tell
us this, except our mother Kunti and wise Vidura. Please do what must be done
next, to carry us through this sorrow, for our setting out and for our victory."

At the king's words Dhaumya, the best of brahmins, did everything the rules
prescribe for a departure. He lit their fires and made offerings with spells for
their prosperity and growth, and for the conquest of the earth. They walked
round the fire and the brahmins rich in austerity, keeping them on their right,
and with Yajnaseni before them, the six set out.

**Divergences.**

* **Dhaumya with them.** Rejected passages (\*127, \*129) have Dhaumya go to the Panchalas with the fires, or set out with the Pandavas. In the CE the fires go to Drupada's house (4.4.2) and "the six" set out (4.4.49).
* **More counsel.** Rejected passages (\*109–\*122) add more rules, such as never despising a kshatriya, a snake or a learned brahmin. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*101–\*108, \*123–\*126, \*128) add phrases and blessings. Not narrated.
