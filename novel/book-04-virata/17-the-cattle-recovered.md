# The Cattle Recovered

As the great Kaurava chariot fighters drew up their divisions like this, Arjuna
came on quickly, filling everything with the roar of his chariot. They saw the
top of his banner and heard the roar of the chariot, and the twang of the
Gandiva as he drew it again and again. Then, when he had looked at it all and
seen the great chariot fighter, the archer of the Gandiva, arrive, Drona spoke.

"That is the top of Partha's banner, shining in the distance. That is his roar,
like a cloud's, and that is the monkey howling. There stands the best of chariot
fighters, on the best of chariots, drawing the Gandiva, the best of bows, with a
sound like thunder. These two arrows have fallen together at my feet, and two
more have passed close by my ears, just touching them. Having lived out his time
in the forest and done superhuman deeds, Partha greets me and asks in my ears
after my health."

"Hold the horses, charioteer, within arrow range of the army," said Arjuna,
"while I look to see where in this army that lowest of the Kuru line is.
Ignoring all the others, when I see that proud man, I shall fall on his head.
Then all these are beaten. There stands Drona, and Drona's son next to him.
Bhishma, Kripa and Karna, great archers, stand there. But I do not see the king
here. He is taking the cattle away along the southern road, I suspect, to save
his life. Leave this army of chariots and go where Suyodhana is. I shall fight
there, son of Virata. There is no battle without its prize. When I have beaten
him I shall come back with the cattle."

When he said this, Virata's son pulled in the horses with care and held them
back with the reins where those bulls of the Kurus were. Then he urged them on
in the direction Duryodhana had gone.

When Shvetavahana had left the mass of chariots and gone, Drona understood his
purpose and said, "Bibhatsu does not want to stop anywhere except by the king.
Let us fall on his rear as he drives on fast. No one but the thousand-eyed god,
or Krishna son of Devaki, could fight him alone in battle when he is angry. What
will the cattle do for us, or all this wealth? Duryodhana is sinking already in
the flood of Partha, like a boat."

Bibhatsu went on as he had said. He announced his own name and quickly covered
that army with arrows, like locusts. As they were covered with the floods of
arrows Partha sent, the warriors could not see the ground or the sky, both
hidden by his arrows. They had no thought of fighting or of running away. In
their hearts they could only admire Partha's speed. Then he blew his conch,
which made his enemies' hair stand on end. He drew his great bow and urged the
creatures in his banner on. The conch sounded, the chariot wheels roared, and
the superhuman creatures who lived in his banner cried out. At that the cows
lifted their tails, bellowed on every side and turned back, taking the southern
road.

---

Arjuna, first among archers, had driven off the enemy army by force and won back
the cattle. Wanting to do more for his friends in battle, he went on again
towards Duryodhana. As the cattle went fast towards the Matsyas, the heroes of
the Kurus thought the diadem-wearer had done what he came for, and they fell on
him suddenly as he went towards Duryodhana.

He saw their many divisions drawn up close together, with many banners. The
killer of enemies called to the son of the Matsya, Virata's son, and said, "Take
these white horses with golden reins and harness quickly along this way. Use all
your speed and effort and bring me to that crowd of lions among chariot
fighters. That evil suta's son wants to fight me as one elephant fights another.
Bring me to him, prince, who has grown so proud from leaning on Duryodhana."

With those great horses, as swift as the wind, with golden girths, the son of
Virata broke up the army of those chariot fighters and carried the Pandava into
the middle of the battle. Chitrasena, Samgramajit, Shatrusaha and Jaya, great
chariot fighters, came against the Bharata as he fell on them, with broad
arrows, wanting to protect Karna. Then that hero among men burned up the masses
of chariots of those bulls of the Kurus in his anger, as fire burns a forest.
His bow was his flame, and the speed of his arrows his heat.

When that tumultuous battle had begun, Vikarna, a hero of the Kurus, came at
Partha, the great chariot fighter, Bhima's terrible younger brother, on his
chariot, with a rain of broad arrows. Then Partha drew his bow, covered with the
finest gold and with a strong string, and crushed and brought down Vikarna's
banner. With his banner cut down, Vikarna fled fast.

Then King Shatruntapa, unable to bear his anger, struck Partha with a
tortoise-nail arrow as he harried the enemy hosts and did superhuman deeds.
Wounded by that king, the great chariot fighter, as he plunged into the Kurus'
army, Partha quickly pierced Shatruntapa with five arrows and then killed his
charioteer with ten. Then, pierced by the bull of the Bharatas with an arrow
that went through his armour, he fell lifeless to the ground in the battle, like
a tree on a mountain top broken by the wind.

Those bulls among chariot fighters, heroes, broken in battle by a greater hero,
a bull among chariot fighters, shook like great forests shaken at the season by
the force of the wind. Killed by Partha, heroes among men lay asleep on the
ground, young and finely dressed, givers of wealth. Their valour was equal to
Vasava's, but Vasava's son had beaten them in battle. They lay bound in armour
of gold and black iron, like great Himalayan elephants. So that hero among men,
the archer of the Gandiva, killing his enemies in battle, ranged through the
battle in every direction and quarter like a fire burning a forest at the end of
the hot season. As the wind in spring scatters the fallen leaves and drives them
through the sky, so the diadem-wearer, the great chariot fighter, ranged through
the battle on his chariot, scattering his enemies.

He killed the horses of Samgramajit, Vaikartana's brother, whose chariot was
drawn by red horses. Then, with his spirit undaunted, the diadem-wearer took off
his head with a single arrow. When his brother had been killed, Vaikartana, the
suta's son, summoned up his strength and ran at him, like a king of elephants
raising his tusks, or a tiger at a great bull. Vaikartana quickly struck the
Pandava with twelve arrows, pierced all his horses in their bodies, and struck
Virata's son with arrows.

Like a king of elephants struck by another elephant, Arjuna took sharp
broad-headed arrows from his quiver, drew his bow to the ear and pierced the
suta's son with arrows. The crusher of enemies split his arms, thighs, head,
forehead, neck and the parts of his chariot with arrows like lightning, loosed
from the Gandiva, as he stood there in the battle. Driven back by Partha's
arrows, like an elephant beaten by an elephant, the swift Vaikartana left the
front of the battle and went away, burning from the Pandava's arrows.

---

When the son of Radha had gone, Duryodhana and the others attacked the Pandava
with arrows, each with his own division. Seeing that army, drawn up in many
parts, falling on them with arrows, and knowing it had to be met, Virata's son
said this.

"Mounting this bright chariot, with me as your charioteer, Jishnu, which
division will you go to? I shall drive where you tell me."

"The one you see with red eyes, in the tiger-skin, standing on his chariot under
a blue flag, safe and well, Uttara," said Arjuna, "that is Kripa's army of
chariots. Take me straight there. I shall show that strong archer my speed with
weapons. The one with the fine golden water pot on his banner is the teacher
Drona, best of all who bear weapons. Go round him with your right side towards
him, hero, with a glad heart. Here there must be no clash with him. That is the
eternal dharma. If Drona strikes my body first, then I shall strike him, and he
will not be angry.

"Not far from him, the one on whose banner a bow is seen is the teacher's son,
Ashvatthaman, the great chariot fighter. He is always to be honoured by me, and
by all who bear weapons. When you reach his chariot, turn aside again and again.
Now see the one in the army of chariots, clad in golden armour, standing with a
third of the best of the army. On his banner is an elephant, set on a golden
staff. That is Dhritarashtra's glorious son, King Suyodhana. Take the chariot
straight at him, hero. He breaks enemy chariots. He is proud of his energy and
mad for battle. He is counted first among Drona's pupils for speed with weapons.
I shall show him great speed with weapons, with my arrows.

"The one on whose banner stands the bright elephant's girth rope is Vaikartana
Karna, whom you have known from before. When you reach the chariot of that
wicked son of Radha, be on your guard in battle. He always competes with me.

"Then there is the mighty one standing on his chariot with a banner of five
stars on blue, with a hand guard and a great bow. On his chariot is that fine
banner bright with stars and suns, and over his head that pure white umbrella.
He stands at the head of the great mass of chariots with their many banners and
flags, like the sun before the clouds. His golden armour, like the moon or the
sun, can be seen, and his helmet of gold, and he seems to frighten my heart.
That is Bhishma, Shantanu's son, grandfather to us all. He is bound by royal
fortune and obeys Duryodhana. He should be approached last, so that he does not
stand in my way. When I fight him, hold my horses carefully."

Then Virata's son, steady, carried the Left-handed Archer to where Kripa stood,
king, ready to fight Dhananjaya.

---

The divisions of the Kurus with their terrible bows could be seen, moving like
clouds with gentle winds at the end of the hot season. Close by stood the
horses, mounted by fighters, and terrible elephants, driven with lances and
goads.

Then Shakra came there, king, with the hosts of the gods, riding in Sudarshana,
with the Vishvas, the Ashvins and the hosts of the Maruts. Crowded with gods,
yakshas, gandharvas and great snakes, the sky shone as if with planets, free of
clouds. They came to see the power of their weapons used among men, and that
terrible great battle when Bhishma and Arjuna met.

A hundred hundred thousand golden pillars and others of gems and jewels held up
the palace there. There the king of the gods' heavenly sky chariot, going where
it wished, adorned with every jewel, shone as it moved through the sky. In it
stood the thirty-three gods with Vasava, gandharvas, rakshasas, snakes and the
ancestors with the great seers. So too King Vasumanas, Balaksha, Supratardana,
Ashtaka, Shibi, Yayati, Nahusha, Gaya, Manu, Kshupa, Raghu, Bhanu, Krishashva,
Sagara and Shala could be seen, shining brightly, in the sky chariot of the king
of the gods.

The sky chariots of Agni, Isha, Soma, Varuna and Prajapati, of Dhatri and
Vidhatri, of Kubera and Yama, of Alambusha and Ugrasena, and of the gandharva
Tumburu shone in their places, each in its part. All the hosts of the gods, the
siddhas and the highest seers came to see the battle between Arjuna and the
Kurus.

The pure scent of the heavenly garlands spread everywhere, like that of
flowering forests at the beginning of spring. The umbrellas, clothes, garlands
and fans of the gods standing there could be seen, red and not red. The dust of
the earth settled. Everything was filled with rays of light. The wind, carrying
heavenly scents, waited on the warriors. The sky seemed lit up, adorned and
bright with colours, with the many bright sky chariots, shining with jewels of
every kind, that the best of the gods had brought, some flying and some standing
still.
