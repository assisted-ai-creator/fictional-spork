# Bhima's Promise

"I shall tell you a great sorrow of mine, Bharata," said Draupadi. "You must not
resent it. I say it out of pain. When you fight tigers, buffaloes and lions in
the palace, and Kaikeyi watches, I feel faint. And Kaikeyi, faultless in every
limb, rises from the show and sees me, as if struck down by faintness. Then she
says to her women, 'I think this bright-smiling one grieves for the cook as he
is made to fight great beasts, out of affection born of living together. The
sairandhri is lovely, and Ballava very handsome. A woman's heart is hard to
know, and I think they are well suited. The sairandhri is always tender-hearted
because of the one she lives with. They both came to live in this royal house at
the same time.' Saying such words, she always pained me. And when she saw me
angry, she suspected me of you. When she spoke like this, great sorrow came over
me. Drowning in grief for Yudhishthira, I cannot bear to live.

"The young man who beat gods, men and snakes with a single chariot is now the
dancer of King Virata's daughters. He of boundless spirit who satisfied
Jatavedas at Khandava is now in the women's quarters, like fire hidden in a
well. The bull among men who was always a terror to his enemies, Dhananjaya,
lives in a dress that the world despises. He at the slap of whose bowstring
enemies trembled is now surrounded by women, who delight in the sound of his
singing. He on whose head a diadem bright as the sun used to shine, this
Dhananjaya, now wears his hair in a braid. That great one in whom all the divine
weapons are, the store of all learning, now wears earrings. He whom thousands of
kings of matchless energy could not pass in battle, as the great ocean does not
pass its shore, is now the young dancer of King Virata's daughters. He lives
disguised, a servant of the maidens.

"At the sound of his chariot the earth trembled, Bhima, with its mountains and
forests, with all that moves and does not move. At his birth, blessed as he was,
Kunti's grief vanished. That younger brother of yours makes me grieve today,
Bhimasena. When I see him coming, dressed in ornaments, with earrings of pure
gold and shell bangles on his arms, my heart sinks. When I see Arjuna, with his
terrible bow, his hair in a braid, surrounded by maidens, Bhima, my heart sinks.
When I see Partha like a god surrounded by maidens, like a rutting elephant
surrounded by she-elephants, waiting on Virata, the lord of wealth of the
Matsyas, in the midst of the music, the directions vanish for me. Surely our
noble lady does not know that Dhananjaya has come into hardship, and
Ajatashatru, the Kauravya, drowned by his evil dice.

"And when I see the youngest, Sahadeva, lord of fighters, coming among the cows
in a cowherd's dress, I turn pale, Bharata. Thinking again and again of
Sahadeva's conduct, strong-armed one, I cannot find any wrong he has done, for
which such sorrow should come to him, whose valour is truth. I am pained, best
of the Bharatas, to see your dear brother set by the Matsya among the cows, like
a bull among bulls. When I see him in red clothes, eager, at the head of the
cowherds, greeting Virata, a fever comes over me. For our noble lady always
praised heroic Sahadeva to me: 'He is of great family, of good conduct and
character, restrained by modesty, sweet-spoken, devoted to dharma and dear to
me. You must look after him in the forests, Yajnaseni, even at night.' When I
see Sahadeva, best of fighters, busy among the cows, sleeping at night on
calfskins, how can I live, Pandava?

"He who always had three gifts, beauty, skill in weapons and understanding, is
now Virata's keeper of horses. See how time turns round. Crowds gathered to
watch Damagranthi as he trained the horses at speed before the great king's
eyes. I saw him, glorious and shining, waiting on Virata, the great Matsya, and
showing him the horses. Do you think I am happy, Partha, scorcher of enemies,
filled with a hundred sorrows because of Yudhishthira? And there are other
sorrows, even greater, Bharata. I shall tell them too, son of Kunti. Listen.
While all of you are alive, sorrows of many kinds are drying up my body. What
sorrow could be greater?"

---

"In the dress of a sairandhri I go about the king's house and bring Sudeshna
water for her washing, because of a gambler," said Draupadi. "See my bitter
change, scorcher of enemies, I, a king's daughter. I sit and wait for the time,
for all sorrow, they say, passes like the seasons. Men's success is not lasting,
they say, nor victory and defeat. Thinking this, I wait for my husbands to rise
again. The very thing that brings a man victory is the cause of his defeat too.
With this in mind I wait. Men give and then beg. Some kill and are killed by
others. They bring others down and are brought down themselves. So I have heard.
Nothing is too heavy for fate, and nothing gets past fate. So I wait for fate to
turn again. Where the water once stood, it stands again. Hoping for the turn, I
wait for our rise. The man whose affairs are ruined by fate, though well
managed, must strive to bring fate round, if he is wise.

"As for why I have said all this, ask me, sad as I am, or I shall tell you
unasked. I am the queen of the Pandavas and the daughter of Drupada. What other
woman in this state would want to live? My misery is an insult to all the Kurus,
to the Panchalas too, Bharata, and to the sons of Pandu, tamer of enemies. What
other woman, surrounded by so many brothers, fathers-in-law and sons, slayer of
enemy heroes, would be so wretched? Surely, as a child, I did something to
displease the Maker, by whose grace I have come to this ill fortune, bull of the
Bharatas. See my complexion, Pandava. It was not like this even in the greatest
sorrow, then. You yourself know, Bhima, what happiness I had before, Partha. Now
I have come to slavery, and, helpless, I find no peace.

"I do not think it is without the will of fate that Partha Dhananjaya,
strong-armed, with his terrible bow, sits like a fire gone out. The way of
living beings cannot be known by men, Partha. I think this fall of yours was
never imagined. I, whose face you, who are like Indra, always watched, now watch
the faces of other, lower women, though I am the highest. See my state, Pandava,
which I do not deserve. While you are all alive, see how time turns round. I, to
whom the earth to its ocean rim was obedient, am now obedient to Sudeshna,
afraid. I, who had people going before me and following behind, now go before
and behind Sudeshna. And this is a sorrow I cannot bear, son of Kunti. Hear it.
I, who never ground ointment for my own body, except for Kunti, bless you, now
grind sandal paste. See my hands, son of Kunti. They were not like this before."

With that she showed him her two hands, hardened with calluses.

"I, who was never afraid of Kunti or of any of you," said Draupadi, "now stand
before Virata as a servant, afraid. 'What will the emperor say to me? Is the
paste well made or not?' For the Matsya, they say, likes no sandal paste ground
by anyone else."

As she told Bhimasena her sorrows, lovely Krishna wept quietly, looking at him.
In a voice broken by tears, sighing again and again, she said this, striking at
Bhimasena's heart. "It was no small wrong I did to the gods long ago, Bhima,
since I am so unlucky as to live when I ought to die, Pandava."

Then Vrikodara, slayer of enemy heroes, took her swollen, callused hands, as she
trembled, and put them to his face and wept. Holding them, the mighty son of
Kunti shed tears, and then, in the greatest distress, he said this.

---

"Shame on the strength of my arms," said Bhimasena, "and on Phalguna's Gandiva,
since your hands, which were red before, have grown hard with calluses. I was
about to make a great slaughter in Virata's hall. But the Dharma King held me
back with a sideways glance. Understanding him, I stayed where I was, lady. We
fell from the kingdom, and the Kurus were not killed. I did not take off the
heads of Suyodhana, of Karna, of Shakuni son of Subala and of wicked Duhshasana.
That burns me, lovely one, like a spike driven into my heart. Do not give up
dharma, lovely-hipped one. Give up your anger, wise one. If King Yudhishthira
heard this reproach of yours, lovely one, he would give up his life altogether.
And if Dhananjaya or the twins went to the other world, lovely-hipped,
slender-waisted one, I could not live.

"Sukanya, daughter of Sharyati, followed Chyavana of Bhrigu's line in the forest
when he had become an anthill in his calm, lovely one. Nadayani Indrasena too,
if you have heard of her, with her beauty, served her husband long ago, an old
man of a thousand years. Janaka's daughter, Vaidehi Sita, if you have heard of
her, followed her husband who lived in the great forest. Rama's beloved queen,
taken captive by a rakshasa, though she suffered, the lovely-hipped one followed
Rama. Lopamudra too, timid one, young and beautiful, followed Agastya, leaving
behind all pleasures beyond human. As these women I have named were beautiful
and faithful to their husbands, so are you, lovely one, with every virtue. Bear
it a little time longer, a month and a half. When the thirteenth year is full,
you will be a king's queen."

"I have shed these tears, Bhima, because I am in pain," said Draupadi. "I could
not bear my sorrows. I do not blame the king. What is past is past, mighty
Bhimasena. Turn to the work that has come at its time. Kaikeyi here, Bhima, is
always anxious because my beauty outshines hers: 'How can the king not go to
her?' Knowing her mind, and being false-hearted himself, this most wicked
Kichaka keeps asking me.

"Angry as I was, Bhima, I held my anger back and said to him, blind with lust,
'Guard yourself, Kichaka. I am the wife, the beloved queen, of five gandharvas.
They are hard to overcome, brave and rash, and they would kill you.' When I said
this, wicked Kichaka answered, 'I am not afraid of gandharvas, bright-smiling
sairandhri. I would kill a hundred thousand gandharvas, if they met me in
battle. Give me your time, timid one.' When he said this, I said again to the
suta, sick with lust, 'You are no match for those glorious gandharvas. I always
keep to dharma, with good family and good conduct. I do not want anyone killed.
That is why you are alive, Kichaka.' When I said this, the wicked one laughed
aloud. He does not keep to the good road, and does not want dharma.

"He is wicked in self and wicked in nature, a slave to lust and passion, unruly
and wicked. Refused again and again, he may strike me each time he sees me, and
so I would give up my life. Then, while you strive for dharma, great dharma will
be lost. While you keep your pact, you will have no wife. When a wife is
protected, the children are protected. When the children are protected, the self
is protected. I have heard the brahmins tell the dharmas of the classes: the
kshatriya has no dharma but to destroy his enemies.

"Kichaka kicked me before the Dharma King's eyes, and before yours too, mighty
Bhimasena. You saved me from that terrible Jatasura. You beat Jayadratha, with
your brothers. Kill this wicked man too, who dishonours me. Kichaka, as the
king's favourite, is a cause of grief to me, Bharata. Smash him, mad with lust,
like a pot on a stone, the cause of so many wrongs to me, Bharata. If the sun
rises in the morning on him alive, I shall mix poison and drink it. I will not
come into Kichaka's power. It is better for me to die before your eyes,
Bhimasena."

When she had said this, Krishna wept, leaning on Bhima's chest. Bhima embraced
her, comforted her greatly, and, licking the corners of his mouth, went to
Kichaka in his mind.
