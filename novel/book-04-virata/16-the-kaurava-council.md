# The Kaurava Council

Then King Duryodhana, on the field, spoke to Bhishma, to Drona, tiger among
chariot fighters, and to Kripa, the great chariot fighter. "Karna and I have
told the teacher this more than once. But I shall say it again, for I never tire
of saying it. When they were beaten, they were to live for twelve years in the
forest and for a year unrecognised in the country. That was our wager. That
thirteenth year of theirs, the year of living unrecognised, has not yet ended,
it is still going on, and here is Bibhatsu come up against us. If Bibhatsu has
come before the exile is over, the Pandavas will live in the forest for another
twelve years. Whether in their greed they have lost count, or we have fallen
into error, Bhishma should know whether their time is short or over. But there
is always doubt when a matter can go two ways. A thing is thought of one way and
turns out another.

"We were looking for the Matsya army to fight, towards the north. If Bibhatsu
has come, which of us would turn his back? We came here for the Trigartas' sake,
to fight the Matsyas. They told us of the many wrongs the Matsyas had done them.
They were terrified, and we promised them this: they were to seize the Matsyas'
great herd first, in the afternoon of the seventh day, and we were to seize it
again on the eighth at sunrise. That was our arrangement. Either they have not
seen the cattle, or they have been beaten, or they have tricked us and joined
with the Matsya. Or the Matsya has come against them with his people and his
whole army and has come to fight us. Or some mighty man among them, going ahead
of them, has come here to beat us, or it may be the Matsya himself. Whether this
is the king of the Matsyas or Bibhatsu who has come, we all have to fight. That
was our agreement.

"So why are these best of chariot fighters standing on their chariots, Bhishma,
Drona, Kripa, Vikarna and Drona's son, all these great chariot fighters,
confused in their minds at such a time? There is nothing better than battle. Set
your minds on that. If our herds were taken from us even by the god with the
thunderbolt, or by Yama, in battle, who could go back to Hastinapura? When the
foot soldiers are driven back by arrows and broken in the deep forest, who of
them would live? Even the horsemen may not be safe. Put the teacher behind you
and make a plan like that.

"He knows their minds, and so he is trying to frighten us. I see he has a
greater affection for Arjuna. When he sees Bibhatsu coming he praises him. Make
a plan so that the army does not break. It is in a great forest, in the hot
season, without guides, in the power of the enemy. Make a plan so that the army
does not scatter. What praise does the enemy deserve because horses neigh?
Horses always neigh, standing or moving. The winds always blow, Vasava always
sends rain, and the roll of thunder is often heard. What has this to do with
Partha, and why is he praised for it, except out of love, or out of hatred, or
out of anger with us alone?

"Teachers are merciful, wise and quick to see danger. They must never be
consulted when great danger comes. In fine palaces, in gatherings and in
lodgings, telling fine stories, the learned look well there. Doing many
wonderful things before the people, in archery and in fitting an arrow
gracefully, the learned look well there. In knowing others' weak points, in the
ways of men, and in the faults of cooking, the learned look well there. Put
behind you the learned, who praise the enemy's virtues, and make a plan by which
the enemy may be killed. Let the cattle be sent on, and form the army into
battle order without delay, and set guards where we are to fight the enemy."

---

"I see all these long-lived men are frightened, as if terrified, with no mind to
fight, and none of them steady," said Karna. "Whether it is the king of the
Matsyas or Bibhatsu who has come, I shall hold him back, as the shore holds the
sea. My arrows with their smooth joints, loosed from my bow, never turn back as
they go, like snakes gliding. Let my gold-feathered, sharp-pointed arrows,
loosed by my skilled hand, cover Partha as locusts cover a tree. Let the slap of
my palms be heard, as the arrows set to the string strike firmly against the
bowstring, like the sound of two drums being beaten. Bibhatsu has been held down
for eight years and five, and now, full of love for battle, he will strike at
me. Like a brahmin of good qualities, a worthy vessel for gifts, let the son of
Kunti receive the floods of arrows I loose in their thousands.

"This great archer is famous in the three worlds, and I too, best of the Kurus,
am in no way inferior to Arjuna. Let the sky be seen today covered as if with
fireflies, with golden arrows fitted with vulture feathers, loosed on this side
and that. Today I shall pay the debt I promised long ago to Dhritarashtra's son,
a debt that never runs out, by killing Arjuna in battle. Let the flight of the
feathered arrows be seen in the sky like locusts, as they are cut in two and
scattered between us. Partha's touch is like Indra's thunderbolt, his energy
like great Indra's. I shall wound him as one wounds an elephant with firebrands.
The Pandava is a fire hard to face, fed with swords, darts and arrows, blazing,
as if burning up his enemies. I shall put it out as a great cloud of arrows,
with the speed of my horses for its wind in front and the roar of my chariots
for its thunder. Let the arrows loosed from my bow, like venomous snakes, glide
into Partha as snakes glide into an anthill.

"Relying on the weapon I got from Jamadagni's son, best of seers, and on my own
strength, I could fight even Vasava. Let the monkey on the top of his banner be
struck down by me with a broad arrow today, and fall to the ground howling
terribly. Let the cry of the creatures that live in the banner, attacked by me
as they flee in all directions, reach heaven. Today I shall pull out, root and
all, the dart that has long been lodged in Duryodhana's heart, by bringing
Bibhatsu down from his chariot. Let the Kauravas see Partha today, his horses
killed, without his chariot, relying on his manhood, hissing like a snake. Let
the Kurus go if they like, taking the wealth with them, or let them stand on
their chariots and watch me fight."

---

"Your mind is always the cruellest in battle, son of Radha," said Kripa. "You do
not know the nature of things, or look at their consequences. There are many
policies, thought out from the treatises. Of them, those who know the past say
war is the worst. War joined at the right place and time brings victory. At the
wrong time the same war bears no fruit. Valour at the right place and time leads
to good. Let the gap between one's aims be judged by what favours them. The wise
do not decide the load of a carriage-maker's cart by talk.

"When we think it over, a clash with Partha is not right for us. Alone he
protected the Kurus, and alone he satisfied Agni. Alone he kept celibacy for
five years. Alone he set Subhadra on his chariot and challenged Krishna to
single combat. In this very forest Krishna won back Krishna when she was carried
off. Alone he learned the weapons from Shakra for five years. Alone he conquered
Samyamini and won fame for the Kurus. Alone that tamer of enemies beat
Chitrasena, king of the gandharvas, in battle by force, and his army, so hard to
beat. So too the Nivatakavachas and the Kalakhanja danavas, whom not even the
gods could kill, were brought down in battle by him alone. What have you ever
done alone, Karna, in the same way that each of them brought kings under his
power alone?

"Not even Indra is fit to fight Partha in battle. Whoever hopes to fight him
needs medicine. You want to raise your right hand and, without thinking, pull
out the fang of an angry venomous snake with your forefinger. Or you want to go
alone through the forest, climb a rutting elephant without a goad, and ride to
the city. Or you want to cross through the middle of a blazing fire fed with
ghee, fat and marrow, wearing bark clothes and smeared with ghee. If a man tied
himself up, fastened a great stone round his neck and tried to cross the sea
with his arms, what manhood would there be in that? Unskilled in weapons and
very weak, Karna, the man who wants to fight Partha, so skilled and strong, is a
fool.

"We cheated him and kept him here for thirteen years. A lion set loose from the
net, he will leave nothing of us. Partha was sitting apart, like fire hidden in
a well, and we fell on him in ignorance and have come into the greatest danger.
Let us fight Partha together, now that he has come, mad for battle. Let the
soldiers stand armed, ready to strike, in battle order. Drona, Duryodhana,
Bhishma, you, Drona's son and I, let us all fight Partha. Karna, do not do
anything rash. If we six chariot fighters stand together, we can fight Partha,
determined as he is, raised like the wielder of the thunderbolt. With the armies
in battle order and the greatest archers ready, let us fight Arjuna in battle as
the danavas fought Vasava."

---

"The cattle have not been won yet," said Ashvatthama, "nor have they crossed the
border, nor reached Hastinapura, and you, Karna, are boasting. Men who have won
many battles and gained great wealth and conquered the enemy's land say nothing
of their own manhood. Fire cooks without a word. The sun shines in silence. The
earth bears the worlds silently, with all that moves and does not move.

"The wise have laid down the work of the four classes, by which each may gain
wealth and do no wrong. A brahmin, having learned the Vedas, should sacrifice
for others and sacrifice himself. A kshatriya, relying on the bow, should
sacrifice but not sacrifice for others. A vaishya, having gained goods, should
have the rites of the brahmins done. Those fortunate men who live by the
treatises, even when they have won this earth, still honour their elders,
however unworthy. What kshatriya can be pleased at winning a kingdom by dicing,
in that cruel way, like any common man? What wise man would boast of wealth won
like that, by trickery and cheating, living like a bird-catcher?

"In what single combat did you beat Dhananjaya, or Nakula, or Sahadeva, whose
wealth you took? In which battle did you beat Yudhishthira, and Bhima, the best
of the strong? In which battle did you conquer Indraprastha long ago? And in
which battle did you win Krishna, you evil-doer, dragged into the hall in a
single garment, in her season? You cut through their great root as a man cuts a
sandal tree for its heartwood. When you made them do servants' work, hero, what
did Vidura say then? As far as we can see, men and other creatures, even worms
and ants, bear what they can. The Pandava cannot forgive that suffering of
Draupadi's. Dhananjaya has appeared to bring sorrow to Dhritarashtra's sons.

"And now you want to play the wise man and make speeches here. Jishnu, who
brings enmity to its end, will leave nothing of us. The son of Kunti,
Dhananjaya, would not refuse to fight out of fear, not gods, not gandharvas, not
asuras, not rakshasas. Whomever he falls on in anger in battle, he will strike
down like a tree struck by Garuda's speed. Greater than you in valour, equal to
the king of the immortals with the bow, equal to Vasudeva in battle, who would
not honour Partha? He would meet the divine with the divine and the human with
the human. He would strike weapon with weapon. What man is equal to Arjuna?
Those who know dharma know that a pupil comes next after a son. For that reason
too the Pandava is dear to Drona.

"As you played at dice, as you took Indraprastha, as you brought Krishna into
the hall, so now fight the Pandava. Here is your wise uncle, who knows the
dharma of kshatriyas, Shakuni of Gandhara, the crooked dicer. Let him fight
here. The Gandiva does not throw the kṛta or the dvāpara. The Gandiva throws
blazing, sharp, whetted arrows. The well-sharpened arrows with vulture feathers,
loosed from the Gandiva, do not stop halfway even through mountains; they split
them. Death the Ender, the Calmer, Mrityu, and the mare-headed fire under the
sea might leave something behind, but not Dhananjaya in his anger. Let the
teacher fight if he likes. I shall not fight Dhananjaya. It is the Matsya we
have to fight, if he comes after the cattle."

---

"Drona sees rightly, and Kripa sees rightly too," said Bhishma. "But Karna wants
to fight properly, by the dharma of kshatriyas. A man of understanding should
not blame the teacher. Having looked at the place and the time, one should
fight. That is my view. Why should a wise man not be troubled when five enemies
like suns, ready to strike, are rising? Everyone is confused about his own
interest, even those who know dharma. So I say this, king, if you like it.

"What Karna said to us was said to raise our spirits. Let the teacher's son
forgive it. A great work is before us. This is no time for a quarrel, now that
the son of Kunti has come. You must forgive everything, you and the teacher and
Kripa. Your mastery of weapons is like the light in the sun. As the mark on the
moon can never be removed, so brahmin learning and the brahma weapon are fixed
in you. The four Vedas are found in one place and the power of a kshatriya in
another. We have never heard of both of them together in anyone except the
teacher of the Bharatas and his son. That is my view. The brahma weapon and the
Vedas are not found together anywhere else.

"Let the teacher's son forgive. This is no time for division among ourselves.
Let us all join together and fight the son of the Punisher of Paka, who has
come. Of the troubles of an army that the wise have named, division is the
chief, and the wise think it the worst."

"Let the teacher himself forgive, then," said Ashvatthama. "Let peace be made
here. When the teacher was blamed, what I said was said in anger."

Then Duryodhana asked Drona's pardon, Bharata, together with Karna, Bhishma and
great-souled Kripa.

"I was pleased by the very first words that Bhishma, Shantanu's son, spoke,"
said Drona. "Let the next thing be arranged. Make a plan so that no one attacks
Duryodhana while he is off his guard, out of rashness or confusion. If the exile
were not over, Dhananjaya would not show himself. Nor will he forgive us today
unless he gets back the cattle. Make a plan so that he does not attack
Dhritarashtra's sons, and so that we are not beaten. Duryodhana too spoke
earlier in the same way. Remember it, son of Ganga, and say what is right."

---

"The parts and portions of time, dear son, the moments and days, the half-months
and months, the lunar mansions and planets, the seasons and the years, all are
joined together," said Bhishma. "So, by the division of time, the wheel of time
turns. Because of the excess of time and the shifting of the heavenly bodies,
two months are added in every fifth year. For these thirteen years, I think, the
extra comes to five months and twelve nights. Everything they promised has been
carried out exactly. Knowing this for certain, Bibhatsu has come.

"They are all great-souled and all know dharma and profit. With Yudhishthira as
their king, why would they go wrong in dharma? The sons of Kunti are not greedy,
and they have done hard things. They would not want the kingdom by any wrong
means alone. Even then, the joys of the Kurus wanted to show their valour. But
they were bound by the noose of dharma and did not stray from the vow of
kshatriyas. The Parthas would choose death rather than be called liars or be
brought low, never falsehood. But when the time has come, those bulls among men
will not give up what is theirs to get, even if it is guarded by the wielder of
the thunderbolt. Such is the Pandavas' valour. We are to fight the best of all
who bear weapons in battle. So let whatever good the good do in the world be
done quickly, so that our interest does not pass to others.

"I have never seen a battle in which victory was certain for one side, Kaurava,
king of kings, and Dhananjaya has come. Once a battle begins, being and not
being, victory and defeat, one of them surely touches each side. That has been
seen, no doubt of it. So, king of kings, let what belongs to war be done
quickly, or what agrees with dharma, for Dhananjaya has come."

"I shall not give the Pandavas the kingdom, grandfather," said Duryodhana. "Let
what belongs to war be arranged quickly."

"Hear what I think about this, if you like it," said Bhishma. "Take a quarter of
the army quickly and go towards the city. Let another quarter take the cattle
and go. We shall fight the Pandava with half the army, or the Matsya if he comes
back, or even Shatakratu. Let the teacher stand in the middle and Ashvatthama on
the left. Let wise Kripa, son of Sharadvat, guard the right flank. Let Karna,
the suta's son, stand in front in his armour. I shall stand behind the whole
army and protect it."

**Divergences.**

* **Found out too soon?** The CE settles this in Bhishma's reckoning: with the extra months, the thirteen years are complete, with five months and twelve nights over (4.47.3–5), and the narrator has already said the time had passed (4.30.2–3). Rejected lines (\*854–\*855) add that the year ended the day before, and give a rule for the extra month. See L-168.
* **Not even a village.** A rejected line (\*859) has Duryodhana refuse even a village, an army, a slave girl or the smallest thing. In the CE he says only, "I shall not give the Pandavas the kingdom" (4.47.15).
* **Kripa's list.** Rejected lines (\*832–\*834) add Arjuna's fight with Shiva as a hunter, his winning the great weapon from Indra and his winning of Draupadi. Not narrated.
* **Karna's retort.** A rejected appendix passage (@42) gives Karna an angry answer to Ashvatthama. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*803–\*831, \*835–\*853, \*856–\*858, \*860–\*870, @41) add details. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 4.42.11 *saptamīm aparāhṇe … aṣṭamyām … ādityasyodayaṃ prati*: "in the afternoon of the seventh day … on the eighth at sunrise".
* 4.42.29 *annasaṃskāradoṣeṣu*: "in the faults of cooking". Duryodhana is sneering.
* 4.43.6 *varṣāṇy aṣṭau ca pañca ca*: "for eight years and five".
* 4.43.10: the "debt" is Karna's promise to kill Arjuna (3.243.15, see L-151).
* 4.43.16: the weapon from Rama Jamadagnya.
* 4.44.4 *bhāraṃ hi rathakārasya na vyavasyanti paṇḍitāḥ*: "The wise do not decide the load of a carriage-maker's cart by talk." The sense is uncertain; it seems to mean that talk does not settle what can be carried.
* 4.44.6: "Krishna won back Krishna": Arjuna (Krishna) won back Draupadi (Krishna) from Jayadratha (3.255).
* 4.44.7 *sāṃyaminīṃ jitvā*: "conquered Samyamini". The sense is uncertain; Ganguli, from another reading, has "vanquishing all foes".
* 4.45.8 *vaitaṃsikaḥ*: "a bird-catcher", one who lives by snares.
* 4.45.11 *ekavastrā … rajasvalā*: "in a single garment, in her season", as at 2.60.
* 4.45.23 *kṛta*, *dvāpara*: the throws of the dice (see Book 2).
* 4.46.8–9: Bhishma's praise of Drona and Ashvatthama is kept.
* 4.47.3–4: Bhishma's reckoning is given as he says it. Two months are added every five years, and the thirteen years yield five months and twelve nights over.
