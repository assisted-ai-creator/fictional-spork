# The Dance Hall

"I shall do as you say, dear, timid one," said Bhimasena. "Today I shall kill
Kichaka and his kin. Tomorrow evening, when night comes on, make a meeting with
him, Yajnaseni. Shake off your sorrow and grief, bright-smiling one. There is a
dance hall the king of the Matsyas has had built. The girls dance there by day
and go home at night. There is a bed there, timid one, strong and firmly set.
There I shall show him his forefathers, who died before him. Only take care,
lovely one, that no one sees you making the arrangement with him, and that he
comes to that place."

When they had spoken like this, the two of them shed tears in their grief. They
held out through what was left of that dreadful night, with the thing in their
hearts.

When the night had passed, Kichaka rose in the morning, went to the palace and
said this to Draupadi. "I threw you down in the hall and kicked you before the
king's eyes. You were in the power of a stronger man, and you found no one to
protect you. This one is called the king of the Matsyas only in name. I am the
real king of the Matsyas, the lord of the army. So take your pleasure willingly.
I shall be your slave, timid one. I shall give you a hundred gold pieces every
day, lovely-hipped one. I would give you a hundred slave women and as many slave
men, and a chariot drawn by mares. Let us come together, timid one."

"Agree to one condition of mine today, Kichaka," said Draupadi. "No friend or
brother of yours must know that you have met me. For I am afraid of being found
out by the glorious gandharvas. Promise me this, and then I am in your power."

"I shall do just as you say, lovely-hipped one," said Kichaka. "I shall come
alone, dear, to your empty lodging, to be with you, woman with thighs like
plantain stems, mad with love for you, so that the gandharvas, bright as the
sun, will not find you out."

"There is a dance hall the king of the Matsyas has had built," said Draupadi.
"The girls dance there by day and go home at night. Go there in the dark. The
gandharvas do not know that place. There we shall be free of any fault, without
a doubt."

As Krishna talked the matter over with Kichaka, king, the half a day that
remained became as long as a month to her. Then Kichaka went home, overflowing
with joy. The fool did not know that the sairandhri was death in disguise. He
was more than ever taken up with perfumes, ornaments and garlands, and in his
hurry, dazed with desire, he dressed himself up. As he did it, the time seemed
long to him, thinking only of her, the woman with long eyes. His beauty grew
brighter just as he was about to lose it, like the wick of a lamp flaring when
it is about to go out. Trusting her word, Kichaka, dazed with desire, did not
notice the day go by as he thought of their meeting.

---

Then lovely Draupadi went to the kitchen and came near to Bhima, her husband,
the Kauravya. The woman with beautiful hair said to him, "I have made a meeting
with Kichaka in the dance hall, as you told me, scorcher of enemies. Kichaka
will come alone at night to the empty dance hall, strong-armed one. Kill that
Kichaka there. Go to the dance hall, son of Kunti, and take the life out of that
suta's son, Kichaka, drunk with pride, Pandava. In his arrogance that suta's son
despises the gandharvas. Best of fighters, pull him up as an elephant pulls up a
reed. Wipe away the tears of my grief, Bharata. Bless you, do honour to yourself
and to your family."

"Welcome, lovely one, since you bring me good news," said Bhimasena. "I want no
one's help against him, beautiful one. The joy you have given me with this
meeting with Kichaka is the joy I had when I killed Hidimba, beautiful one. I
tell you this truly, by my brothers and by dharma. I shall kill Kichaka as the
lord of the gods killed Vritra. In secret or in the open I shall crush Kichaka.
And if the Matsyas find out, I shall surely kill them too. Then I shall kill
Duryodhana and win back the earth. And let Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, go on
waiting on the Matsya if he likes."

"Kill Kichaka in secret, hero," said Draupadi, "so that you do not have to give
up the truth of your pledge for my sake, lord."

"I shall do just as you say, timid one," said Bhimasena. "Unseen by him,
faultless one, in the dark, I shall crush the head of that wicked Kichaka, who
wants what he cannot have, as an elephant treads on a bel fruit."

Bhima went there first at night and sat down, hidden. Unseen, he waited for
Kichaka, as a lion waits for a deer.

Kichaka too dressed himself as he pleased and came at the appointed time to the
dance hall, hoping to meet Panchali. Thinking it the meeting place, he went into
the room. And when he had gone into that great chamber, wrapped in darkness, the
fool came to Bhima of matchless energy, who had come there before him and was
waiting apart. There on the bed the suta touched death, lying there, blazing
with an anger born of the wrong done to Krishna.

Kichaka came up to him, dazed with desire, his mind and heart churned with
delight. Smiling, he said, "I have sent you wealth of many kinds, endless
wealth. With all of it meant for you, I have come to you in haste. It is not for
nothing that the women of my house always praise me. 'You are well dressed and
handsome. There is no other man like you.'"

"How lucky that you are handsome," said Bhimasena. "How lucky that you praise
yourself. But you have never felt a touch like this before."

---

With that, strong-armed Bhima of terrible valour leapt up and laughed at that
worst of men. The son of Kunti seized him by the hair, garlanded and sweetly
scented. Seized by the hair with force, that best of strong men pulled his hair
free with a jerk and grabbed the Pandava in his arms.

Then there was a fight with bare arms between those two angry lions among men,
like two mighty elephants fighting over a she-elephant in spring. Bhima
staggered a little, and his footing was unsteady. Then mighty Kichaka, in his
anger, threw him to the ground with his knees. Thrown to the ground by powerful
Kichaka, Bhima sprang up at once, like a snake struck with a stick. Mad with
strength and rivalry, the suta and the Pandava, both powerful, dragged each
other about in the middle of the night, in the deserted place. That best of
buildings shook again and again, and the two of them roared at each other in
their fury. Struck on the chest by Bhima's palms, mighty Kichaka, burning with
anger, did not move a single step.

For a short while he held out on the ground against that unbearable force. Then
the suta, worn down by Bhima's strength, began to weaken. Seeing him weaken,
mighty Bhimasena pulled him to his chest with force and crushed him until he
lost his senses. Filled with anger, Vrikodara, best of victors, breathed hard
and seized him again, firmly, by the hair. Holding Kichaka, mighty Bhima roared,
like a tiger that longs for flesh when it has caught a great deer.

He pushed Kichaka's feet, his hands, his head and his neck all into his body, as
the bearer of the Pinaka bow did with the beast. When every limb was crushed and
he was made into a ball of flesh, mighty Bhimasena showed him to Krishna. The
son of Pandu, full of energy, said to Draupadi, "Come, Panchali, look at him.
See what has been made of this lover."

So he killed Kichaka, and his anger was calmed. He said goodbye to Draupadi
Krishna and quickly went back to the kitchen.

When she had had Kichaka killed, Draupadi, best of women, was full of joy, and
her grief was gone. She said to the guards of the hall, "Here lies Kichaka,
killed by the gandharvas, my husbands. He was mad with desire for another man's
wife. Come and see."

When they heard what she said, the watchmen of the dance hall came running with
torches in their thousands. They went into the building and saw Kichaka there,
struck down, lifeless on the ground, soaked in blood. "Where is his neck? Where
are his feet? Where are his hands, and where is his head?" So they said as they
looked at him, killed by a gandharva.

**Divergences.**

* **Bhima's disguise.** Many retellings have Bhima wait for Kichaka dressed as a woman, in Draupadi's place. In the CE he simply goes first at night and lies on the bed in the dark (4.21.38, 4.21.42). Rejected lines say that Ballava "changed his form" (\*433) and that he was covered with a soft white cloth (\*436), but none says he dressed as a woman. See L-165.
* **Virata summons Kichaka.** A rejected appendix passage (@22) has Virata call Kichaka and warn him about the sairandhri. Not narrated.
* **Kichaka's boasts.** Rejected lines (\*442, \*444) give Kichaka and Bhima longer speeches about his looks and his skill with women; another (\*445) has Bhima tell him his sister will see him dragged on the ground. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*409–\*441, \*443, \*446–\*447) add details of the arrangement and the fight. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 4.21.2 *asyāḥ pradoṣe śarvaryāḥ*: "when night comes on". The meeting is on the evening of the coming day, as the text then shows (4.21.7, 4.21.18).
* 4.21.4 *darśayiṣyāmi pūrvapretān pitāmahān*: "I shall show him his forefathers, who died before him".
* 4.21.10 *niṣka*: "gold pieces".
* 4.21.15 *rambhoru*: "woman with thighs like plantain stems".
* 4.21.18: "the half a day that remained became as long as a month to her".
* 4.21.19 *sairandhrīrūpiṇaṃ … mṛtyum*: "death in disguise", literally "death in the form of the sairandhri".
* 4.21.22: the lamp simile is kept.
* 4.21.28 *naḍaṃ nāga ivoddhara*: "as an elephant pulls up a reed".
* 4.21.37 *nāgo bilvam ivākramya*: "as an elephant treads on a bel fruit".
* 4.21.42 *mṛtyuṃ sūtaḥ parāmṛśat*: "the suta touched death".
* 4.21.59 *paśor iva pinākadhṛk*: "as the bearer of the Pinaka bow did with the beast", Shiva with the sacrificial animal.
* 4.21.63 *sabhāpālān*: "the guards of the hall". At 4.21.65 they are *nartanāgārarakṣiṇaḥ*, "the watchmen of the dance hall".
