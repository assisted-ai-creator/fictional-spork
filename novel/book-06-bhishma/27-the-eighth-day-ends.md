summary: The evening of the eighth day. Duryodhana wounds Bhima, and Bhima's arrows leave Drona senseless. Nila of Anupa fights Ashvatthama, and Ghatotkacha's magic makes the Kauravas see their own leaders lying dead, so that they run to their camp though Sanjaya and Bhishma shout that it is only a rakshasa's trick. Duryodhana begs Bhishma to kill Ghatotkacha, and Bhishma sends Bhagadatta on his elephant Supratika. Ghatotkacha catches Bhagadatta's spear in the air and breaks it over his knee. Bhima kills nine more of Dhritarashtra's sons, Abhimanyu unhorses the king of the Ambashthas, and night ends the fighting.

# The Eighth Day Ends

"Seeing his own army killed," said Sanjaya, "King Duryodhana himself charged
Bhimasena, the tamer of enemies, in anger. Taking his very great bow, which
sounded like Indra's thunderbolt, he covered the Pandava with a great shower of
arrows. In anger he fitted a very sharp crescent-headed arrow, fledged with
hair, and cut Bhimasena's bow. Seeing that opening, the great chariot fighter
quickly fitted a sharp arrow that could split even mountains, and with it the
strong-armed man struck Bhimasena in the chest. Badly wounded and in pain,
licking the corners of his mouth, the splendid man clung to his banner adorned
with gold.

"Seeing Bhimasena dejected like that, Ghatotkacha blazed up with anger like a
fire about to burn. The great chariot fighters of the Pandavas, led by
Abhimanyu, charged the king too, shouting, alarmed. Seeing them come on, angry
and alarmed, Bharadvaja's son said to your great chariot fighters, 'Go quickly,
bless you, and guard the king. He has come into the greatest danger and is
sinking in an ocean of disaster. These angry great archers, the great chariot
fighters of the Pandavas, have put Bhimasena in front and are attacking
Duryodhana. Loosing weapons of many kinds, set on victory, they roar terrible
roars and terrify this earth.'

"Hearing the teacher's words, your men led by Somadatta went against the army of
the Pandavas. Kripa, Bhurishravas, Shalya, Drona's son, Vivimshati, Chitrasena,
Vikarna, the Saindhava, Brihadbala and the two great archers of Avanti
surrounded the Kaurava. They went twenty steps and began to fight, the Pandavas
and the sons of Dhritarashtra, each wanting to kill the other.

"Having said this, strong-armed Bharadvaja's son stretched his great bow and
struck Bhima with twenty-six arrows. Again the strong-armed man quickly covered
him with arrows, as a cloud in autumn covers a mountain with streams. The mighty
great archer Bhimasena quickly pierced him back in the left side with ten
arrows. Badly wounded, in pain, and old in years, Bharata, he suddenly lost his
senses and sat down on the floor of his chariot.

"Seeing the teacher in pain, King Duryodhana himself and the son of Drona
charged Bhimasena in anger. Strong-armed Bhimasena saw those two coming on like
Yama the Ender at the end of time. He quickly took his mace, leapt down from his
chariot and stood like a mountain, raising the heavy mace like Yama's staff in
the battle. Seeing him with his mace raised, like Kailasa with its peaks, the
Kaurava and Drona's son charged him together. As those two best of the mighty
came at him together, Vrikodara charged them at speed. Seeing him charge, angry
and terrible to see, the great chariot fighters of the Kauravas rushed at him,
all led by Bharadvaja's son, wanting to kill Bhimasena. They brought down
weapons of many kinds on Bhima's chest, and together they pressed the Pandava on
every side.

"The great chariot fighters of the Pandavas, led by Abhimanyu, saw the great
chariot fighter in danger and hard pressed. They charged, wanting to save him,
giving up the lives that are so hard to give up. The brave lord of Anupa,
Bhima's dear friend Nila, who looked like a dark cloud, charged Drona's son in
anger. The great archer was always a rival of Drona's son. He stretched his
great bow and pierced Drona's son with a feathered arrow, great king. So Shakra
long ago pierced the danava Viprachitti, that terror of the gods, so hard to
face, who had terrified the three worlds in his anger with his own power. Split
by Nila's well-aimed feathered arrow, bleeding and full of anger, Drona's son,
best of the wise, stretched his bright bow, which sounded like Indra's
thunderbolt, and set his mind on destroying Nila. He fitted bright broad arrows
sharpened by the smith, killed his four horses and brought down his banner. With
a seventh broad arrow he pierced Nila in the chest, and Nila, badly wounded and
in pain, sat down on the floor of his chariot.

"Seeing King Nila, like a mass of cloud, in a faint, Ghatotkacha too, in anger,
surrounded by his brothers, charged at speed at Drona's son, who shone in
battle. So did the other rakshasas, mad in battle. Seeing that rakshasa of
terrible looks coming, splendid Bharadvaja's son quickly charged him. In anger
he killed the rakshasas of terrible looks who had come on angrily in front of
the rakshasa, his forerunners. Seeing them turned away by the arrows from
Drona's son's bow, huge Ghatotkacha, Bhimasena's son, grew angry. The master of
magic, lord of rakshasas, made a great magic, terrible in form and most cruel,
bewildering Drona's son in the battle.

"Then all your men were turned away by that magic. They saw each other cut down
on the ground, writhing, wretched, soaked in blood: Drona, Duryodhana, Shalya
and Ashvatthama, and most of the great archers who were chief among the
Kauravas. All the chariot fighters were smashed, the elephants struck down, and
the horses cut to pieces with their riders by the thousand. Seeing that, your
army ran towards the camp, king, though I and Devavrata shouted, 'Fight! Do not
run! This is a rakshasa's magic in the battle, made by Ghatotkacha!' Bewildered,
they did not stand. In their fear they did not believe what the two of us said.

"Seeing them run, the victorious Pandavas with Ghatotkacha gave lion roars, and
the sound of conches and drums rang loudly on every side. So your whole army,
broken by Hidimba's evil-hearted son, ran in every direction at the time of
sunset.

"In that great uproar King Duryodhana went to Ganga's son, greeted him humbly
and began to tell him all that had happened. Sighing again and again, the
unassailable man told him of Ghatotkacha's victory and his own defeat. Then he
said to Bhishma, grandfather of the Kurus, 'Relying on you, as the enemy rely on
Vasudeva, I began this terrible war with the Pandavas, lord. The eleven famous
armies that are mine stand under your command with me, scorcher of enemies. And
yet I have been beaten in battle, tiger of the Bharatas, by the Pandavas led by
Bhimasena, relying on Ghatotkacha. It burns my limbs as fire burns a dry tree.
So, fortunate one, scorcher of enemies, by your grace I want to kill that
outcast among rakshasas myself, grandfather, relying on you, the unassailable.
You should do that for me.'

"Hearing the king's words, best of the Bharatas, Bhishma, son of Shantanu, said
this to Duryodhana. 'Listen, king, to what I shall tell you, Kaurava, about how
you should act, great king, scorcher of enemies. In battle you must always guard
yourself, my son, in every state, tamer of enemies. You must always fight the
Dharma king, blameless one, or Arjuna, or the twins, or Bhimasena. Holding to
the dharma of kings, a king goes against a king. I, Drona, Kripa, Drona's son,
Kritavarma the Satvata, Shalya, Somadatta's son and Vikarna, the great chariot
fighter, and your brave brothers led by Duhshasana will fight that mighty
rakshasa for your sake. Or, if your heart is set on that fierce lord of
rakshasas, let this man go into battle against the evil-minded one: King
Bhagadatta, who is Purandara's equal in battle.'

"Having said this much to the king, the master of speech then spoke to
Bhagadatta in front of the lord of kings. 'Go quickly, great king, against
Hidimba's son, mad in battle. Hold him back in the battle with care, while all
the bowmen watch, that rakshasa of cruel deeds, as Indra held back Taraka long
ago. You have divine weapons and valour, scorcher of enemies, and you have met
many asuras in battle before. You are his match in the great battle, tiger among
kings. Surrounded by your own army, king, kill that bull among rakshasas.'

"Hearing those words of Bhishma, the commander of the army, he went quickly with
a lion roar, facing the enemy. He charged, roaring like a thundercloud, and the
great chariot fighters of the Pandavas came against him in anger. They were
Bhimasena, Abhimanyu and the rakshasa Ghatotkacha, the sons of Draupadi,
Satyadhriti and Kshatradeva, sir, the lord of the Chedis, Vasudana, and the lord
of the Dasharnas. And Bhagadatta charged them on Supratika. Then there was a
terrible battle, fearful to see, between the Pandavas and Bhagadatta, filling
Yama's kingdom.

"Arrows of terrible speed, well sharpened, loosed by chariot fighters, fell on
elephants and chariots, great king. Great elephants in rut, trained by their
riders, met each other and closed fearlessly. Blind with rut, furious, they met
in the great battle and split each other with the points of their tusks, their
tusks like pestles. Horses with plumes on their heads, ridden by men holding
lances, driven quickly by their riders, fell on each other. Foot soldiers,
struck with spears and javelins by masses of foot soldiers, fell to the ground
by the hundred and the thousand. And chariot fighters, king, killing heroes in
the battle with barbed arrows and reeds, roared lion roars.

"As that hair-raising battle went on, the great archer Bhagadatta charged
Bhimasena on an elephant in rut, streaming rut from seven places like a mountain
streaming water on every side. From the head of Supratika he scattered thousands
of arrows, blameless one, as Maghavan on Airavata scatters streams of rain. The
king struck Bhima with streams of arrows, as a cloud in the rains strikes a
mountain with streams of water. But Bhimasena in anger killed more than a
hundred of his foot guards with showers of arrows. Seeing them killed, mighty
Bhagadatta in anger drove the lordly elephant at Bhimasena's chariot. Sent by
him, the elephant charged Bhimasena, tamer of enemies, at speed, like an arrow
driven by the bowstring.

"Seeing it come, the great chariot fighters of the Pandavas, led by Bhimasena,
came against it at speed. The Kekayas came, and Abhimanyu, all the sons of
Draupadi, the brave lord of the Dasharnas and Kshatradeva, sir, and the lord of
the Chedis, Chitraketu, all in anger. Showing the finest divine weapons, the
mighty men surrounded that one elephant in anger on every side. Pierced by many
arrows, bleeding, the great elephant shone like the king of mountains streaked
with red ores.

"Then the lord of the Dasharnas, on an elephant like a mountain, charged
Bhagadatta's elephant. But Supratika, lord of elephants, held back that elephant
as it came on in the battle, as the shore holds back the sea. Seeing great
Dasharna's lordly elephant held back, the Pandava armies cried out, 'Well done!
Well done!' Then the angry lord of Pragjyotisha sent fourteen javelins at the
front of that elephant, best of kings. They split its armour and its gold-worked
face guard and went into it quickly, like snakes into an anthill. Badly wounded
and in pain, the elephant's rut stopped, best of the Bharatas, and it quickly
turned back at speed. It ran at speed, trumpeting a terrible trumpet, crushing
its own army as the wind crushes trees by force.

"When that elephant had been beaten, the great chariot fighters of the Pandavas
gave a loud lion roar and stood ready for battle. Then, putting Bhima in front,
they charged Bhagadatta, scattering arrows of many kinds and weapons of many
kinds. Hearing the terrible roar of those angry, unforgiving men as they came
on, the great archer Bhagadatta, his fear gone in his anger, urged on his own
elephant. Driven by the goad and the toe, that best of elephants became in a
moment like the fire at the end of the world. In great anger it crushed masses
of chariots, elephants, horses with their riders and foot soldiers by the
hundred and the thousand, running this way and that in the battle, king. Churned
up by it, the great army of the Pandavas shrank, great king, like leather put on
a fire.

"Seeing his own army broken by wise Bhagadatta, Ghatotkacha charged Bhagadatta
in anger. The monstrous man, king, with a blazing mouth and blazing eyes, took
on a fearful form, as if blazing with fury. The mighty one took a huge spear
that could split even mountains and suddenly hurled it, wanting to kill the
elephant. It was wrapped on every side with flames and sparks. Seeing it come
suddenly in the battle, full of flames, the king shot a beautiful, sharp
crescent-headed arrow and cut that very great, swift spear with it. The spear,
adorned with gold, fell cut in two, like a great thunderbolt loosed by Shakra
falling from the sky. Seeing the spear fallen and cut in two, the king took a
great lance with a golden shaft, like a flame of fire, and hurled it at the
rakshasa, saying, 'Stand! Stand!' Seeing it coming like a thunderbolt in the
sky, the rakshasa leapt up quickly, caught it and roared. Quickly he put it
across his knee and broke it, Bharata, while the lord of kings watched, and it
was a marvel. Seeing that deed done by the mighty rakshasa, the gods in heaven
with the gandharvas, and the sages too, were amazed. And the Pandava great
archers led by Bhimasena made the earth ring with the cry, 'Well done! Well
done!'

"Hearing that great roar from the glad great men, the mighty great archer
Bhagadatta could not bear it. Stretching his great bow, which sounded like
Indra's thunderbolt, he charged the great chariot fighters of the Pandavas at
speed, loosing bright, sharp iron arrows blazing like fire. He pierced Bhima
with one, the rakshasa with nine arrows, Abhimanyu with three and the Kekayas
with five. With a gold-feathered arrow drawn full and loosed he split
Kshatradeva's right arm in the battle, and his fine bow fell suddenly with its
arrow. He struck the five sons of Draupadi with five arrows, and in anger he
killed Bhimasena's horses. With three arrows he cut his lion banner, and with
three others he pierced his charioteer. Badly wounded by Bhagadatta in the
battle, Vishoka sat down in pain on the floor of the chariot, best of the
Bharatas. Then Bhima, great king, best of chariot fighters, without a chariot,
took his mace and leapt at speed from the great chariot. Seeing him with his
mace raised, like a mountain with its peak, a terrible fear came on your men,
Bharata.

"At that very time the Pandava whose charioteer is Krishna came up, great king,
killing enemies by the thousand. He came to where those two tigers among men,
the father and son, scorchers of enemies, Bhimasena and Ghatotkacha, were locked
in battle with the lord of Pragjyotisha. Seeing the great chariot fighters
fighting, best of the Bharatas, the Pandava came quickly there, scattering
arrows. Then King Duryodhana, the great chariot fighter, quickly urged on the
army, crowded with chariots, elephants and horses. The Pandava with the white
horses charged at speed that great army of the Kauravas as it suddenly came on.
Bhagadatta too, on that elephant, Bharata, crushed the Pandava army in the
battle and charged Yudhishthira. Then there was a tumultuous battle, sir,
between Bhagadatta and the Panchalas, the Srinjayas and the Kekayas with their
weapons raised. And in the battle Bhimasena told Keshava and Arjuna, those two,
the whole grievous story of Iravan's killing, as it had happened.

"When Dhananjaya heard that his son Iravan had been killed, he was filled with
great grief and sighed like a snake. In the battle, king, he said this to
Vasudeva: 'Surely the wise Vidura, of great understanding, saw long ago this
terrible destruction of the Kurus and the Pandavas. That is why he tried to hold
back King Dhritarashtra. Many heroes who should not be killed have been killed
in battle by the Kauravas, Madhusudana, and we have killed theirs. For the sake
of wealth, best of men, a vile deed is being done. Curse the wealth for which
this destruction of kinsmen is done! Death is better for a man without wealth
than wealth from the killing of kin. What shall we gain, Krishna, by killing the
kinsmen who have gathered here?

"'Through Duryodhana's wrongdoing, and Shakuni, son of Subala's, and through
Karna's bad counsel, the kshatriyas go to their deaths. Now I understand,
Madhusudana, that the king did well, strong-armed one, when he begged Suyodhana
for half the kingdom or five villages. And that evil-minded man would not give
them. Seeing brave kshatriyas lying on the ground, I blame myself bitterly.
Curse the kshatriya's way of life! These kshatriyas will think me weak in
battle. And fighting these kinsmen is hateful to me, Madhusudana. Drive the
horses quickly towards the army of the son of Dhritarashtra. I will cross this
ocean of battle, with its far shore, with my arms. This is no time to act like a
eunuch, Madhava, anywhere.'

"Told this by Partha, Keshava, killer of enemy heroes, drove on the pale horses,
as swift as the wind. Then there was a great noise from your army, Bharata, like
the sea at the full moon when its force is whipped up by the wind. In the
afternoon, great king, a battle began between Bhishma and the Pandavas, rumbling
like Parjanya.

"Then, king, your sons charged Bhimasena, surrounding Drona in the battle as the
Vasus surround Vasava. Then Bhishma, son of Shantanu, Kripa, best of chariot
fighters, Bhagadatta and Susharma charged Dhananjaya. Hridika's son and Bahlika
charged Satyaki, and the king of the Ambashthas held back Abhimanyu. The rest,
great king, met the rest of the great chariot fighters. Then a battle began,
terrible in form and full of fear.

"Seeing your sons, lord of men, Bhimasena blazed up in anger in the battle like
fire fed with butter. Your sons covered Kunti's son with arrows, great king, as
clouds in the rains cover a mountain. Covered many times over by your sons, lord
of the people, the hero licked the corners of his mouth like a proud tiger.
Then, king, Bhima brought down Vyudhoraska with a very sharp razor arrow, and he
lost his life. With another broad arrow, yellow and sharp, he brought down
Kundalin, as a lion brings down a small beast. Then he took up sharp, yellow
arrows and quickly reached seven of your sons, sir. Sent by strong-bowed
Bhimasena, those arrows brought your sons, those great chariot fighters, down
from their chariots: Anadhrishti, Kundabheda, Vairata, Dirghalochana,
Dirghabahu, Subahu and Kanakadhvaja. As they fell, the heroes shone, bull of the
Bharatas, like flowering mango trees fallen in spring. Then your other sons ran,
lord of the people, thinking mighty Bhimasena was Time itself.

"Drona covered the hero with arrows on every side as he burned up your sons in
the battle, as clouds cover a mountain with streams. There we saw a marvel of
manhood in Kunti's son: though Drona held him back, he killed your sons. As a
bull bears the rain falling from the sky, so Bhima bore the shower of arrows
Drona loosed. Vrikodara did a marvellous thing there, great king: he killed your
sons in the battle and fought Drona too. Arjuna's elder brother played among
your heroic sons, great king, like a mighty tiger moving among deer. As a wolf
among cattle scatters the cattle, so Vrikodara scattered your sons in the
battle.

"Ganga's son, Bhagadatta and Gautama, the great chariot fighter, held back the
fierce Pandava Arjuna in the battle. Holding off their weapons with his weapons,
that chariot fighter beyond the rest sent the heroes in your army to Death.
Abhimanyu made the famous king of the Ambashthas, best of chariot fighters,
chariotless with his arrows. Without his chariot, being killed by famous
Subhadra's son, the lord of men leapt quickly down from his chariot, ashamed. He
hurled his sword at great Subhadra's son in the battle and climbed into great
Hridika's son's chariot. As the sword came at him, Subhadra's son, killer of
enemy heroes, skilled in the ways of battle, dodged it with his speed. Seeing
the sword dodged by Subhadra's son in the battle, the armies cried, 'Well done!
Well done!' lord of the people.

"Others, led by Dhrishtadyumna, fought your army, and in the same way all your
men fought the Pandava army. There was a great outcry there from yours and
theirs, Bharata, as they killed each other hard and did hard deeds. Brave men
seized each other by the hair in the battle, sir, and fought with nails and
teeth, with fists and knees, with arms and slaps, and with well-sharpened
swords. Finding an opening, they sent each other to Yama's house. Father killed
son and son father in the battle. Men fought there with their minds in turmoil.

"On the field lay the beautiful bows, gold-backed, of the killed, thrown down,
Bharata, and costly quivers. Sharp arrows with gold and silver feathers, washed
in oil, shone like snakes that have shed their skins. There were swords with
ivory hilts adorned with gold, and the thrown-down gold-backed shields of the
bowmen. There were lances worked with gold, battle-axes adorned with gold,
golden spears and darts shining with gold. Heavy pestles lay cut off and fallen,
iron bars, battle-axes and short javelins, sir. Fallen javelins lay there,
bright and adorned with gold, and saddle-cloths of many kinds, and yak-tail
fans. Men who had let fall weapons of many kinds lay there. The great chariot
fighters, lifeless, looked as if they were alive. Men lay on the ground with
their limbs crushed by maces and their heads split by pestles, trampled by
elephants, horses and chariots. The earth, king, was covered on every side with
the bodies of horses, men and elephants, as if with mountains.

"The earth was strewn with spears, swords, arrows and javelins fallen in the
battle, with swords, axes, lances, iron darts and battle-axes, with iron bars,
short javelins and hundred-killers, and with bodies cut by weapons. The earth
was covered with the lifeless, destroyer of enemies, some silent, some making
small sounds, bathed in floods of blood. The ground shone with severed arms in
arm-guards and armlets, smeared with sandal, and with the cut thighs of strong
men like elephants' trunks. It shone with the fallen heads of bull-eyed men,
bound with crest jewels and wearing earrings, Bharata. With golden armour
smeared with blood and scattered, the ground shone brightly as if with fires
whose flames had died down. There were quivers thrown down and bows fallen, and
arrows with golden feathers scattered on every side. There were many broken
chariots hung with nets of small bells, and dead horses strewn about, their
tongues lolling and bloody. There were chariot poles, flags, quivers and
banners, and the great white conches of the heroes scattered about. There were
elephants lying with their trunks limp. With all these the earth shone like a
woman adorned with ornaments of many kinds.

"With other elephants there, pierced by lances, in deep pain, blowing out sound
and spray again and again from their trunks, the field looked like mountains
being blown by the wind. It was strewn with blankets of many colours and the
saddle-cloths of elephants, and with fine goads with shafts of beryl. The bells
of lordly elephants had fallen on every side. There were torn bright
saddle-cloths and deerskin covers, bright neck ornaments and golden girths,
machines cut to pieces in many ways, and javelins and darts. There were the
dust-coloured breastplates of horses, covered with gold, and the fallen arms of
horsemen with their armlets. There were bright, sharp lances and bright spears,
and turbans cut and thrown here and there. There were bright crescent ornaments
worked with gold, crushed horse-cloths and deerskins, and the bright, costly
crest jewels of kings. There were thrown-down umbrellas, fans and yak-tails. And
there were the faces of heroes, bright as lotuses and the moon, with fine
earrings and well-trimmed beards, thrown down, great king, with ear ornaments
shining with gold. The earth was like the sky mottled with planets and stars.

"So, Bharata, these two great armies, yours and theirs, met and were crushed
there in the battle. When they were tired, broken and crushed, Bharata, a
terrible night came on, and we could not see the battle. Then the Kurus and the
Pandavas withdrew their armies as the terrible, cruel, very fierce mouth of
night came on. Having made the withdrawal, the Kurus and the Pandavas together
went to their own camps at the proper time and rested there."

**Divergences.**

* **Sanjaya on the field.** When Ghatotkacha's magic sends the Kauravas running, Sanjaya says that "I and Devavrata" shouted that it was only a rakshasa's trick (6.90.43–44). He speaks here as one who was there.
* **Arjuna's grief.** His words echo the start of the Gita: "Curse the wealth for which this destruction of kinsmen is done", and "this is no time to act like a eunuch" (6.92.5–11; compare 6.24.3). He also recalls that Yudhishthira asked for "half the kingdom or five villages" (6.92.8; ledger L-174).
* **Nine more brothers.** Bhima kills Vyudhoraska, Kundalin, Anadhrishti, Kundabheda, Vairata, Dirghalochana, Dirghabahu, Subahu and Kanakadhvaja (6.92.22–26). With the eight at 6.84 that makes seventeen sons of Dhritarashtra on the eighth day. See ledger L-194.
* **Bhishma's advice.** A king should fight a king: Duryodhana should fight Yudhishthira, Arjuna, the twins or Bhima, and leave the rakshasa to others (6.91.11–15). The rejected \*358 adds "you must not fight the evil-hearted son of Hidimba".
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*353–\*373. \*365–\*366 have Bhima close his eyes to take Drona's arrows, like a bull in the rain.

**Choices.**

* 6.90.29: Viprachitti is named as the danava Shakra pierced.
* 6.91.23 *supratīka*: Bhagadatta's elephant, Supratika, named here for the first time in the war.
* 6.91.52 *aṅkuśāṅguṣṭhanuditaḥ*: "driven by the goad and the toe".
* 6.92.26 *vairāṭa*: taken as the name of a son of Dhritarashtra, Vairata, since the text counts him among "your sons".
* 6.92.57 *śataghnī*: "hundred-killers", a weapon that kills a hundred at once.
* The long catalogue of the field at 6.92.47–75 is kept in full, grouped into sentences.
