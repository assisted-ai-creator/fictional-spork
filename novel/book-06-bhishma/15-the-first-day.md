summary: The first day of the war. Bhima's roar opens the battle; Duryodhana and his brothers surround him; duels break out along the whole line, Bhishma against Arjuna, Satyaki against Kritavarma, Abhimanyu against Brihadbala and many more, until the fighting becomes a blind melee in which fathers kill sons and friends kill friends; in the afternoon Abhimanyu cuts down Bhishma's banner; Shalya kills Virata's son Uttara, who charges him on an elephant; Uttara's brother Shankha is saved by Arjuna; Bhishma rages through the Pandava army until the sun sets and the Pandavas withdraw.

# The First Day

"When the armies had been drawn up like this," said Dhritarashtra, "mine and
theirs, who struck first, the Kurus or the Pandavas?"

"Your son Duryodhana, king," said Sanjaya, "went forward with his brothers and
his army, with Bhishma at the front. The Pandavas, led by Bhimasena, went
forward too, glad at heart, wanting to fight Bhishma. There were war cries and
shouts, the noise of rattles and cow-horns, drums, tabors and kettledrums, the
neighing of horses and the trumpeting of elephants. The armies of both sides
charged, king, and we roared back at them, and there was a tremendous uproar.

"When the Pandava and Kaurava armies met in that great clash, the huge forces
shook with the sound of conches and drums, like forests shaken by the wind. At
that evil hour the roar of the armies, crowded with kings, elephants, horses and
chariots, was like the roar of oceans whipped up by a gale. When that
tremendous, hair-raising noise rose, strong-armed Bhimasena bellowed like a
bull. Bhimasena's roar drowned the sound of conches and drums, the trumpeting of
the elephants and the lion roars of the soldiers. It drowned the neighing of
thousands of horses in the ranks. When your soldiers heard him roar like a
thundercloud, with a sound like Shakra's thunderbolt, they were terrified. All
the animals let loose dung and urine at the sound of that hero, as other beasts
do at a lion's roar. Showing a terrible form, roaring like a great cloud and
frightening your sons, he charged your army.

"As that great archer came on, his brothers, your sons, surrounded him and
covered him with floods of arrows, as clouds cover the sun. There were your son
Duryodhana, Durmukha, Duhsaha, Shala, Duhshasana, an outstanding chariot
fighter, and Durmarshana, king, Vivimshati, Chitrasena and Vikarna, a great
chariot fighter, Purumitra, Jaya, Bhoja and mighty Somadatta's son. They shook
their great bows like clouds with lightning and drew out iron arrows like snakes
that have shed their skins.

"Then Draupadi's sons and Subhadra's son, the great chariot fighter, Nakula,
Sahadeva and Dhrishtadyumna, son of Prishata, charged the sons of Dhritarashtra,
tearing them with sharp arrows, as swift thunderbolts tear the peaks of
mountains. In that first clash, with the terrible twang of bowstrings, no one on
your side or theirs turned his back. I saw the skill of Drona's pupils, bull
among the Bharatas, as they shot their arrows fast and hit their marks. The
humming of the bows never stopped, and blazing arrows flew out like stars from
the sky. All the other kings, Bharata, watched that fine, terrible meeting of
kin like spectators. Then those great chariot fighters, angry and each wronged
by the other, struggled in rivalry. The Kuru and Pandava armies, crowded with
elephants, horses and chariots, shone in the battle like a painted cloth.

"Then all the kings took up their bows and charged with their armies at your
son's command. The kings Yudhishthira had ordered charged your son's army by the
thousand, roaring. The clash between the two armies was fierce. The sun
vanished, covered by the army's dust. Some fought, some broke and came back
again, and there was no telling our own from the enemy. In that tumultuous and
terrible battle your father outshone all the armies.

"In the morning of that terrible day, lord of the people, a very fierce battle
began that cut the bodies of kings. As the Kurus and the Pandavas fought to win,
there was a roar like lions, filling heaven and earth. There were shouts, and
the sound of palms and conches, and the lion roars of heroes roaring back at one
another. There was the snap of bowstrings on arm-guards, the tramp of foot
soldiers and the great noise of horses, the fall of goads and hooks, the clash
of weapons and the bells of elephants charging one another. In that tremendous,
hair-raising noise the rumble of chariots was like thunder.

"Setting their minds on cruelty and giving up all thought of life, they all
charged the Pandavas with their banners raised. The son of Shantanu himself
charged Dhananjaya in battle, king, gripping his terrible bow, which was like
the rod of Death. Arjuna too took up the Gandiva, famous in the world, and
charged Ganga's son at the head of the battle. Those two tigers of the Kurus
each wanted to kill the other. Mighty Bhishma pierced Partha, but he could not
shake him, and in the same way the Pandava could not shake Bhishma.

"The great archer Satyaki went against Kritavarma, and they fought a
hair-raising battle, cutting each other with terrible arrows. Their bodies
covered with arrows, those two mighty men shone like two flowering kimshuka
trees in spring.

"The great archer Abhimanyu fought Brihadbala. The king of Kosala cut down
Subhadra's son's banner and brought down his charioteer. Subhadra's son, angry
that his charioteer had been brought down, pierced Brihadbala with nine arrows,
lord of the people, great king. With two more yellow arrows the destroyer of
enemies cut down his banner with one and his rear guard with the other. Then, in
anger, they cut each other with sharp arrows.

"Bhimasena fought your son Duryodhana, proud and arrogant in battle, a great
chariot fighter who had made himself his enemy. Those two tigers among men,
mighty chiefs of the Kurus, rained showers of arrows on each other on the
battlefield. Seeing those two great, skilled men fighting in so many ways,
Bharata, all beings were amazed.

"Duhshasana charged Nakula, the great chariot fighter, and pierced him with many
sharp arrows that cut into his vitals. Madri's son, almost laughing, cut down
his banner and his bow and arrows with sharp arrows, and struck him with
twenty-five small arrows. But your son, whom no one can face, cut down Nakula's
yoke and pole with arrows in the great battle and brought down his banner.

"Durmukha went against mighty Sahadeva and pierced him with a shower of arrows
as he strove in the great battle. Then heroic Sahadeva brought down Durmukha's
charioteer with a very sharp arrow. The two of them, mad for battle, met and
frightened each other with terrible arrows, each trying to pay the other back.

"King Yudhishthira himself went against the king of the Madras. The lord of the
Madras cut his bow in two, sir. Kunti's son Yudhishthira threw away the broken
bow, took another, faster and stronger, and in his anger covered the lord of the
Madras with straight arrows, crying, 'Stand! Stand!'

"Then Dhrishtadyumna charged Drona, Bharata. Drona, in great anger, cut his
strong, deadly bow in three as he strove in the battle. And he shot a terrible
arrow like a second rod of Death, which sank into his body. Then Drupada's son
took another bow and fourteen arrows, and pierced Drona in return. In great
anger they fought a fierce fight.

"Fierce Shankha went against fierce Somadatta's son in battle, great king,
crying, 'Stand! Stand!' The hero pierced his right arm, and Somadatta's son
struck Shankha on the collarbone. Their fight was terrible, lord of the people,
as swift and proud as the fight of Vritra and Vasava.

"Dhrishtaketu, the great chariot fighter of boundless spirit, charged Bahlika in
fury, lord of the people. Bahlika struck the angry Dhrishtaketu with many arrows
and gave a lion roar. The king of the Chedis, in great anger, quickly pierced
Bahlika with nine arrows, like a maddened elephant goring another. Roaring again
and again, those two angry men met like Mars and Mercury.

"Ghatotkacha, of cruel deeds, went against the rakshasa Alambusha, of cruel
deeds, like Shakra against Bala in battle. Ghatotkacha in his fury tore the
mighty rakshasa with ninety sharp arrows, Bharata. Alambusha held off Bhima's
mighty son again and again with straight arrows. Wounded with arrows, the two of
them shone in the battle like mighty Bala and Shakra in the war of the gods and
asuras.

"Mighty Shikhandi went against Drona's son, king. Ashvatthama, in anger, pierced
Shikhandi hard with a very sharp iron arrow and made him reel. Then Shikhandi,
king, struck Drona's son with a well-tempered, sharp, keen arrow. They struck
each other with arrows of many kinds.

"Virata, commander of an army, charged brave Bhagadatta quickly, king, and they
fought. Struck by Bhagadatta's shower of arrows, Virata in his fury rained
arrows on him, like a cloud raining on a mountain. Then Bhagadatta quickly
covered King Virata in the battle, as a cloud covers the rising sun.

"Kripa, son of Sharadvat, went against Brihatkshatra of the Kekayas, and covered
him with a shower of arrows, Bharata. The Kekaya in his anger filled Gautama
with a rain of arrows. Each killed the other's horses and cut the other's bow,
and then, without chariots, the two angry men met to fight with swords. Their
fight was terrible and very cruel.

"King Drupada, glad at heart, went against Jayadratha of Sindhu, who was glad
too, scorcher of enemies. The king of Sindhu struck Drupada with three arrows in
the battle, and Drupada pierced him back. Their fight was terrible and very
cruel, and it pleased those who watched, like the meeting of Venus and Mars.

"Your son Vikarna charged mighty Sutasoma with swift horses, and they fought.
Vikarna pierced Sutasoma with arrows but could not shake him, nor could Sutasoma
shake Vikarna. It was a marvel.

"Chekitana, the great chariot fighter, charged Susharma, tiger among men, in
great anger, fighting bravely for the Pandavas. Susharma held off Chekitana with
a great shower of arrows in the battle, great king, and Chekitana too, in fury,
covered Susharma with arrows in the great battle, as a great cloud covers a
mountain.

"Mighty Shakuni charged mighty Prativindhya, lord of kings, like a maddened
elephant charging another. Yudhishthira's son, in fury, tore Subala's son with
sharp arrows in the battle, as Maghavat tears a danava. And wise Shakuni tore
Prativindhya with straight arrows as he struck back.

"Shrutakarma charged Sudakshina, the brave great chariot fighter of the
Kambojas, lord of kings. Sudakshina pierced Sahadeva's son, the great chariot
fighter, but could not shake him, any more than the mountain Mainaka. Then
Shrutakarma, in anger, struck the great chariot fighter of the Kambojas with
many arrows, as if to tear him open.

"Iravan, in anger, went against the fierce Shrutayus, ready for battle. Arjuna's
son killed his horses in battle, and the great chariot fighter gave a great roar
that filled the army. Then Shrutayus, in anger, killed the horses of Phalguna's
son with the tip of his mace, and they fought on.

"Vinda and Anuvinda of Avanti engaged the great chariot fighter Kuntibhoja in
battle, with his army and his son. There we saw a wonderful feat of the Avantis,
who fought steadily with a great army. Anuvinda struck Kuntibhoja with his mace,
and Kuntibhoja quickly covered him with floods of arrows. Kuntibhoja's son
pierced Vinda with arrows, and Vinda pierced him back. It was a marvel.

"The five Kekaya brothers fought the five Gandharas, sir, each with their
armies. Your son Virabahu fought Virata's son Uttara, the fine chariot fighter,
and pierced him with sharp arrows, and Uttara pierced that steady man with sharp
arrows too. The king of the Chedis charged Uluka in battle, king, and Uluka shot
sharp feathered arrows at him. Their fight was terrible, lord of the people.
Those two unbeaten men tore each other in their anger.

"So there were thousands of single fights of chariots, elephants, horses and
foot soldiers, yours and theirs, all mixed together. For a moment the battle was
fine to look at. Then it became like madness, king, and nothing could be made
out. Elephant went at elephant in the battle, and chariot fighter at chariot
fighter, horse at horse and foot soldier at foot soldier. Then the fight became
a confusion that no one could stand, as the heroes met one another. The divine
seers, the siddhas and the charanas gathered there and watched that terrible
battle, like the war of the gods and the asuras. Then thousands of elephants and
chariots, floods of horses and floods of men met in disorder, sir. Here and
there chariots, elephants and foot soldiers, and horsemen, tiger among men, were
seen fighting again and again.

"I will tell you, Bharata, how hundreds of thousands fought there, here and
there, again and again, with no rules left. A son did not know his father, nor a
father the son of his own body. A brother did not know his brother, nor an uncle
his sister's son, nor a sister's son his uncle, nor a friend his friend. The
Pandavas fought the Kurus as if possessed.

"Some tigers among men charged the chariot ranks with their chariots. Yokes
broke against yokes, bull among the Bharatas, chariot poles against chariot
poles and shafts against shafts. Some, locked together, each trying to kill the
other, could not move, chariots jammed against chariots. Huge elephants in rut
met elephants and tore at one another fiercely with their tusks. Elephants
carrying lances and flags charged the enemy's great elephants at speed and,
struck by their tusks, screamed in great pain. Trained elephants, struck with
goads and hooks, went straight at elephants in rut. Some great elephants, locked
with elephants in rut, gave cries like herons and fled this way and that. Fine
elephants well driven, their temples and mouths streaming with rut, pierced with
spears, lances and iron arrows, roared with their vitals torn, and fell dead.
Some ran off in all directions, bellowing terribly.

"The guards at the elephants' feet, broad-chested fighters, ran here and there,
eager to kill, with spears and bows, bright axes, maces and clubs, short
javelins and lances, iron bars and bright, sharp swords. The bright swords of
heroes, running at one another, were seen wet with men's blood. There was a
great noise of swords swung and brought down by heroes' arms, falling on the
enemy's vitals. There were terrible cries here and there from crowds of men
calling to one another, crushed by maces and clubs, cut by fine swords, pierced
by elephants' tusks and trampled by elephants, like the cries of ghosts,
Bharata.

"Horsemen on horses with plumes of yak tails charged one another on horses swift
as swans. The great spears they hurled, adorned with Jambunada gold, swift,
bright and sharp, fell like snakes. Some heroes on the fastest horses leapt up
on to great chariots and took the heads of chariot fighters. A chariot fighter
would reach many horsemen within arrow's range and kill them with straight
arrows. Elephants in rut, decorated with gold, like clouds on mountains, threw
horses down and trampled them with their feet. Some elephants, struck by spears
in their temples and flanks, screamed in great pain. In that terrible press some
fine elephants tore up horses with their riders and flung them down. Elephants
tossed horses and riders on the tips of their tusks and went round crushing
chariots and banners. Some great elephants, in their fury and the heat of rut,
killed horses and riders with their trunks and feet. Some seized chariots with
their horses in their trunks and dragged them off in all directions, going
wherever they heard a noise.

"Bright, sharp arrows fell like snakes, piercing the bodies of men and horses
and their iron armour. Bright spears thrown by the arms of heroes fell here and
there, lord of the people, terrible as great meteors. They struck the enemy in
battle with bright swords drawn from sheaths of leopard and tiger skin. Charging
in fury, split open on one side, they rushed on with swords, shields and axes.
Some were torn by spears, some cut by axes, some crushed by elephants and others
trampled by horses. Men cut by chariot wheels and by sharp arrows cried out here
and there for their kin, king. Some called for their sons, some for their
fathers, some for their brothers and kin, their uncles, their sisters' sons,
even for strangers, in the battle.

"Very many, their entrails spilled, their thighs broken, their arms cut off,
their sides torn open, were seen crying out, thirsty and longing to live. Some
of little strength, overcome by thirst, lying fallen on the ground in battle,
begged only for water, lord of the people. Soaked in floods of blood and in
torment, they cursed themselves bitterly, Bharata, and your sons who had brought
them together. Other brave kshatriyas, who had made themselves enemies of one
another, would not let go of their weapons and did not cry out, sir. Glad, they
threatened one another here and there. They bit their lips with their teeth in
anger and glared at one another with frowning faces. Others, strong and
steadfast, tormented by wounds and struck by arrows, fell silent. Other heroes
who had lost their chariots, begging for another's chariot in the battle, fell
and were trampled by great elephants, and they looked like flowering kimshuka
trees, great king.

"In the ranks there were many terrible cries as that most frightful destruction
of heroes went on. Father killed son and son killed father in the battle. The
sister's son killed the uncle, and the uncle the sister's son. Friend killed
friend, king, and kinsman killed kinsman. So the Kurus fought the Pandavas
there. In that terror, in that great battle with no rules left, the army of the
Parthas shook when it met Bhishma. With his silver banner of the palm and its
five stars raised high on his great chariot, strong-armed Bhishma shone then,
king, like the moon above Meru.

"When most of the morning of that terrible day had gone, as the fearful
destruction of the best of heroes went on, Durmukha, Kritavarma, Kripa, Shalya
and Vivimshati came to guard Bhishma at your son's command. Guarded by these
five outstanding chariot fighters, bull among the Bharatas, the great chariot
fighter plunged into the Pandava ranks. Bhishma's palm banner was seen moving
about among the Chedis, the Kashis, the Karushas and the Panchalas, Bharata.
Bhishma cut off heads, and arms with their weapons, with swift, straight arrows.
As Bhishma danced along the chariot tracks, bull among the Bharatas, some
elephants struck in their vitals screamed in pain.

"Abhimanyu, in great anger, mounted his chariot yoked to fine tawny horses and
went at Bhishma's chariot. Under his banner of the karnikara tree, bright with
Jambunada gold, he rained arrows on Bhishma and on those fine chariot fighters.
The hero struck the palm-bannered one's banner with a sharp arrow and fought
Bhishma and his followers. He pierced Kritavarma with one arrow and Shalya with
five iron ones, and struck the great grandfather with nine sharp-pointed arrows.
With one arrow, drawn full and well aimed, he pierced his banner, adorned with
Jambunada gold. With a straight arrow that pierced all armour he struck the head
of Durmukha's charioteer from his body. With a sharp-pointed arrow he cut
Kripa's bow, adorned with gold. And the great chariot fighter, in great anger,
as if dancing, struck them with sharp-pointed arrows. Even the gods were pleased
when they saw his speed. Because he hit every mark, all the chariot fighters led
by Bhishma thought Krishna's nephew was as mighty as Dhananjaya himself. His
bow, moving so fast it looked like a whirling torch, flashed in every direction
and twanged like the Gandiva.

"Bhishma, killer of enemy heroes, came at him and quickly pierced Arjuna's son
with nine swift arrows in the battle. With three arrows the man of strict vows
cut down that mighty man's banner, and struck his charioteer with three.
Kritavarma, Kripa and Shalya pierced Krishna's nephew too, sir, but could not
shake him, any more than the mountain Mainaka. Surrounded by those great chariot
fighters of Dhritarashtra's son, the brave son of Krishna's sister rained
showers of arrows on the five chariot fighters. Then, holding off their great
weapons with his showers of arrows, mighty Abhimanyu gave a great roar and shot
his arrows at Bhishma. There, king, the great strength of his arms was seen, as
he strove in battle and tormented Bhishma with arrows. As he attacked, Bhishma
shot arrows at him too, and he cut down in the battle the arrows that flew from
Bhishma's bow. Then that hero, whose arrows never missed, cut down Bhishma's
banner with nine arrows, and the people cried out. That tall silver palm,
adorned with gold, cut down by the arrows of Subhadra's son, fell to the ground,
Bharata. When he saw the banner fallen to the arrows of Subhadra's son, bull
among the Bharatas, Bhima roared with joy to cheer him on.

"Then, at that moment, fierce and mighty Bhishma brought out many great divine
weapons. The great-grandfather, whose spirit had no measure, covered Subhadra's
son with a hundred thousand straight arrows. Then ten great archers, great
chariot fighters of the Pandavas, quickly charged in their chariots to protect
Subhadra's son: Virata with his son, Dhrishtadyumna, son of Prishata, Bhima, the
Kekayas and Satyaki, lord of the people. As they came on at speed, Bhishma, son
of Shantanu, struck the Panchala with three arrows in the battle, and Satyaki
with sharp arrows. With one sharp razor-headed arrow, drawn full, he cut down
Bhimasena's banner. Bhimasena's golden lion banner, best of men, fell from his
chariot, crushed by Bhishma. Bhimasena pierced Bhishma, son of Shantanu, with
three arrows in the battle, Kripa with one and Kritavarma with eight.

"Then Virata's son Uttara, on an elephant with its trunk raised, charged the
king of the Madras. As that lord of elephants came on at speed, Shalya in his
chariot stopped its matchless rush. The great elephant, angry, set its foot on
the yoke of the huge, well-trained chariot and killed the four horses. Standing
in the chariot with its horses dead, the lord of the Madras hurled an iron spear
like a snake, the death of Uttara. It pierced his armour, and he went into deep
darkness. He fell from the elephant's neck, and his hook and lance dropped from
his hands. Shalya took his sword, leapt down from his fine chariot and, striding
up, cut off the great trunk of that lord of elephants. With its vitals torn by
floods of arrows and its trunk cut off, the elephant gave a terrible scream,
fell, and died. When he had done this, the king of the Madras, a great chariot
fighter, quickly climbed into Kritavarma's shining chariot.

"When Virata's son Shankha saw his fine brother Uttara killed, and Shalya
standing there with Kritavarma, he blazed up in anger like fire fed with butter.
He stretched his great bow, adorned with gold, and charged Shalya, lord of the
Madras, meaning to kill him. Surrounded by a great line of chariots on every
side, and pouring out a shower of arrows, he went at Shalya's chariot. When they
saw him coming on with the valour of a maddened elephant, seven chariot fighters
of yours surrounded him on every side, trying to save the king of the Madras,
who was already between the jaws of death.

"Then strong-armed Bhishma, roaring like a cloud, took up a bow as tall as a
palm tree and charged Shankha in the battle. When they saw that mighty great
archer bearing down, the Pandava army was terrified, like a ship struck by a
gale. Then Arjuna quickly put himself in front of Shankha. 'He must be saved
from Bhishma today,' he thought, and the battle went on. There was a great cry
from the warriors fighting there. 'Splendour has met splendour,' they said, and
they were amazed.

"Then Shalya, mace in hand, got down from his great chariot and killed Shankha's
four horses, bull among the Bharatas. Shankha took his sword and ran quickly
from his chariot with its dead horses, reached Bibhatsu's chariot, and was safe.

"Then arrows flew fast from Bhishma's chariot and covered the sky and the earth
on every side. Bhishma, best of fighters, brought down the Panchalas, the
Matsyas, the Kekayas and the Prabhadrakas with his arrows. He left the Pandava
Savyasachin in the battle and charged Drupada of Panchala, his dear kinsman by
marriage, surrounded by his army, scattering many arrows. Drupada's forces were
seen burned by his arrows, like forests burned by fire at the end of winter.
Bhishma stood in the battle like a smokeless fire. The Pandava's warriors could
not look at Bhishma, burning with splendour like the midday sun. Tormented by
fear, the Pandavas looked all round, but found no protector, like cows tormented
by cold.

"As the army was killed and scattered, crushed and without heart, there was a
great cry in the Pandava army, Bharata. Then Bhishma, son of Shantanu, his bow
always drawn to a circle, shot blazing-tipped arrows like venomous snakes.
Filling all the quarters with his arrows, the man of strict vows killed the
Pandava chariot fighters, naming them one by one, Bharata. When the armies were
broken and crushed on every side, and the sun had set, nothing could be seen.
When the Parthas saw Bhishma rising up in the great battle, bull among the
Bharatas, they withdrew their armies."

**Divergences.**

* **No Shveta.** In many versions of the first day, Shveta, a son of Virata and a Pandava commander, fights a long duel with Bhishma and is killed by him with a brahma weapon. In the CE the whole Shveta episode is Appendix I No. 4, after 6.45.43, and is not narrated. In the CE text, after Uttara's death it is his brother *Shankha* who blazes up and attacks Shalya (6.45.43–52), and he survives. See ledger L-193.
* **Uttara's death.** Virata's son Uttara, who drove Arjuna's chariot in Book 4, charges Shalya on an elephant and is killed by Shalya's spear (6.45.35–41).
* **Kin against kin.** "A son did not know his father … The Pandavas fought the Kurus as if possessed" (6.44.2–3); "Father killed son and son killed father" (6.44.45).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*134–\*152 and App. I No. 4.
