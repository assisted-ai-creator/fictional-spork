summary: The eighth day. Bhishma draws up an array like the sea, and Dhrishtadyumna answers with the horned array. Bhishma cuts down the Panchalas and Somakas, and only Bhima stands against him. Bhima kills Bhishma's charioteer and then eight of Duryodhana's brothers. Duryodhana complains to Bhishma, who tells him Bhima will kill every son of Dhritarashtra he sees. Sanjaya reminds Dhritarashtra that Vidura warned him. In the afternoon the six sons of Subala charge Iravan, Arjuna's son by a daughter of the naga king, and five of them die under his sword. Duryodhana then sends the rakshasa Alambusa against him. The two fight with magic, and Iravan calls up his mother's kin, the nagas, but the rakshasa takes the form of Garuda and eats them. Then he cuts off Iravan's head. Arjuna, fighting elsewhere, does not yet know that his son is dead.

# Iravan

"When the lords of men had slept well and the night was over," said Sanjaya,
"the Kurus and the Pandavas went out to battle again. There was a great noise
from both armies as they went out to battle, as great as the sea. Then King
Duryodhana, Chitrasena, Vivimshati, Bhishma, best of chariot fighters, and the
brahmin Bharadvaja came together, set on it, in their armour. They drew up the
great army of the Kauravas in an array against the Pandavas, king.

"Your father Bhishma, lord of the people, made a great array, terrible, like the
sea, its waves and ripples the mounts. Bhishma, son of Shantanu, went at the
head of all the armies with the Malavas, the southerners and the men of Avanti.
Next to him was mighty Bharadvaja's son, with the Pulindas, the Paradas and the
Kshudrakas and Malavas. After Drona came mighty Bhagadatta, set on it, with the
Magadhas, the Kalingas and the Pishachas, lord of the people. After the king of
Pragjyotisha came King Brihadbala of Kosala, with the Mekalas, the Traipuras and
the Chicchilas. After Brihadbala came the brave Trigarta, lord of Prasthala,
with many Kambojas and Yavanas by the thousand. After the Trigarta, Bharata,
went Drona's son, fierce and brave, making the earth ring with his lion roar.
After Drona's son King Duryodhana went, with the whole army, surrounded by his
brothers. After Duryodhana went Kripa, Sharadvat's son. So this great array went
out, like the sea. Flags and white umbrellas shone there, lord, and bright
armlets and costly bows.

"Seeing that great array of yours, Yudhishthira, the great chariot fighter,
quickly said to Prishata's son, the commander of the army, 'Look, great archer,
at the array they have made, like the sea. Make a counter array too, son of
Prishata, without delay.' Then brave Prishata's son made a most terrible array,
great king, the horned array, which destroys the enemy's arrays. At the horns
were Bhimasena and Satyaki, the great chariot fighter, with many thousands of
chariots, horses and foot soldiers. At its navel was the best of men, with his
white horses and monkey banner. In the middle were King Yudhishthira and the two
Pandava sons of Madri. Then the other kings, great archers skilled in the
science of arrays, filled out the array with their armies. Behind were Abhimanyu
and Virata, the great chariot fighter, the glad sons of Draupadi and the
rakshasa Ghatotkacha. So the Pandavas drew up this great array, Bharata, and the
heroes stood in the battle, eager to fight and wanting victory.

"The tumultuous noise of drums, mixed with the sound of conches, with shouts,
arm-slapping and yells, rose very terrible on every side. Then the heroes met
each other in the battle and stared at each other in anger, king, with eyes that
did not blink. Lord of men, the warriors had challenged each other in their
minds before they came together to fight. Then a battle began, terrible to see
and full of fear, yours and theirs, killing each other.

"Sharp iron arrows flew in the battle, Bharata, like terrifying snakes with open
mouths, in swarms. Bright spears, washed in oil and well sharpened, flew out,
king, like flashing lightning from the clouds. Bright maces bound with clean
bands, adorned with gold, were seen falling there like mountain peaks, and
swords shone like the clear sky. Shields of bull's hide with a hundred moons
shone as they fell on every side in the battle, Bharata. As the two armies
fought each other in the battle, lord of men, they shone like the armies of the
daityas and the gods rising up. They charged each other on every side in the
battle.

"Chariots were driven swiftly by their fighters in the great battle. The bulls
among kings locked yoke with yoke and fought. From the clash of the fighting
elephants' tusks fire sprang up, with smoke, on every side, best of the
Bharatas. Some elephant fighters, struck with lances, were seen falling on every
side, like trees from mountain peaks. Foot soldiers too were seen killing one
another, brave men of wonderful looks fighting with claws and lances. Meeting
each other, the soldiers of the Kurus and the Pandavas sent each other to Yama's
house in the battle with terrible weapons of many kinds.

"Then Bhishma, son of Shantanu, came against the Pandavas in the battle, making
it ring with the rumble of his chariot and bewildering them with the sound of
his bow. The Pandavas' chariots too, set on it, led by Dhrishtadyumna, charged,
roaring a terrible roar. Then a battle began between yours and theirs, Bharata,
the men, horses, chariots and elephants tangled together.

"The Pandavas could not look at Bhishma, angry in the battle, scorching on every
side, like the burning sun. Then all the armies, at the command of the son of
Dharma, charged Ganga's son, who was crushing them with sharp arrows. Bhishma,
who gloried in battle, brought down the Somakas and the Srinjayas and the great
Panchala archers with his arrows. Being killed by Bhishma, the Panchalas with
the Somakas threw off the fear Death brings and went straight at Bhishma. In the
battle the heroic Bhishma, son of Shantanu, suddenly cut off the arms and heads
of those chariot fighters, king. Your father Devavrata made chariot fighters
chariotless. The heads of horsemen fell from their horses. We saw elephants
without their men, lying like mountains, great king, bewildered by Bhishma's
weapon. There was no man there among the Pandavas, lord of the people, except
the best of chariot fighters, mighty Bhimasena. He came up to Bhishma and struck
him in the battle.

"Then at that meeting of Bhishma and Bhima a terrible groan went up from all the
armies, terrible and fearful to hear. And the Pandavas, glad, gave a lion roar.
Then King Duryodhana, surrounded by his brothers, guarded Bhishma in the battle
as the killing went on. Bhima, best of chariot fighters, killed Bhishma's
charioteer. With its horses bolting, the chariot ran this way and that. Then the
killer of enemies quickly cut off Sunabha's head with an arrow, a very sharp
razor arrow, and he fell dead to the ground.

"Your son, that great chariot fighter, had been killed, great king. Seven of his
brothers, brave men, could not bear it in the battle: Adityaketu, Bahvashi,
Kundadhara, Mahodara, Aparajita, Panditaka and Vishalaksha, so hard to beat. In
bright gear, with bright armour and banners, those crushers of enemies charged
the Pandava in the battle, wanting to fight. Mahodara pierced Bhima in the
battle with nine feathered arrows like thunderbolts, as the killer of Vritra
pierced Namuchi. Adityaketu pierced him with seventy, Bahvashi with five,
Kundadhara with ninety and Vishalaksha with seven. Aparajita, great king, the
great chariot fighter who meant to conquer, struck mighty Bhimasena with many
arrows. Panditaka hurt him with three arrows in the battle. Bhima could not bear
being hit by his enemies in the battle.

"The destroyer of enemies pressed his bow with his left hand and cut off a head
in the battle with a straight arrow. The head of your son Aparajita, with its
fine nose, beaten by Bhima, fell to the ground in the battle. Then with another
broad arrow he sent Kundadhara, the great chariot fighter, to the world of the
dead while all the world watched. Then he whose spirit had no measure fitted
another arrow and sent it at Panditaka in the battle, Bharata. The arrow killed
Panditaka and went into the ground, as a snake driven by Time kills a man
quickly and goes into the earth. Remembering the old wrongs, he cut off
Vishalaksha's head with three arrows, undaunted, and brought it down to the
ground. He pierced the great archer Mahodara in the middle of the chest with an
iron arrow in the battle, king, and he fell dead to the ground. He cut down
Adityaketu's banner with an arrow in the battle, and the killer of enemies cut
off his head with a very sharp broad arrow. Then Bhima in anger sent Bahvashi to
Yama's house with a straight arrow.

"Then the others, your sons, lord of the people, ran, thinking that what he had
said in the hall was true. Then King Duryodhana, worn down by the disaster to
his brothers, said to your warriors, 'Kill this Bhima in battle!' So those great
archers, your sons, lord of the people, saw their brothers killed and remembered
those words. What the wise steward said, the good and healthy words of that man
of divine sight, have come to pass. Overcome by greed and delusion, from love of
your son, lord of men, you did not understand the great true words that were
spoken long ago. Surely the mighty, strong-armed Pandava was born to kill your
sons, just as he is killing the Kauravas.

"Then King Duryodhana went to Bhishma, sir. Overcome by great grief and worn
down, he lamented. 'My brave brothers have been killed by Bhimasena in battle.
The other soldiers too are being killed, striving as they are. And you always
stand aside and neglect us. I have taken a bad road. See my fate!'

"When he heard these cruel words, your father Devavrata said this to Duryodhana,
his eyes full of tears. 'I told you this before, and so did Drona, and Vidura,
and famous Gandhari. You did not understand it, my son. And I made an agreement
with you before, destroyer of enemies, that neither I nor the teacher would ever
be released from battle. Whichever of the sons of Dhritarashtra Bhima sees in
battle, he will kill him in the fight. I tell you the truth. So, king, be
steady, set your mind firmly on battle and fight the Parthas, taking heaven as
your goal. The Pandavas cannot be beaten even by the gods and asuras with Indra.
So set your mind firmly on battle and fight, Bharata.'"

"Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "when they saw my many sons killed by one man,
what did Bhishma, Drona and Kripa do in the battle? Day after day my sons go to
their destruction, Sanjaya. I think they have been struck hard by fate in every
way, charioteer, since my sons are all beaten and do not win. My sons are killed
in battle though they stand in the middle of Bhishma, Drona and great Kripa, of
heroic Somadatta's son, of Bhagadatta, of Ashvatthama, my son, and of other
great heroes. What else is it but fate? Foolish Duryodhana did not understand
what was said before, my son, when I held him back, and Bhishma and Vidura, and
Gandhari, always wanting his good. The fool did not understand it before, in his
delusion, and this is its fruit: day after day Bhimasena, angry in battle, takes
my senseless sons to Yama's house."

"What the steward said so well has come to you now, lord," said Sanjaya. "You
did not understand it when that good counsel was spoken. 'Keep your sons from
the dice, and do not wrong the Pandavas.' Your friends, wanting your good, told
you that and the like. You would not listen to the words, as a dying man will
not take healthy medicine. Now those well-spoken words have come home to you.
The Kauravas are going to their destruction because they did not take the
healthy advice of Vidura, Drona, Bhishma and others who wished them well. All
that is past now, lord of the people. So hear from me truly how the battle went.

"At midday a very cruel battle began, king, destroying the world. Hear it as I
tell it. Then all the armies, at the command of the son of Dharma, charged in
fury at Bhishma himself, wanting to kill him. Dhrishtadyumna, Shikhandi and
Satyaki, the great chariot fighter, with their divisions yoked, great king, went
against Bhishma himself. Arjuna, the sons of Draupadi and Chekitana went in the
battle against all the kings Duryodhana had ordered there. Heroic Abhimanyu, the
great chariot fighter Hidimba's son, and Bhimasena in his anger charged the
Kauravas. So the Kauravas were killed in the battle by the Pandavas in three
divisions, and in the same way the enemy were killed in the battle by the
Kauravas, king.

"Drona, best of chariot fighters, charged the Somakas with the Srinjayas in
anger, to send them to Yama's house. There was a great cry from the great
Srinjayas, king, as they were killed in the battle by Bharadvaja's son the
bowman. Many kshatriyas killed there by Drona in the battle were seen writhing
like men racked by sickness. There was an endless sound in the battle of men
groaning, crying out and moaning, like men wasted by hunger.

"In the same way mighty Bhimasena made a terrible slaughter of the Kauravas in
his anger, like a second Time. As the armies killed each other in the great
battle a terrible river began to flow, carrying floods of blood. That great
battle of the Kurus and the Pandavas was terrible to see, great king, filling
Yama's kingdom. Then Bhima, angry in the battle and above all fierce, reached
the elephant division and sent it to Death. Struck by Bhima's iron arrows there,
Bharata, the elephants fell, sank down, trumpeted and ran about in all
directions. Great elephants with their trunks cut off and their feet cut off,
sir, cried out in fear like herons and lay on the ground.

"Nakula and Sahadeva charged the horse division. The horses, with their golden
headbands and trappings of gold, were seen being killed by the hundred and the
thousand. The earth was strewn with falling horses, king. With horses lolling
their tongues, gasping, moaning and dead, of many shapes, the earth shone, best
of men. And with the horses killed by Arjuna in the battle, Bharata, the ground
looked terrible here and there, lord of the people. The earth was covered with
broken chariots, cut banners and very bright umbrellas, with necklaces, gold
ornaments, armlets and heads with earrings. It was covered with thrown-off
turbans and flags on every side, and with fine chariot poles, traces and reins,
king, and it shone as if with flowers in spring. So this destruction came about
for the Pandavas too, Bharata, when Bhishma, son of Shantanu, was angry, and
Drona, best of chariot fighters, Ashvatthama, Kripa and Kritavarma. And when the
others were angry, destruction came to your men too.

"As that cruel destruction of the best heroes went on, king, splendid Shakuni,
son of Subala, charged the Pandavas. In the same way, king, the Satvata,
Hridika's son, killer of enemy heroes, charged the army of the Pandavas in the
battle. Then the mighty son of the Pandava, scorcher of enemies, glad, attacked
that army with the finest horses. They were of Kamboja stock, river-bred, from
Aratta, from Mahi and from Sindhu, white horses from Vanayu and horses from the
hills, and other swift horses from Tittira. They were adorned with gold,
armoured, well trained, as swift as the wind.

"Arjuna's heir was named Iravan, a man of courage. He was born to wise Partha by
the daughter of the naga king. Airavata had given her away. She was childless,
and wretched and low in spirit after her husband had been killed by Suparna.
Partha took her as his wife, since she was ruled by desire. So Arjuna's son was
born in another's field. He grew up in the world of the nagas, guarded by his
mother. His evil-hearted uncle cast him out, from hatred of Partha. Handsome,
full of courage, virtuous, whose valour was true, he went quickly to Indra's
world when he heard that Arjuna had gone there. He came up to his great father,
whose valour was true, and greeted him calmly and humbly with joined hands. 'I
am Iravan, bless you, and I am your son, lord.' He told him everything about his
meeting with his mother, and the Pandava remembered it all as it had happened.
Partha embraced his son, who was like himself in his virtues, and was glad in
the home of the king of the gods. Then in the world of the gods, king, Arjuna
gave the strong-armed man a charge of his own, with affection: 'When the time
for battle comes, you must give us help, lord.' He said, 'Yes!' and when the
time for battle came, he came, king, surrounded by many horses of every colour
and speed he wished.

"Those horses with golden headbands, of many colours, swift as the mind, leapt
up suddenly, king, like swans on the great ocean. Meeting your troops of very
swift horses, they struck breast against breast and nose against nose, and fell
suddenly to the ground, king, struck by the great force. As the troops of horses
fell against each other, a cruel noise was heard, like the fall of Suparna. In
the same way, great king, the horsemen met each other in the battle and made a
terrible slaughter of each other. As that tumultuous, confused fight went on,
the troops of horses on both sides were destroyed all round. The brave men,
their arrows spent, their horses killed, sick with weariness, came to their end,
cutting each other down.

"Then, when the horse division was destroyed and little was left, Bharata, the
brave sons of Subala came out at the head of the battle. They rode the best
horses, as swift as the wind to the touch and as fast as the wind in their
speed, well trained and in their prime: Gaja, Gavaksha, Vrishaka, Charmavan,
Arjava and Shuka. These six, full of strength, went out from the great army,
though Shakuni and their own mighty warriors tried to hold them back. In their
armour, skilled in battle, fierce in form and mighty, the Gandharas, mad in
battle, broke into that army, strong-armed one, so very hard to beat. With a
great force they went in, glad, wanting heaven and wanting victory.

"Courageous Iravan saw them go in. In the battle he said to his warriors, with
their bright ornaments and weapons, 'Make a plan so that all these warriors of
the son of Dhritarashtra, with their followers and mounts, are killed in the
battle.' Saying 'Yes!', all Iravan's warriors killed that enemy army, so hard
for others to beat in battle. Seeing their army brought down by his army in the
battle, the sons of Subala could not bear it. They charged Iravan in the battle
and surrounded him on every side. Striking with sharp lances and urging each
other on, the heroes rode round him, making a great turmoil.

"Then Iravan, pierced by the great men's sharp lances, smeared with flowing
blood, was like an elephant pricked with goads. Struck hard in the chest, in the
back and in the sides, one against many, he did not flinch, king, for he was
steadfast. Then Iravan, conqueror of enemy cities, in anger, pierced them all
with sharp arrows in the battle and made them faint. The tamer of enemies pulled
all the lances out of his own body and struck the sons of Subala with them in
the battle. He drew a sharp sword, took a shield, and came quickly on foot to
kill the sons of Subala in the battle. Then all the sons of Subala came to again
and, filled with anger once more, charged Iravan. Iravan too, proud of his
strength, went against all the sons of Subala with his sword, showing the speed
of his hands. As he moved about with that speed, the sons of Subala, moving
swiftly, could find no opening in him. Seeing him on the ground in the battle,
they all surrounded him closely again and set about taking him alive. But as
they came close, the tamer of enemies cut their limbs with his sword, with
forehand and backhand strokes. Their weapons and their adorned arms fell, and
they fell to the ground with their limbs cut, lifeless. Vrishaka, great king,
much wounded, escaped from that most cruel destruction of heroes.

"Seeing them all fallen, Duryodhana was afraid. In his anger he spoke to the
rakshasa of terrible looks, Rishyashringa's son, the great archer, master of
magic, tamer of enemies, Bhimasena's enemy since the killing of Baka. 'Look,
hero, how this mighty son of Phalguna, a master of magic, has done me a terrible
wrong by destroying my army. You go where you please, my son, and you are
skilled in the weapon of magic, and you have made an enemy of Partha. So kill
him in battle.'

"Saying 'Yes!', the rakshasa of terrible looks went with a lion roar to where
Arjuna's young son was. He was surrounded by his own divisions, heroes who
struck hard, well mounted, skilled in battle, fighting with bright lances, and
he meant to kill mighty Iravan in the battle. Iravan too, the valiant killer of
enemies, in anger, quickly held back the rakshasa who meant to kill him. Seeing
him coming, the mighty rakshasa quickly set about using magic. He made as many
horses by magic, well ridden by terrible rakshasas holding spears and
battle-axes. Those two thousand, meeting in fury, striking hard, soon sent each
other to the world of the dead.

"When that army had been killed, those two, both mad in battle, stood facing
each other in the battle like Vritra and Vasava. Seeing the rakshasa, mad in
battle, charging him, mighty Iravan, full of anger, ran at him. As the
evil-minded one came close in the battle, Iravan cut his blazing bow with his
sword, and his quiver with its five arrows. Seeing his bow cut, the rakshasa
went swiftly up into the sky, bewildering angry Iravan with magic. Then Iravan
too flew up into the sky, bewildered the rakshasa with his own magic and cut his
limbs with arrows. For he knew every vital point, could take any form he wished,
and was hard to come at. But the best of rakshasas, cut again and again with
arrows, grew whole again, great king, and got his youth back. For magic is
natural to them, and they take whatever age and form they wish. So the
rakshasa's body grew back each time it was cut. Iravan in his anger cut that
mighty rakshasa again and again with a sharp axe. Being cut by that mighty hero
like a tree, the rakshasa gave a terrible roar, and the noise was tumultuous.
Wounded by the axe, the rakshasa streamed with blood. Then the mighty one grew
angry and put out his strength in the battle.

"Seeing his enemy grow strong in the battle, Rishyashringa's son took on a huge
and terrible form and set about seizing him, in the middle of the front of the
battle, while everyone there watched. Seeing such magic from the great rakshasa,
Iravan too, in anger, set about making magic. As he was overcome with anger, he
who never turned back in battle, his mother's kin came to him. He was surrounded
on every side in the battle by many nagas, king, and he took on a very great
form, like Ananta with his coils. Then he covered the rakshasa with nagas of
many kinds. Covered by the nagas, the bull among rakshasas thought for a moment,
took the form of Suparna and ate the snakes. When his mother's kin had been
eaten by that magic, the rakshasa killed the bewildered Iravan with his sword.
The rakshasa brought Iravan's head down to the ground, with its earrings and its
crown, bright as a lotus or the moon.

"When that hero, Arjuna's son, had been killed by the rakshasa, the sons of
Dhritarashtra and the kings were freed of their grief. In that great and
terrible battle there was again a great, terrible confusion of the armies.
Horses, elephants and foot soldiers, mixed together, were killed by elephants.
Chariots and elephants were killed there by foot soldiers. And masses of foot
soldiers and chariots, and many horses, were killed in the battle by chariot
fighters, king, yours and theirs, in the confusion. Arjuna, not knowing that the
son of his body had been killed, killed the brave kings who were guarding
Bhishma in the battle. In the same way your men, king, and the mighty Srinjayas
killed each other in the battle, offering up their lives. With their hair loose,
without armour, without chariots, their bows cut, they fought each other with
their arms, locked together.

"In the same way Bhishma, mighty, killed great chariot fighters with arrows that
went through their vital parts, making the Pandava army shake. By him many men
were killed in Yudhishthira's army, elephant riders, horsemen, chariot fighters
and horses. There, Bharata, seeing Bhishma's valour in the battle, we saw a
valour most marvellous, like Shakra's. In the same way Bhimasena's fighting, and
Prishata's son's, and the bowman Satvata's, were terrible then, Bharata. Seeing
Drona's courage, fear came on the Pandavas. Hard pressed by Drona in the battle,
great king, they said, 'He alone can kill us with our armies in battle. How much
more, surrounded by throngs of warriors, the heroes of the earth!'

"As that cruel battle went on, bull of the Bharatas, the heroes of both armies
could not bear one another. The mighty bowmen, yours and the Pandavas', fought
in fury as if they were possessed, sir, become like rakshasas. In that battle,
like a battle of the daityas, lord of men, we saw no fighter who guarded his
life."

**Divergences.**

* **Iravan's death.** In the CE Iravan dies in battle. The rakshasa Alambusa, sent by Duryodhana, bewilders him with magic and cuts off his head with a sword (6.86.44–70). He is not sacrificed to Kali. See ledger L-37.
* **Iravan's mother.** The text calls her the daughter of the naga king, given to Arjuna by Airavata after Suparna had killed her husband (6.86.6–8). It does not name her. The rejected \*342 calls Iravan "Ulupi's son". See ledger L-103.
* **Eight more brothers.** Bhima kills Sunabha, then Aparajita, Kundadhara, Panditaka, Vishalaksha, Mahodara, Adityaketu and Bahvashi (6.84.12–28), eight in all, as on the fourth day. See ledger L-194.
* **Vidura's words.** The text moves here from Sanjaya's report into Sanjaya's own reproach of Dhritarashtra (6.84.31–34). The shift is kept.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*335–\*346. \*344 has Iravan offer himself to Arjuna as "your servant and your son".

**Choices.**

* 6.83.17 *śṛṅgāṭaka*: "the horned array".
* 6.84.14 *sudurjayaḥ*: taken as describing Vishalaksha, "so hard to beat", since the text counts seven brothers.
* 6.84.18 *aparājito … parājiṣṇuḥ*: "Aparajita … who meant to conquer", a play on his name (Aparajita means "unconquered").
* 6.86.8 *parakṣetre*: "born in another's field", the old phrase for a son born to another man's wife.
* 6.86.24: the six sons of Subala are named as the text names them.
* 6.86.41 *upahastāvahastābhyām*: "with forehand and backhand strokes".
* 6.86.45 *pūrvaṃ bakavadhena*: Alambusa has been Bhima's enemy "since the killing of Baka" (Book 1).
* 6.86.66 *anvayo mātṛkaḥ*: "his mother's kin", the nagas.
* 6.86.68 *sauparṇaṃ rūpam*: "the form of Suparna", that is, of Garuda, the eater of snakes.
