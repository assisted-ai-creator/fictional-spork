# Krishna Takes Up the Discus

Dhritarashtra said, "When Bhishma had made his promise in that cruel battle,
angered by my son, who was in great distress, what did Bhishma do there against
the Pandavas, Sanjaya? Or what did the Panchalas do against the grandfather?
Tell me that, Sanjaya."

"Bharata," said Sanjaya, "most of the morning of that day had passed. The
great-souled Pandavas had won and were glad. Then your father Devavrata, who
knows the finer points of all dharma, rode with swift horses against the Pandava
army. A great force guarded him, and your sons on every side. Then, through your
bad policy, Bharata, a battle broke out between us and the Pandavas, tumultuous
and making the hair stand on end. There was a great noise of twanging bows and
of palms striking, like mountains splitting. 'Stand!' 'Here I am!' 'Know this
one!' 'Turn back!' 'Hold firm!' 'I am standing here, strike!' These cries were
heard on every side. Weapons falling on golden armour, on diadems and banners,
made a sound like rocks falling on hills. Heads and adorned arms fell and
writhed on the ground in hundreds and thousands. Some of the best of men stood
with their heads cut off, their bows still raised and their weapons still held.

"A river of blood began to flow, very swift, terrible with the bodies of
elephants for rocks, with flesh and blood for mud. It rose from the bodies of
fine horses, men and elephants. It ran towards the ocean of the other world and
delighted the vultures and jackals. Such a battle was never seen or heard of,
king, as this between your sons and the Pandavas, Bharata. There was no road for
the chariots there, with the warriors brought down in battle and the dark fallen
elephants covering it like mountain peaks. Strewn with bright armour, banners
and parasols, sir, the battlefield shone like the sky in autumn. Some, torn by
arrows and dragging their entrails in pain, still ran fearless in their armour
at the enemy. Others, fallen in battle, cried out, 'Father! Brother! Friend!
Kinsman! Companion! Uncle! Do not leave me!' Others shouted, 'Come here! Come
back! Do not go! Why are you afraid? Where will you go? I am standing in the
battle. Do not be afraid!'

"There Bhishma son of Shantanu, his bow always drawn into a circle, let fly
flaming-tipped arrows like venomous snakes. Keeping his vows, he filled all the
quarters with arrows as with one road, and killed the Pandava chariot fighters,
naming each one first, Bharata. Dancing on the chariot floor and showing the
lightness of his hands, he was seen here and there like a whirling firebrand,
king. Because of his lightness, the Pandavas and the Srinjayas saw that one hero
in battle as many hundreds of thousands. People there thought Bhishma had made
himself many by magic. Having seen him in the east, they saw him in the west.
Having seen him in the north, they saw him again in the south, lord. So the
hero, Ganga's son, appeared in that battle. None of the Pandavas could even look
at him. They saw only the many arrows loosed from Bhishma's bow. The heroes
there cried out in many ways and much at your father, as he did his work in
battle, destroying the army, moving in a more than human form. Driven by fate,
kings fell in thousands on that angry fire Bhishma to their destruction, like
moths.

"Not one of Bhishma's arrows was wasted in battle among the bodies of men,
elephants and horses, so many were they and so quick his aim. With one arrow
well loosed he split an elephant's armour as a thunderbolt splits a great
mountain. Your father killed two or three elephant riders packed together, even
in armour, with one very sharp iron arrow. Whoever came against Bhishma, the
tiger among men, in battle, I saw him for a moment, and then I saw him brought
down on the ground. So the great army of the King of Dharma, killed by Bhishma
of matchless strength, broke into a thousand pieces. Scorched by the rain of
arrows, the great army scattered, while Vasudeva and the great-souled Partha
watched. Though the heroes tried, they could not stop the great chariot fighters
running, crushed by Bhishma's arrows. Killed by one with courage like great
Indra's, the great army broke, great king, so that not even two ran together.
With men, elephants and horses pierced, with banners and chariot poles fallen,
the army of Pandu's sons was crying out and senseless. There father killed son
and son father, and friend, forced by the power of fate, killed dear friend.
Other soldiers of Pandu's son were seen running with their armour thrown off and
their hair loose, Bharata. The army of Pandu's son looked like a herd of cattle
in a panic, its leaders of chariot troops bewildered, crying out in distress.

---

"Seeing that army breaking, the joy of Devaki held back the fine chariot and
said to Partha Bibhatsu, 'Partha, the time you wished for has come. Strike him,
tiger among men, unless you are lost in confusion. Hero, long ago at the meeting
of the kings you said, "I will kill all Dhritarashtra's son's soldiers, led by
Bhishma and Drona, with their followers, all who fight me in battle." Make that
word true, Kaunteya, tamer of enemies. Bibhatsu, see your own army breaking on
every side, and all the kings in Yudhishthira's force running. They have seen
Bhishma in battle like Death with his mouth open, and they are lost in fear,
like small deer before a lion.'

"So addressed, Dhananjaya answered Vasudeva, 'Drive the horses to where Bhishma
is. Plunge into this ocean of an army.' Then Madhava drove the silver-white
horses towards Bhishma's chariot, king, which was as hard to look at as the sun.
Then that great army of Yudhishthira turned back, seeing the strong-armed Partha
going out against Bhishma in battle.

"Then Bhishma, best of the Kurus, roaring again and again like a lion, quickly
covered Dhananjaya's chariot with showers of arrows. In a moment that chariot,
with its horses and charioteer, was hidden by the great shower of arrows and
could not be seen. But Vasudeva was not flustered. Steady and strong, he drove
on the horses, though Bhishma's arrows had torn them. Then Partha took up his
divine bow, which sounds like a cloud, cut Bhishma's bow with three arrows and
brought it down. His bow cut, the Kauravya, your father, strung another great
bow in the space of a blink. He drew that bow, which sounded like a cloud, with
both arms. Then Arjuna in his anger cut that bow too.

"Shantanu's son praised that lightness of his. 'Well done, Partha, strong-armed
one! Well done, joy of Pandu! This great deed is fitting for you alone,
Dhananjaya. I am very pleased, son. Fight with me.' So the hero praised Partha.
Then he took another great bow and loosed arrows at Partha's chariot in battle.
Vasudeva showed the highest skill in driving horses. He made Bhishma's arrows
useless, wheeling quickly in circles. Even so, sir, Bhishma pierced Vasudeva and
Dhananjaya hard with sharp arrows all over their bodies. Wounded by Bhishma's
arrows, the two tigers among men shone like two bellowing bulls marked by the
scratches of horns. Again, very angry and roused in battle, Bhishma blocked
every quarter of the two Krishnas with arrows with bent joints. In his fury he
made the Varshneya shake with sharp arrows, Bhishma smiling again and again and
laughing aloud.

"Then the strong-armed Krishna saw Bhishma's courage in battle, and saw how
softly Partha fought. He saw Bhishma loosing showers of arrows without pause,
blazing like the sun in the middle of the two armies. He saw Bhishma killing the
best of the best soldiers of Pandu's son and bringing a kind of end of an age on
Yudhishthira's force. The Blessed One, Keshava, killer of enemy heroes, whose
self is beyond measure, could not bear it. He thought, 'Yudhishthira's force is
finished. In one day Bhishma could destroy the gods and the danavas in battle.
How much more the sons of Pandu, with their forces and followers? The
great-souled Pandava's great army is running. And these Kauravas, seeing the
Somakas broken, are rushing quickly into battle, glad, gladdening the
grandfather. So I will arm myself and kill Bhishma today for the Pandavas' sake.
I will lift this burden from the great-souled Pandavas. Arjuna too, though he is
being struck with sharp arrows in battle, does not know what he must do, out of
respect for Bhishma.'

"While he was thinking this, the grandfather in his anger sent still more arrows
at Partha's chariot. There were so many arrows that all the quarters were
covered. Neither sky nor quarters nor earth could be seen, nor the sun with its
garland of rays. Tumultuous winds blew, mixed with smoke, and all the quarters
were thrown into turmoil.

"Drona, Vikarna and Jayadratha, Bhurishravas, Kritavarma and Kripa, Shrutayu and
the king who was lord of the Ambashthas, Vinda and Anuvinda and Sudakshina, the
easterners, all the Sauvira bands, the Vasatis, the Kshudrakas and the Malavas
all hurried at the diademed one at the command of King Bhishma son of Shantanu.
Shini's grandson saw the diademed one being surrounded by nets of horses, foot
soldiers and chariots in many hundreds of thousands, and by leaders of elephant
herds. Seeing Arjuna and Vasudeva, the best of those who bear weapons, attacked
on every side by foot soldiers, elephants, horses and chariots, the hero of the
Shinis rushed there quickly. That great archer, the hero of the Shinis, fell on
those ranks at once and gave Arjuna help, as Vishnu helped the killer of Vritra.

"The hero of the Shinis saw Yudhishthira's army running, its crowds of
elephants, horses, chariots and banners shattered, all its warriors terrified by
Bhishma. He said, 'Where will you go, kshatriyas? This is not the dharma of good
men as the ancients told it. Do not abandon your own promise, heroes. Keep the
dharma of heroes.'

"Vasava's younger brother heard that. He saw the chief kings running on every
side. He saw how softly Partha fought, and saw Bhishma rising in the battle, and
the Kurus rushing in on every side. The great-souled lord of all the Dasharhas
could not bear it, and he spoke to the glorious Shaineya, praising him. 'Let
those who are going go, hero of the Shinis. Let those who are standing go too,
Satvata. Watch Bhishma thrown from his chariot today by me, and Drona with his
troops in battle. Satvata, there is no chariot fighter among the Kauravas who
could escape me in battle today in my anger. So I will take up my fierce discus
and take the life of him of the great vow. Having killed Bhishma and his troops
in battle, Shaineya, and Drona, the hero among chariot fighters, I will please
Dhananjaya, the king, Bhima and the two Ashvins' sons. I will kill all the sons
of Dhritarashtra and the chief kings who are on their side. Then today I will
gladly give the kingdom to King Ajatashatru.'

"Then Vasudeva's son raised in his arm the discus, with its fine nave,
razor-edged, bright as the sun, its power like the thunderbolt. He let go of the
horses, leapt down from the chariot, and the great-souled Krishna ran at Bhishma
with speed, making the earth shake under his feet. He was like a lion in high
pride in battle, running to kill a lordly elephant blind with rut. Great Indra's
younger brother, the crusher, ran in anger at Bhishma in the middle of the army.
The end of his yellow garment hung loose, and he shone like a cloud in the sky
wrapped in lightning. Shauri's discus Sudarshana shone like a lotus, with his
fine arm for its long stalk. It shone like the first lotus, the colour of the
young sun, that grew from Narayana's navel. Its bud was opened by the rising sun
of Krishna's anger. Its fine petals were its sharp, razor-edged rim. It grew
from the great lake of his body, and Narayana's arm was its stalk. Seeing great
Indra's younger brother angry, roaring aloud, the discus in his hand, all beings
cried out, thinking, "This is the end of the Kurus." Vasudeva, the teacher of
the worlds, discus in hand, rushing forward, looked as if he would roll up the
living world. He looked like the fire of Time about to burn up all beings.

"Seeing the god, the best of two-footed beings, coming at him discus in hand,
Shantanu's son stood on his chariot, bow and arrow in hand, and was not
flustered. He said, 'Come, come, lord of the gods, dwelling place of the world!
I bow to you, bearer of the Sharnga bow and the discus. Throw me by force from
this fine chariot in battle, lord of the world, refuge of beings. If I am killed
by you here today, Krishna, there is good for me in the other world and in this
one. Lord of the Andhakas and Vrishnis, I am honoured in the three worlds, hero,
by your coming against me.'

"Then Partha too leapt quickly down from the chariot and ran after the hero of
the Yadus. With his own long, thick arms he seized Hari by his two arms, thick,
fine and long. But the first god, the yogin, held back, grew very angry, they
say. Vishnu took Jishnu with him at speed, as a great wind carries off a lone
tree. Partha, wearing the diadem and garland, planted his feet with all his
strength. Somehow, king, at the tenth step, he held him back by force as he
rushed quickly at Bhishma.

"When Krishna stood still, Arjuna bowed to him, glad, wearing a bright golden
garland. He said, 'Hold back your anger. You are the refuge of the Pandavas,
Keshava. I will not give up the work as I promised, Keshava. I swear it by my
sons and my brothers. Younger brother of Indra, urged on by you, I will make an
end of the Kurus.' Then Janardana, pleased at heart, heard his promise and his
pledge. Standing in the best of the Kauravas' favour, he climbed back onto the
chariot with the discus. The killer of enemies took up the reins again, took his
conch and filled the quarters with the sound of Panchajanya. His necklace,
armlets and earrings were swinging, his curved eyelashes and eyes were covered
with dust, his teeth were pure white, and he held his conch. Seeing him so, the
Kuru heroes shouted.

---

"Then fierce sounds of mridangas, kettledrums and tabors, the noise of chariot
wheels and drums, with lion roars, rose from all the Kuru ranks. The roar of
Partha's Gandiva, like thunder, went through the sky and the quarters. Bright,
clean arrows loosed from the Pandava's bow went in every direction. The lord of
the Kauravas, with Bhishma and Bhurishravas, went out against him with a force,
arrows raised in his hands, like a comet wanting to burn up dry brush.
Bhurishravas shot seven broad arrows with golden feathers at Arjuna. Duryodhana
hurled a lance of fierce speed, Shalya a club, and Shantanu's son a spear. With
seven arrows Arjuna stopped the seven fine arrows Bhurishravas had loosed. With
a sharp razor-headed arrow he cut down the lance that came from Duryodhana's
arm. Then with two arrows the hero cut down the bright spear, flashing like
lightning, that Shantanu's son had loosed, and the club from the arm of the lord
of the Madras as they came at him.

"Then he drew his bright, measureless bow Gandiva hard with both arms and, by
the rule, brought into being in the sky the wonderful and very terrible weapon
of Mahendra. With that supreme weapon the great-souled great archer, wearing the
diadem and garland, held off all the ranks with nets of arrows like clean fire.
The arrows loosed from Partha's bow cut chariots, banner tops, bows and arms,
and went into the bodies of the enemy, of kings, lordly elephants and horses.
Then Partha spread the quarters and the points between them with sharp arrows,
keen-edged, and the twang of Gandiva troubled the enemies' minds. In that most
terrible battle the sounds of conches and drums and the fierce cries of war were
drowned by Gandiva's twang. Knowing that sound of Gandiva, the heroes led by the
King of Virata, and the hero Drupada, king of the Panchalas, came to that place,
high in spirit. And all your troops, wherever the sound of Gandiva came from,
there they only bowed down. No one went against him.

"In that very terrible clash of kings, heroes were killed with their chariots
and charioteers. Elephants, burned by falling iron arrows, with great flags and
bright golden girths, fell at once, lifeless, their armour and bodies split by
the diademed one. They had been struck hard by Partha with feathered arrows of
fierce speed and sharp-pointed broad arrows. At the heads of the army great
banners fell, their fittings cut and their supporting pins shattered. Crowds of
foot soldiers, chariots, horses and elephants, struck by Dhananjaya's arrows in
battle, quickly lost their lives. With their limbs stiffened they fell to the
earth, king, their armour and bodies split in the great battle by that supreme
weapon of Indra.

"Then with floods of sharp arrows the diademed one set flowing on that
battlefield a very terrible river. Its water was the blood from the wounds of
men's bodies, and its foam was the flesh of men. It ran very fast and wide, and
its sound was frightful. The bodies of dead elephants and horses were its banks,
and its mud was the guts, marrow and flesh of men. Crowds of rakshasas and
spirits haunted it. Its grass was the hair on skulls. It carried thousands of
heaps of bodies, and its waves were broken armour of many kinds. Its gravel was
the bones of men, horses and elephants. It led down to the underworld of
destruction and brought fear. They saw it on every side, its banks crowded with
herons, vultures and kankas, with crowds of flesh-eaters and hyenas. It was
cruel and looked like the great Vaitarani, set flowing by Arjuna's floods of
arrows, very fearful, running with fat, marrow and blood.

"Then the Chedis, the Panchalas, the Karushas, the Matsyas and all the Parthas
roared together. Having terrified the army of the leaders of hosts, as a lion
terrifies herds of deer, Gandiva's bowman and Janardana both roared, filled with
joy. Then the Kurus saw the sun drawing in its net of rays. Their bodies were
badly wounded by weapons, and they saw that terrible Indra weapon spread
everywhere, unbearable, like the end of an age. Seeing that twilight had come,
red-streaked from the sun, the Kurus withdrew, with Bhishma, Drona, Duryodhana
and the Bahlikas. Dhananjaya too, having won fame and glory in the world and
conquered his enemies, his work done, went at nightfall to the camp with the
kings and his brothers.

"Then at the start of night a tumultuous and very terrible noise rose among the
Kurus. 'In battle Arjuna has killed ten thousand chariot fighters and seven
hundred elephants. All the easterners, the Sauvira bands, the Kshudrakas and the
Malavas have been brought down. Dhananjaya has done a great deed, such as no one
else could do. Shrutayu and the king who is lord of the Ambashthas, Durmarshana
and Chitrasena, Drona, Kripa, the Sindhu king and Bahlika, Bhurishravas, Shalya
and Shala, king, with Bhishma, have been conquered by the strength of his own
arms by the diademed one, the great chariot fighter of the world.' Saying this,
Bharata, all the bands on your side went to their camps. Lit with thousands of
torches blazing brightly, and with shining lamps, the Kuru host, all its
warriors terrified by the diademed one, made camp."

**Divergences.**

* Krishna runs at Bhishma with the discus on day 3 (6.55.86–92), and Arjuna stops him "at the tenth step" (6.55.98). See ledger L-36.
* In the rejected line \*201, Krishna calls the discus Sudarshana to mind and it comes into his hand by itself. In the CE text he simply raises it (6.55.86). In \*204, Krishna and Bhishma argue about whose fault the war is. Neither is narrated, nor are the other rejected lines (\*195–\*217).

**Choices.**

* 6.55.83 *rathāṅgam*: "discus". The word can mean a chariot wheel, but 6.55.86 calls it *cakraṃ* with a razor rim, and 6.55.94 names Krishna "bearer of the Sharnga bow and the discus" (*śārṅgarathāṅgapāṇe*).
* 6.55.89 *sudarśanaṃ*: taken as the discus's name.
* 6.55.89–90: the lotus image is kept in full.
* 6.55.97 *kila nāma*: "they say".
* 6.55.101 *sthitaḥ priye kauravasattamasya*: "standing in the best of the Kauravas' favour", meaning that he did what Arjuna wanted.
