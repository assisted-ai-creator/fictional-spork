summary: The morning of the seventh day. Duryodhana, wounded and afraid of Bhima, asks Bhishma for victory. Bhishma promises to fight the Pandavas with his whole self, though he warns that they cannot be beaten, and gives him a herb that heals his arrow wounds. Bhishma forms the circle array and Yudhishthira the thunderbolt. Arjuna breaks the Trigarta kings with the Indra weapon. Drona kills Virata's son Shankha in his father's sight. Ashvatthama unhorses Shikhandi, and Satyaki routs the rakshasa Alambusa with the Indra weapon. Dhrishtadyumna unhorses Duryodhana, and Bhima leaves Kritavarma looking like a porcupine. Dhritarashtra complains that his men are always losing, and Sanjaya answers that their courage is wasted against the Pandavas like Ganga's sweet water in the sea. Iravan beats the two princes of Avanti, Bhagadatta drives off Ghatotkacha, and Sahadeva's arrow leaves his uncle Shalya senseless.

# The Seventh Day

"Then, great king," said Sanjaya, "the heroes, each wronged by the other, went
to their own camps, soaked in blood. They rested as was fitting and honoured one
another, and then they were seen in their armour again, ready to fight.

"Then your son, king, overwhelmed with worry, his limbs smeared with flowing
blood, asked the grandfather, 'The armies were fierce and fearful, well drawn
up, with many banners. The Pandavas' heroes, their swift floods of chariots,
have torn them, killed them and crushed them. Those famous men have bewildered
everyone in battle. Bhima entered that makara array, as hard as a thunderbolt,
and struck me down with terrible arrows like the staff of Death. When I saw him
in his anger I fainted with fear, king, and I have found no peace today. By your
grace, you who are true to your word, I want to win victory and kill the
Pandavas.'

"When he said this, the great, high-minded son of Ganga, best of those who bear
weapons, knew that Duryodhana was angry. Laughing, and not at all troubled, he
answered him, 'Prince, I plunge into the army with the greatest effort and with
my whole self, and I want to give you victory and happiness. I do not hide
myself for your sake. But these fierce great chariot fighters, many of them,
famous, the bravest, masters of weapons, who are the Pandavas' helpers in
battle, have overcome weariness and spit the poison of their anger. They cannot
be beaten here in a hurry. They are swollen with courage, and you have made
enemies of them. I will fight them, king, with my whole self, giving up my life,
hero. In battle, for your sake, great one, my life is not the thing I most want
to keep today. For your sake I would burn all the worlds, with the gods and
daityas, let alone your enemies here. So I will fight the Pandavas, king, and I
will do everything you want.'

"When he heard this Duryodhana was very pleased, and his heart was glad.
Delighted, he told all the armies and all the kings, 'Go out!' At his command
they went out quickly, tens of thousands of chariots, horses, foot soldiers and
elephants. Full of joy, great, bearing weapons of many kinds, standing with
their elephants, horses and foot soldiers, your forces shone on the field, king.
The herds of elephants, standing in groups and well yoked, shone on every side.
Your armies were led by warriors who knew weapons and missiles, god among men.
The chariots and the masses of foot soldiers, elephants and horses moved forward
in the battle as they were ordered. The dust rose, the colour of the young sun,
and shone, hiding the sun's rays. The flags on the chariots and elephants shone,
blown about by the wind on every side, of many colours, like lightning with
clouds in the sky. As the kings stretched their bows there was a very terrible,
tumultuous noise, like the ocean long ago when the hosts of gods and great
asuras churned it. So your sons' army rose up, with its fierce noise and its
many forms and colours, like the masses of clouds at the end of an age, ready to
kill the enemy's army.

"Then Ganga's son, best of the Bharatas, spoke again to your son, who was lost
in thought, words to give him joy. 'Think of me and Drona and Shalya, Kritavarma
the Satvata, Ashvatthama and Vikarna, Somadatta and the Saindhava. Think of
Vinda and Anuvinda of Avanti, Bahlika with the Bahlikas, the mighty Trigarta
king and the Magadha, so hard to beat, Brihadbala of Kosala, Chitrasena and
Vivimshati. There are many thousands of chariots, splendid with great banners,
and horses of our own lands ridden by good horsemen. There are lordly elephants
in rut with streaming temples, and brave foot soldiers from many lands with
weapons of many kinds, ready to fight for you. These, and many others who have
given up their lives for you, are able, I think, to beat even the gods in
battle.

"'But I must always tell you what is good for you, king. The Pandavas cannot be
beaten even by the gods with Vasava. They have Vasudeva to help them, and their
valour is equal to great Indra's. Still, lord of kings, I will do what you say
in every way. Either I will beat the Pandavas in battle, or the Pandavas will
beat me.'

"Having said this, he gave him a fine and powerful herb that heals the wounds of
arrows, and he was healed of his arrow wounds then.

"Then, in the clear morning, mighty Bhishma, skilled in arrays, drew up an array
himself with his own army. It was the circle, best of men, crowded with weapons
of many kinds, full of chief warriors, elephants and foot soldiers. It was
surrounded on every side by many thousands of chariots and great troops of
horsemen bearing spears and javelins. With every elephant were seven chariots,
and with every chariot seven horsemen. With every horseman were ten archers, and
with every archer seven men with shields. So your army, great king, drawn up
with its great chariot fighters, stood ready for the great battle, guarded in
the fight by Bhishma. Ten thousand horses, as many elephants and ten thousand
chariots, and your sons in their armour, brave men led by Chitrasena, guarded
the grandfather. Guarded by those heroes, and themselves guarding him, the
mighty kings were seen in their armour. Duryodhana, in his armour, standing in
his chariot in the battle, shone with splendour like Shakra in heaven. Then
there was a great noise among your sons, Bharata, a tumultuous rumble of
chariots and the sound of instruments. Bhishma drew up the great circle array of
the sons of Dhritarashtra facing west for battle. Hard for the killers of
enemies to break, it shone on every side, king, hard for the enemy to come at.

"Seeing the circle, that most terrible array, King Yudhishthira himself drew up
the thunderbolt array. When the armies had been drawn up like this, standing in
their places, the chariot fighters and horsemen roared like lions. Then the
heroes went out, eager for battle, wanting to break each other's arrays,
striking hard, with their armies.

"Bharadvaja's son went against the Matsya, and Drona's son against Shikhandi.
King Duryodhana himself charged Prishata's son. Nakula and Sahadeva, king, went
against the lord of the Madras. Vinda and Anuvinda of Avanti charged Iravan. All
the kings fought Dhananjaya in the battle. Bhimasena, set on it, held back
Hridika's son. Arjuna's son fought your sons Chitrasena, Vikarna and Durmarshana
in the battle, lord. Hidimba's son, best of rakshasas, charged the great archer
of Pragjyotisha, like a mad elephant against a mad elephant. Then the rakshasa
Alambusa, king, charged Satyaki, mad in battle, and his army, in anger.
Bhurishravas, set on it, fought Dhrishtaketu, and Yudhishthira, the son of
Dharma, fought King Shrutayus. Chekitana fought Kripa in the battle. The rest,
set on it, went against Bhima, the great chariot fighter.

"Then thousands of kings surrounded Dhananjaya, holding spears, javelins, iron
arrows, maces and iron bars. Arjuna, in great anger, said to the Varshneya,
'Madhava, look at the armies of Dhritarashtra's son in the battle, drawn up by
great Ganga's son, who knows arrays. Look at the heroes in their armour,
Madhava, eager for battle. Look at the Trigarta king with his brothers, Keshava.
Today, while you watch, Janardana, I will bring down these men who want to fight
me on the field, best of the Yadus.'

"Having said this, Kunti's son wiped his bowstring and rained showers of arrows
on the hosts of kings. And those supreme archers filled him with showers of
arrows, as clouds fill a lake with streams in the rains. A great cry of 'Alas!'
rose in your army, lord of the people, when they saw the two Krishnas covered
deep with arrows in the great battle. The gods and the divine seers, the
gandharvas and the great serpents, were full of wonder to see the two Krishnas
like that. Then, king, Arjuna in anger called up the Indra weapon. There we saw
Vijaya's marvellous valour: he held off with floods of arrows the shower of
weapons the enemy had loosed, and not one of them was left unpierced, lord of
the people. Partha pierced the thousands of kings, and their horses and
elephants, with two or three arrows each, sir. Being killed by Partha, they went
to Bhishma, son of Shantanu. Bhishma was then their rescuer as they sank in
bottomless water. As they came pouring in, your army broke and heaved, great
king, like the great ocean in the winds.

"So the battle went. Susharma had turned back, and the heroes had been broken by
the great Pandava. Your army was heaving quickly like the sea, and Ganga's son
had set out swiftly against Vijaya. Then King Duryodhana, seeing Partha's valour
in battle, came up quickly and spoke to all those kings. To brave, mighty
Susharma at their head, in the middle of the whole army, he spoke words to give
them great joy. 'Here is Bhishma, son of Shantanu, best of the Kurus, ready to
fight Dhananjaya with his whole self, giving up his own life. As the Bharata
goes against the enemy's army with the whole army, all of you, set on it, guard
the grandfather in the battle.'

"Saying, 'Yes!', all those divisions of the kings came together to the
grandfather, great king. Then Bhishma, son of Shantanu, set out at once and met
mighty Arjuna, the Bharata, coming at him in the battle. Arjuna shone in his
great chariot, yoked with great white horses, with its terrible monkey banner
and its thunder like a great cloud. When they saw Kiritin come against them in
the battle, a tumultuous noise of fear rose from all the armies. Seeing Krishna
with the reins in his hand, like a second sun at midday in the battle, they
could not look at him. In the same way the Pandavas could not look at Bhishma,
son of Shantanu, with his white horses and white bow, like the white planet
risen. He was surrounded on every side by the great Trigartas, by his brothers,
by your sons and by other great chariot fighters.

"Bharadvaja's son pierced the Matsya with a feathered arrow in the battle, and
cut his banner with one arrow and his bow with another. Virata, lord of an army,
threw away the cut bow and quickly took another, strong enough to bear any load,
and arrows like venomous snakes, like blazing serpents. He pierced Drona with
three, and his horses with four. He pierced his banner with one and his
charioteer with five. He pierced his bow with one arrow, and at that the bull
among brahmins grew angry. Drona killed his horses with eight straight arrows,
best of the Bharatas, and his charioteer with one feathered arrow. With his
horses and his charioteer killed, the best of chariot fighters leapt down from
his chariot and quickly climbed into Shankha's chariot.

"Then the father and son, standing in one chariot, held back Bharadvaja's son by
force with a great shower of arrows. Then Bharadvaja's son, in anger, quickly
hurled an arrow like a venomous snake at Shankha in the battle, lord of men. It
split his heart, drank his blood in the battle, and went into the ground, its
colour wet with red. Struck by Bharadvaja's son's arrow, he quickly fell from
the chariot, dropping his bow and arrows, right beside his father. Seeing his
own son killed, Virata ran in fear, leaving Drona in the battle, like Death with
his mouth open. Then Bharadvaja's son quickly tore the great army of the
Pandavas apart in the battle, by the hundred and the thousand.

"Shikhandi too, great king, reached Drona's son in the battle and struck him
between the brows with three swift iron arrows. With the three arrows standing
in his forehead, that tiger among men looked like Mount Meru with three tall
golden peaks. Then Ashvatthama in anger, in half the blink of an eye, aimed many
arrows at Shikhandi and brought down his charioteer, his banner, his horses and
his weapons in the battle, king. With his horses killed, the best of chariot
fighters leapt down from his chariot. Taking a sharp, bright sword and a shield,
Shikhandi, scorcher of enemies, moved about in anger like a hawk. As he moved
about in the battle with his sword, great king, Drona's son could find no
opening in him, and it was a marvel. Then Drona's son, in great anger, sent many
thousands of arrows at him in the battle, bull of the Bharatas. The best of the
mighty cut down that very cruel shower of arrows with his sharp-edged sword as
it came at him. Then Drona's son cut his bright, lovely shield with its hundred
moons, and broke his sword in the battle, and pierced him again and again with
sharp arrows, king. Shikhandi whirled the sword that Drona's son had broken with
his arrows and quickly hurled it, like a blazing snake. As it came suddenly at
him, bright as the fire of Time, Drona's son cut it down in the battle, showing
the speed of his hands, and pierced Shikhandi with many iron arrows. Badly hurt
by the sharp arrows, king, Shikhandi quickly climbed into great Madhava's
chariot.

"Then Satyaki in anger pierced the cruel rakshasa Alambusa with terrible arrows
in the battle, mighty against the mighty. The lord of rakshasas cut his bow with
a crescent-headed arrow, Bharata, and pierced him with arrows in the battle.
Then he made a rakshasa's magic and covered him with showers of arrows. There we
saw a marvel of valour in Shaineya: he was not shaken in the battle, though
struck by sharp arrows. The Varshneya called up the Indra weapon, Bharata, which
famous Madhava had got from Vijaya. That weapon burned the rakshasa's magic to
ashes, and he covered Alambusa all over with terrible arrows, like a cloud
pouring streams of rain on a mountain in the rains. Harassed like that by great
Madhava, the rakshasa ran in fear, leaving Satyaki in the battle. Having beaten
the lord of rakshasas, whom even Maghavan could not beat in battle, Shaineya
roared while your warriors watched. Satyaki, whose valour was true, killed your
men too with many sharp arrows, and they ran, tormented by fear.

"At that very time, great king, Drupada's mighty son Dhrishtadyumna covered your
son, the lord of men, with straight arrows in the battle. Covered with arrows by
Dhrishtadyumna, Bharata, your son, the lord of men, was not shaken, lord of
kings. He quickly pierced Dhrishtadyumna in the battle with sixty arrows and
then thirty, and it was a marvel. The commander, the great chariot fighter, in
anger cut his bow, sir, quickly killed his four horses and swiftly pierced him
with seven very sharp arrows. With his horses killed, the mighty, strong-armed
man leapt down from his chariot, raised his sword and ran on foot at Prishata's
son. Mighty Shakuni, eager for the king's sake, came up and took the king of the
whole world into his own chariot. Then Prishata's son, killer of enemy heroes,
having beaten the king, killed your army as the Wielder of the Thunderbolt
killed the asuras.

"Kritavarma struck Bhima, the great chariot fighter, with arrows in the battle
and covered him as a great cloud covers the sun. Then Bhimasena, scorcher of
enemies, laughing in the battle, in anger sent arrows at Kritavarma. Harassed by
them, the Satvata, a chariot fighter beyond the rest and skilled in weapons, did
not tremble, great king, and struck Bhima with sharp arrows. Mighty Bhimasena
killed his four horses and brought down his charioteer and his finely made
banner. The killer of enemy heroes heaped him with arrows of many kinds, and
with every limb cut about he looked like a porcupine. From his chariot with its
horses killed, he went quickly to the chariot of Vrishaka, your brother-in-law,
great king, while your son watched. Bhimasena too, in anger, charged your army
and killed it in his fury, like Death with his staff in his hand."

"Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "I have heard you tell of many wonderful single
fights between the Pandavas and my men. But you never tell me of any of my men
being glad, Sanjaya. You always tell me that the sons of Pandu are glad and
unbroken. You tell me that my men are being beaten in battle, charioteer,
dejected and without strength. This is surely fate."

"Your men strive in battle as far as their strength and their spirit allow,"
said Sanjaya, "showing the highest manhood they can, bull among men. The water
of Ganga, the river of the gods, is sweet, but it turns salt when it joins the
great ocean. Just so the manhood of your great men, king, comes to nothing in
battle when it meets the heroic sons of Pandu. Best of the Kurus, you should not
blame the Kauravas, who are striving as hard as they can and doing hard deeds.
This great and terrible destruction of the earth, which fills Yama's kingdom,
lord of the people, comes from your wrongdoing and your son's. It has arisen
from your own fault, and you should not grieve over it, king. For kings do not
guard their wealth or even their lives. The lords of the earth, wanting the
worlds of those who do good, always plunge into the army and fight with heaven
as their goal.

"In the morning, great king, the destruction of men went on. Listen with a
single mind to that battle, like the battle of the gods and the asuras.

"The two great archers of Avanti, great and mighty, fierce in battle, saw Iravan
and met him, and a tumultuous, hair-raising battle began between them. Iravan,
in great anger, quickly pierced the two godlike brothers with sharp, straight
arrows. Those two wonderful fighters pierced him back in the battle. As they
fought, king, striving to destroy the enemy, each eager to return blow for blow,
no difference was seen between them. Then, king, Iravan sent Anuvinda's four
horses to Yama's house with four arrows. With two very sharp broad arrows he cut
his bow and his banner in the battle, sir, and it was a marvel. Then Anuvinda
left his chariot and stood in Vinda's chariot, taking up a ninth bow, the best,
able to bear any load. Standing together in the battle, the two heroes of
Avanti, best of chariot fighters, quickly loosed arrows at great Iravan. The
arrows they loosed, of great speed and adorned with gold, reached the path of
the sun and covered the sky. Then Iravan, in anger, rained a shower of arrows on
those two great chariot fighters, and brought down their charioteer. When the
charioteer had fallen lifeless to the ground, the chariot ran off in every
direction with its horses bolting. Having beaten them both, great king, the son
of the naga king's daughter quickly blew away your army, making his manhood
known. Being killed in the battle, the great army of the sons of Dhritarashtra
moved in many kinds of spasms, like a man who has drunk poison.

"Hidimba's son, the mighty lord of rakshasas, charged Bhagadatta in a chariot
the colour of the sun, with its banner. The king of Pragjyotisha was mounted on
a lordly elephant, as the Wielder of the Thunderbolt was long ago in the battle
over Tara. The gods and gandharvas and seers who had gathered there could not
tell the difference between Hidimba's son and Bhagadatta. As Shakra, lord of the
gods, terrified the danavas, so in the battle, king, he terrified the Pandavas.
Put to flight by him in every direction, the Pandavas found no rescuer in their
own ranks, Bharata. There we saw only Bhima's son standing in his chariot,
Bharata. The other great chariot fighters lost heart and ran. When the Pandava
armies turned back again, Bharata, a terrible groan went up among your armies in
the battle.

"Then, king, Ghatotkacha covered Bhagadatta with arrows in the great battle, as
a cloud covers Mount Meru. The king destroyed the arrows loosed from the
rakshasa's bow, and quickly struck Bhima's son in every vital point in the
battle. Struck by many straight arrows, the lord of rakshasas did not flinch,
like a mountain being split. The angry lord of Pragjyotisha sent fourteen
javelins at him in the battle, and the rakshasa cut them. Having cut the
javelins with sharp arrows, the strong-armed one pierced Bhagadatta with seventy
arrows feathered with heron plumes. Then the lord of Pragjyotisha, king, almost
laughing, brought down his four horses in the battle with arrows, Bharata.
Standing in his chariot with its horses killed, the mighty lord of rakshasas
hurled a spear with great force at the elephant of Pragjyotisha. As it suddenly
came at him, with its golden shaft, flying fast, the king cut it in three, and
it was scattered over the ground. Seeing his spear struck down, Hidimba's son
ran in fear, as Namuchi, best of the daityas, ran from Indra's battle long ago.
Bhagadatta had beaten in battle that brave, valiant hero of famous manhood, whom
even Yama and Varuna could not beat, king. Then he crushed the Pandava army with
his elephant, like a wild elephant trampling a lotus pond.

"The lord of the Madras met the twins in the battle and covered his sister's
sons, the sons of Pandu, with floods of arrows. Seeing his mother's brother
engaged with him in the battle, Sahadeva held him back with a flood of arrows,
as a cloud holds back the sun. Covered with the flood of arrows, Shalya looked
still more glad, and they too felt a boundless affection for him, because of
their mother. Then, laughing in the battle, the great chariot fighter sent
Nakula's four horses to Yama's house with four fine arrows, king. The great
chariot fighter leapt quickly down from his chariot with its horses killed and
climbed into his famous brother's chariot. Standing together in the battle, the
two heroes stretched their strong bows and in anger covered the Madra king's
chariot in a moment. Covered with many straight arrows by his sister's sons, the
tiger among men did not tremble, like a mountain. Almost laughing, he struck
down that shower of arrows.

"Then brave Sahadeva in anger raised an arrow, and seeing the Madra king,
Bharata, he sent it at him. Loosed by him, the arrow, as swift as Garuda, split
the Madra king and fell to the ground. Deeply pierced and in pain, the great
chariot fighter sank down on the floor of his chariot, great king, and fainted.
Seeing him fallen senseless and hard pressed by the twins, his charioteer
carried him off in his chariot in the battle. Seeing the Madra lord's chariot
turned away, all the sons of Dhritarashtra lost heart and thought, 'This is the
end.' Having beaten their mother's brother in the battle, the two sons of Madri,
great chariot fighters, blew their conches with joy and roared like lions. Glad,
they charged your army, lord of the people, as the two immortals Indra and
Upendra charged the army of the daityas."

**Divergences.**

* **Shankha.** Drona kills Virata's son Shankha in front of his father on the seventh day (6.78.18–22). On the first day Shankha had survived his brother Uttara (ch 15; ledger L-193).
* **The healing herb.** Bhishma gives Duryodhana "a fine and powerful herb that heals the wounds of arrows" (*viśalyakaraṇī oṣadhi*, 6.77.10).
* **Ghatotkacha runs.** The text says plainly that Ghatotkacha ran in fear from Bhagadatta, "as Namuchi ran from Indra" (6.79.39).
* **Shalya and the twins.** Shalya grows "still more glad" as his nephews cover him with arrows, and they feel "a boundless affection" for him because of their mother (6.79.44).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*322–\*326.
