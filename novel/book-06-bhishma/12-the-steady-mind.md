# The Steady Mind

"To him," said Sanjaya, "so filled with pity, his troubled eyes full of tears,
sinking in despair, Madhusudana said this.

"'Where has this weakness come to you from, Arjuna, at this hard time? It is not
fit for a noble man. It does not lead to heaven, and it brings disgrace. Do not
give way to unmanliness, Partha. It does not suit you. Throw off this mean
weakness of heart and stand up, scorcher of enemies.'

"'How can I fight Bhishma and Drona in battle with arrows, Madhusudana,' said
Arjuna, 'when they are worthy of worship, slayer of enemies? It would be better
to live on alms in this world than to kill my teachers, great men. If I killed
my teachers, however greedy for gain they are, I would enjoy here pleasures
smeared with blood. We do not know which is better for us, that we should win,
or that they should win against us. The sons of Dhritarashtra stand facing us,
and if we killed them we would not want to live. My nature is struck by the
fault of pity, my mind is confused about dharma, and I ask you: tell me for
certain what is better. I am your pupil. I have come to you. Teach me. For I do
not see what could drive away this grief that dries up my senses, even if I won
a rich kingdom without rival on earth, or lordship over the gods.'

"When Gudakesha, scorcher of enemies, had said this to Hrishikesha, he said to
Govinda, 'I will not fight,' and fell silent. Then, Bharata, as if smiling,
Hrishikesha said this to him as he sat in despair between the two armies.

"'You grieve for those who should not be grieved for, and yet you speak words of
wisdom. The wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the living. Never was there
a time when I was not, nor you, nor these lords of men, and never shall we cease
to be, any of us, from now on. As the embodied one passes in this body through
childhood, youth and old age, so it passes to another body. The steady man is
not confused by this. Contacts of the senses with their objects, son of Kunti,
give cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and go and do not last. Bear
them, Bharata. The man whom these do not trouble, bull among men, who is the
same in pain and pleasure and steady, is fit for immortality.

"'What is not does not come to be, and what is does not cease to be. Those who
see the truth have seen the end of both. Know that what pervades all this is
indestructible. No one can destroy what does not decay. These bodies of the
eternal embodied one, indestructible and beyond measure, are said to come to an
end. So fight, Bharata. He who thinks this one a killer, and he who thinks it
killed, neither of them understands. It does not kill and is not killed. It is
never born and never dies. Having been, it will not again cease to be. Unborn,
eternal, everlasting, ancient, it is not killed when the body is killed. If a
man knows it to be indestructible, eternal, unborn and undecaying, how can he
kill, Partha, or cause anyone to kill? As a man throws off worn-out clothes and
puts on others that are new, so the embodied one throws off worn-out bodies and
goes into others that are new. Weapons do not cut it, fire does not burn it,
water does not wet it, and wind does not dry it. It cannot be cut, burned,
wetted or dried. It is eternal, everywhere, fixed, unmoving and everlasting. It
is called unmanifest, unthinkable and unchanging. So, knowing it to be like
this, you should not grieve.

"'And even if you think it is always being born and always dying, even so,
strong-armed one, you should not grieve for it. For death is certain for one who
is born, and birth is certain for one who has died. So you should not grieve
over what cannot be avoided. Beings are unmanifest in their beginning, manifest
in their middle, Bharata, and unmanifest again in their end. What is there to
lament in that? One sees it as a wonder, another speaks of it as a wonder,
another hears of it as a wonder, and even having heard, no one knows it. This
embodied one in the body of everyone is always beyond killing, Bharata. So you
should not grieve for any being.

"'And looking to your own dharma too, you should not waver. For a kshatriya
there is nothing better than a war that is in keeping with dharma. Happy are the
kshatriyas, Partha, who find such a war, come of itself, an open door to heaven.
But if you will not fight this just war, then you will give up your own dharma
and your good name and incur sin. Beings will speak of your disgrace for ever,
and for a man who has been honoured, disgrace is worse than death. The great
chariot fighters will think you turned back from battle out of fear, and you
will become small in the eyes of those who thought much of you. Your enemies
will say many things that should not be said, scorning your ability. What could
be more painful than that? Killed, you will win heaven. Victorious, you will
enjoy the earth. So stand up, son of Kunti, resolved to fight. Making pleasure
and pain alike, gain and loss, victory and defeat, then get ready for battle. So
you will not incur sin.

---

"'This understanding has been told you in terms of Samkhya. Now hear it in terms
of yoga. Joined to this understanding, Partha, you will throw off the bondage of
action. In this no effort is lost and no harm is done. Even a little of this
dharma saves one from great fear. Here the understanding that is resolute is
one, joy of the Kurus. The understandings of the irresolute have many branches
and no end.

"'There are flowery words that the unwise speak, Partha, delighting in the
letter of the Veda, saying there is nothing else. Full of desire, intent on
heaven, they offer rebirth as the fruit of action, with many special rites aimed
at pleasure and power. Those who cling to pleasure and power, whose minds are
carried off by those words, do not have the resolute understanding that rests in
concentration. The Vedas deal with the three qualities. Be free of the three
qualities, Arjuna, free of the pairs of opposites, always standing in goodness,
free of getting and keeping, master of yourself. As much use as there is in a
well when there is flood water everywhere, so much is there in all the Vedas for
a brahmin who knows.

"'Your right is to the work alone, never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit of
action be your motive, and do not cling to doing nothing. Standing in yoga, do
your work, giving up attachment, Dhananjaya, the same in success and failure.
Sameness is called yoga. Action alone is far lower than the yoga of
understanding, Dhananjaya. Seek refuge in understanding. Those who work for the
fruit are wretched. One joined to understanding leaves behind here both good
deeds and bad. So set yourself to yoga. Yoga is skill in action. The wise,
joined to understanding, giving up the fruit born of action, freed from the bond
of birth, go to the place beyond sorrow. When your understanding has crossed the
thicket of confusion, you will grow indifferent to what you will hear and what
you have heard. When your understanding, confused by what you have heard, stands
still, unmoving in concentration, then you will reach yoga.'

"'What is the description of the man of steady wisdom, Keshava,' said Arjuna,
'who stands in concentration? How does the man of steady thought speak? How does
he sit? How does he move?'

"'When he gives up all the desires that have come into his mind, Partha,' said
the Blessed One, 'content in the self by the self, then he is called a man of
steady wisdom. One whose mind is not troubled in sorrows, who has no longing in
pleasures, from whom passion, fear and anger are gone, is called a sage of
steady thought. One who has no attachment anywhere, who neither rejoices nor
hates when he meets this or that, good or bad, his wisdom is firmly set. When he
draws in his senses from their objects on every side, as a tortoise draws in its
limbs, his wisdom is firmly set.

"'The objects turn away from the embodied one who does not feed on them, but the
taste remains. Even the taste turns away when he has seen the highest. For the
senses, son of Kunti, which carry the mind away, carry off by force the mind of
even a wise man who strives. Restraining them all, he should sit, joined, intent
on me. For one whose senses are under control, his wisdom is firmly set.

"'When a man dwells on objects, attachment to them is born. From attachment
comes desire, and from desire anger is born. From anger comes confusion, from
confusion the wandering of memory, from the loss of memory the ruin of
understanding, and from the ruin of understanding he perishes. But one who moves
among objects with senses free of passion and hatred, under the control of the
self, his self disciplined, reaches clarity. In clarity all his sorrows come to
an end, for the understanding of one whose mind is clear soon becomes steady.
The undisciplined man has no understanding, and the undisciplined man has no
power to dwell on it. One who cannot dwell on it has no peace, and how can a man
without peace be happy? For when the mind follows the wandering senses, it
carries off his wisdom as the wind carries a boat on the water. So, strong-armed
one, one whose senses are drawn back from their objects on every side, his
wisdom is firmly set.

"'What is night for all beings, in that the disciplined man is awake. What
beings are awake in is night for the sage who sees. As the waters enter the
ocean, which is always being filled and stays firmly in its place, so the man
whom all desires enter in the same way finds peace, and not the man who desires
desires. The man who gives up all desires and moves without longing, without
"mine" and without self-regard, finds peace. This is the state of Brahman,
Partha. Having reached it, one is not confused. Standing in it even at the hour
of death, one reaches the peace of Brahman.'"
