summary: The evening of the tenth day and the morning after. Dhritarashtra grieves that his heart does not break. Sanjaya tells how the earth roared and the sky went dark when Bhishma fell, and how Drona fell from his chariot at the news and stopped the fighting. The kings of both sides take off their armour and gather round Bhishma. He refuses soft pillows, and Arjuna props up his head with three arrows. Bhishma says he will lie there until the sun turns north, sends away the surgeons, and asks for a trench to be dug around him. Krishna tells Yudhishthira that Bhishma was burnt by his terrible eye. Next morning, crowds come to honour Bhishma. He refuses food and water brought by men, and Arjuna shoots an arrow into the earth beside him and a stream of cool, sweet water springs up. Bhishma begs Duryodhana to make peace and give the Pandavas half the kingdom, but Duryodhana does not listen. When the kings have gone, Karna comes and falls at Bhishma's feet. Bhishma embraces him, tells him that he is Kunti's son, and says his harsh words were meant only to curb his pride. Karna answers that he cannot betray Duryodhana, and asks for leave to fight. Bhishma gives it.

# The Bed of Arrows

"Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "how did the warriors stand it, left without
Bhishma? He was strong as a god, and he had lived chaste for his father's sake.
I think the Kurus and all the other kings were already dead when Bhishma would
not strike Drupada's son, out of pity. What could hurt more than this, that
today I, fool that I am, have heard that my father is dead? My heart must be
made of iron, Sanjaya, because it does not break into a hundred pieces at the
news that Bhishma has been killed. Again and again I cannot bear it, that
Devavrata has been killed in battle. Rama Jamadagnya could not kill him long ago
with all his divine weapons. Tell me, Sanjaya, what did that lion among men do
today, after he was struck down, still wanting victory?"

"He fell to the ground in the evening," said Sanjaya, "the old grandsire of the
Kurus. He left your sons in despair and gave joy to the Panchalas. Bhishma fell
from his chariot and lay on a bed of arrows, not touching the earth. A great cry
of 'Alas! Alas!' went up from every living thing when the tree at the Kurus'
boundary fell in that slaughter. Fear came over the kshatriyas of both armies,
king. The Kurus and the Pandavas both turned back when they saw Bhishma, the son
of Shantanu, with his armour and his banner broken.

"The sky was covered in darkness, and the sun lost its light. The earth roared
when Bhishma, the son of Shantanu, was struck down. 'This is the best of those
who know Brahman,' the beings said of the bull of the Bharatas as he lay there.
'This is the goal of those who know Brahman. This bull among men knew once that
his father Shantanu was in the grip of desire, and made himself one who would
never marry.' So the seers, with the siddhas and charanas, said as they came
running from all sides to the foremost of the Bharatas on his bed of arrows.

"When Bhishma, the son of Shantanu, the grandsire of the Bharatas, was struck
down, your sons did not know what to do, Bharata. Their faces lost their colour
and their shine, and they stood with their heads bowed in shame. The Pandavas
had won, and they stood at the front of the battle and blew their great conches,
set with gold. Amid the loud music of drums and trumpets, king, we saw mighty
Bhimasena, Kunti's son, delighting in play after killing strong enemies in
battle. A great stupor fell on the Kurus. Karna and Duryodhana sighed again and
again. Now that Bhishma, who had carried the whole weight of the Kauravas, had
fallen, there were cries of grief everywhere, and all order broke down.

"When your son Duhshasana saw Bhishma fallen, he raced at full speed to Drona's
division. His brother had sent him out, armoured, at the head of his own
division, and now that tiger among men went, urging his troops on. The Kurus
crowded round Duhshasana as he came, great king, wondering what he would say.
Then the Kaurava told Drona that Bhishma had been killed. When Drona heard the
bitter news, he fell from his chariot at once. When he came to himself, the
mighty son of Bharadvaja called back his troops, sir. The Pandavas saw the Kurus
turn back, and they sent messengers on fast horses to stop their own soldiers.

"When all the armies, one after another, had turned back, all the kings took off
their armour and came to Bhishma. Hundreds of thousands of warriors stopped
fighting and stood before that great man, as the gods stand before Prajapati.
The Pandavas and the Kurus together came up to Bhishma, the bull of the
Bharatas, as he lay there. They bowed to him and stood waiting.

"Then Bhishma, the son of Shantanu, spoke to the Pandavas and the Kurus who
stood bowing before him. 'Welcome, noble ones,' he said. 'Welcome, great chariot
fighters. It pleases me to see you, you who are like gods.' When he had greeted
them, he spoke again, with his head hanging down. 'My head hangs down too far.
Give me a pillow.'

"The kings brought fine pillows, thin and soft, but the grandsire did not want
them. That tiger among men almost laughed, and said to the kings, 'These do not
suit a hero's bed, kings.'

"Then he looked at the best of men, the long-armed Pandava Dhananjaya, the
greatest chariot fighter in all the worlds, and spoke to him. 'Dhananjaya,
strong-armed one, my head is hanging down. Give me whatever pillow you think
right here.'

"Arjuna laid down his great bow and bowed to the grandsire, and with his eyes
full of tears he said, 'Command me, best of the Kurus, first of all who bear
arms. I am your servant, invincible one. What shall I do, grandsire?'

"'My head hangs down, my son,' said Shantanu's son. 'Bring me a pillow,
Phalguna, best of the Kurus. Give me one that suits my bed, and quickly, hero.
For you are the best of all bowmen, strong-armed Partha. You know the duty of
the kshatriya, and you have wisdom and courage.'

"'So be it,' said Phalguna, and he set to work. He took up the Gandiva and some
arrows with smooth joints, asked the great man's leave, and propped up his head
with three sharp, swift arrows. Bhishma, the best of the Bharatas, who knew the
truth of dharma and profit, was pleased that Savyasachin had understood what he
meant. He praised Dhananjaya for the pillow he had given him, and said to
Kunti's son, the best of warriors, who brought joy to his friends, 'Pandava, you
have given me what suits the bed I lie on. If you had done anything else, I
would have cursed you in anger. That is how it must be, strong-armed one, for
one who holds to his duty. In battle a kshatriya should sleep like this, on a
bed of arrows.'

"When he had said this to Bibhatsu, he spoke to all of them, to the kings and
the princes gathered with the Pandavas. 'I will lie on this bed until the sun
turns. The kings who live until then will see me. When the blazing sun, burning
the worlds in his splendid chariot, goes to the north, the quarter that belongs
to Vaishravana, then I will let go of my life, and of my dear friends too. Dig a
trench around my resting place here, kings. Covered as I am with hundreds of
arrows, I will worship the sun. Stop fighting, kings, and let go of your
quarrels.'

"Then surgeons came to him, skilled in drawing out arrows. They had everything
they needed, and they were expert and well trained. When he saw them, Ganga's
son said, 'Pay the physicians, honour them and send them away. Now that I am
like this, I have no use for physicians. I have reached the highest end, the one
that the kshatriya's duty praises. This is not the right thing, kings, for one
who lies on a bed of arrows. When the end comes, I must be burnt with these very
arrows.'

"When he heard this, your son Duryodhana honoured the physicians as they
deserved and sent them away. The kings of many lands were amazed to see Bhishma,
whose power had no measure, stand so firm in his duty.

"When they had given your father the pillow, lord of men, the Pandavas and the
Kurus, all those great chariot fighters, came together to the great man on his
bed of honour. They bowed to Bhishma and walked around him with their right
sides towards him, and they set guards all around him. Then, in the evening,
spattered with blood and deeply troubled, the heroes went back to their own
camps to rest, brooding.

"When the Pandavas had settled in, those great chariot fighters were happy and
glad at Bhishma's fall. Then, when the time was right, the Yadava Krishna came
to Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma. 'By good fortune you are winning, Kaurava,'
he said. 'By good fortune Bhishma has been brought down. He is a great chariot
fighter who keeps his word, and no man could kill him. Nor even the gods,
Partha, for he had mastered every weapon and missile. But he came up against
you, whose eye can kill, and your terrible eye burnt him up.'

"'Victory comes by your grace,' Dharma's king answered Janardana, 'and defeat by
your anger. You are our refuge, Krishna. You take away the fear of those who
love you. It is no wonder that those who have you win, Keshava, when you always
guard them in battle and always look to their good. When we have come to you for
refuge, nothing is a wonder. That is what I think.'

"Janardana smiled at this and answered, 'Such words suit you alone, best of
kings.'

"When the night had passed, great king, all the kings, the Pandavas and the sons
of Dhritarashtra, went to the grandsire. The kshatriyas bowed to that bull of
the kshatriyas, the best of the Kurus, lying on his hero's bed, and stood before
him. Girls came with sandalwood powder, parched grain and garlands. Women,
children and old people came, and ordinary folk came to watch. They all came to
Shantanu's son as living things come to the sun that drives away the dark.
Musicians, courtesans, actors and dancers came, and danced and sang around the
old grandsire of the Kurus. The Kurus and the Pandavas stopped fighting, took
off their armour and laid down their weapons. They sat together around
Devavrata, the invincible tamer of enemies, as friendly with one another as
before, each in his place by age. That gathering, full of hundreds of kings and
graced by Bhishma, shone as brightly as the sun's disc in the sky. As the kings
sat around the grandsire, they looked like the gods sitting around Brahma, the
grandsire and lord of the gods.

"Bhishma held back the pain by force of will, bull of the Bharatas, but he
burned from the arrows, and he spoke without much cheer. 'My body burns with
arrows,' he said to the kings. 'I am faint from the burning of the arrows. I
want water.'

"Then the kshatriyas brought all kinds of food, great and small, king, and pots
of cool water from everywhere. When Bhishma, the son of Shantanu, saw what they
had brought, he said, 'Today I can take no human pleasures, my sons. I have
passed beyond men. I lie on a bed of arrows, and I stay here waiting for the
turning of the moon and the sun.'

"When Shantanu's son had said this to all the kings, in a weak voice, he spoke
to strong-armed Dhananjaya, Bharata. The strong-armed one came up, bowed to the
grandsire, and stood humbly with his hands joined. 'What shall I do?' he said.

"The grandsire was glad to see the Pandava standing bowed before him, and he
spoke to Dhananjaya. 'This body of mine is burning,' he said. 'I am sewn through
with great arrows. My vital parts are in torment, and my mouth is dry. Give me
water to cool my body, Arjuna. You are the one who can give me water in the
right way, great archer.'

"'So be it,' said Arjuna, and the hero climbed into his chariot. He strung the
Gandiva with force and drew the bow. The slap of the string was like the crack
of a thunderbolt, and every creature, and every king who heard it, trembled.
Then that best of chariot fighters drove his chariot round the best of the
Bharatas as he lay there, the first of all who bear arms, keeping him on his
right. The famous Partha fitted a blazing arrow to the string, spoke a spell
over it and joined it to the weapon of Parjanya. Then, while the whole world
watched, he pierced the earth at Bhishma's right side.

"A clear, lovely jet of water shot up. It was cool, like the nectar of the gods,
with a heavenly scent and taste. Partha, whose deeds and courage were godlike,
gave Bhishma, the bull of the Kurus, his fill of that cool stream. The kings
were amazed at Partha's deed, as if Shakra himself had worked a wonder. When the
Kurus saw Bibhatsu's marvellous deed, beyond what men can do, they shook like
cattle stung by the cold. The kings waved their upper cloths in wonder, and the
sound of conches and drums rose everywhere.

"Shantanu's son, his thirst quenched, spoke to Bibhatsu in front of all the
heroes among the kings, as if he were honouring him. 'This is no wonder in you,
strong-armed joy of the Kauravas. Narada, whose splendour has no measure, told
of you as the ancient seer. With Vasudeva to help you, you will do a great deed
that the king of the gods, even with all the gods, surely could not manage.
Those who know, Partha, know you as the death of all the kshatriyas. You alone
are the best of bowmen on earth, among men. Among creatures in the world, men
are best. Among birds, Garuda is best. Among waters, the ocean is best, and
among beasts, the cow. Among lights, the sun is best. Among mountains, the
Himalaya is best. Among births, the brahmin is best, and among bowmen, you are
the best.

"'Dhritarashtra's son did not listen when Vidura tried to wake him to it,' he
went on. 'Nor did he listen when Drona, Rama, Janardana and Sanjaya told him
again and again. Duryodhana's mind is turned, and he is as good as senseless. He
did not welcome my words. He has gone beyond what the teachings allow, and he
will be overcome by Bhima's strength and lie dead for a long time.'

"When Duryodhana, the lord of the Kauravas, heard this, he was downcast.
Shantanu's son looked at him and said, 'Listen, king, and let go of your anger.
You have seen, Duryodhana, how wise Partha made a stream of cool water, with the
scent of nectar. There is no one else in this world who could do it. The weapons
of Agni, Varuna, Soma, Vayu and Vishnu, of Indra, Pashupati and Brahma, of
Parameshthin, Prajapati, Dhatri, Tvashtri and Savitri, all the divine weapons,
are known in the whole world of men to Dhananjaya alone, or to Krishna, Devaki's
son. No one else knows them. The Pandavas cannot be beaten in battle in any way,
my son.

"'His deeds are more than human. He is brave and strong, a hero who shines in
battle, and skilled in war. Make peace with him, my son. While strong-armed
Krishna is still his own master in the assembly of the Kurus, make peace with
brave Partha. Before Arjuna destroys the rest of your army with his smooth-
jointed arrows, make peace, my son. While your surviving brothers and many kings
still stand in battle, king, make peace. Before Yudhishthira, his eyes blazing
with anger, burns up your army, make peace, my son. Before Nakula, Sahadeva and
Bhimasena the Pandava destroy your whole army, great king, choose brotherhood
with the Pandavas.

"'Let the war end with me, my son. Make peace with the Pandavas. Accept what I
have told you, blameless one. I think this is best for you and for the family.
Let go of your anger and make peace with the sons of Pritha. What Phalguna has
done is enough. Let there be friendship between you from Bhishma's end, or a
reconciling. Be kind, king. Give half the kingdom to the Pandavas, and let the
King of Dharma rule Indraprastha. Do not betray your friends and become the
lowest of kings, lord of the Kauravas, or you will win an evil name. Let my end
bring peace to the people. Let the kings meet in friendship. Let father go back
to son, king, nephew to uncle, and brother to brother. If, in your folly and
blind with delusion, you do not follow my timely words, then with Bhishma's end
all of you will come to your end. I speak the truth to you, Bharata.'

"When Ganga's son had spoken these words to the Bharata out of goodwill, among
the kings, he fell silent. His vital parts burned from the arrows, and he held
himself steady and held down the pain.

"Then all the kings went back to their own quarters, great king, now that
Bhishma, the joy of Shantanu, had fallen silent.

"When Radheya, the bull among men, heard that Bhishma had been struck down, he
came quickly, somewhat afraid. He saw the great man lying on his bed of arrows,
like the lord Skanda on the bed where he was born. Bhishma's eyes were closed.
Vrisha, with tears in his throat, went up to the hero and fell at his feet.
'Best of the Kurus,' he said to him, 'I am Radheya, who was always in your
sight, and whom you always hated, though I had done nothing wrong.'

"When he heard this, the old man of the Kurus slowly opened his eyes, though it
cost him great effort. He saw that the place was empty, and he sent the guards
away. Then Ganga's son put one arm around him, as a father holds his son, and
spoke to him with love.

"'Come, come, my rival, who always tries to match me,' he said. 'If you had not
come to me, it would surely not have gone well for you. You are Kunti's son, not
Radha's. I know it from Narada, and from Krishna Dvaipayana and from Keshava,
beyond doubt. I bear you no hatred, my son. I am telling you the truth. I spoke
harshly to you only to curb your fire. I thought you hated the Pandavas for no
reason. That is why I spoke so roughly to you so often, son of the Sun.

"'I know your courage in battle, which enemies can hardly bear, and your
devotion to brahmins, your bravery, and your great generosity. There is no man
like you, you who are like a god. It was because I feared a split in the family
that I always spoke harshly. In archery, in fitting the arrow to the string, in
speed of hand and in the strength of your weapons, you are the equal of Phalguna
and of great Krishna. Karna, you went to Rajapura, alone with your bow, and for
the Kuru king's sake you crushed the kings there in battle. And strong King
Jarasandha, who was so hard to face and so proud of his fighting, was not your
equal in battle. You are devoted to brahmins, you tell the truth, and you shine
like a second sun. You are born of a god, unbeaten in battle and above other men
on earth.

"'The anger I felt towards you before is gone today. No one can overcome fate by
human effort. The heroic Pandavas are your brothers, born of the same mother,
destroyer of enemies. Join them, strong-armed one, if you want to please me. Son
of the Sun, let the enmity end with me. Let all the kings on earth be free from
harm today.'

"'I know it, wise one, all of it, without a doubt,' said Karna. 'It is as you
say, invincible one. I am Kunti's son, not a charioteer's. But Kunti cast me
away, and a charioteer brought me up. I have enjoyed Duryodhana's wealth, and I
cannot make it false now. My wealth, my body, whatever is dear to me, and my
fame, I have given it all up for Duryodhana, generous one. Relying on Suyodhana,
I have always angered the Pandavas. What must be cannot be turned back. Who can
overcome fate by human effort?

"'Grandsire, you saw the omens that foretold the destruction of the earth, and
you told them in the assembly. I know very well that the Pandavas and Vasudeva
cannot be beaten by other men, and yet we dare to face them. Give me leave, my
father. I will fight with a glad heart. With your leave, hero, I would fight.
That is my resolve. Whatever harsh or contrary word I have said, whatever wrong
I have done you in anger or in rashness, please forgive it.'

"'If this terrible enmity truly cannot be given up,' said Bhishma, 'then I give
you leave, Karna. Fight, and seek heaven. Without anger and without passion, do
the king's work as well as you can and with all your spirit, as good men do. I
give you leave. Win what you want. You will reach the worlds that are won by the
kshatriya's duty, without doubt. Fight without pride, trusting in your strength
and courage. For a kshatriya there is no duty better than fighting. I tried for
a long, long time to bring about peace, but I could not do it. Where dharma is,
there is victory.'

"When the son of Ganga had said this, Radheya bowed to him and won his favour.
Then he climbed into his chariot and drove off to your son."

**Divergences.**

* **The pillow.** Bhishma refuses the kings' soft pillows and asks Arjuna for one that suits "a hero's bed". Arjuna props up his head "with three sharp, swift arrows" (6.115.33–46).
* **The water.** Arjuna joins an arrow to the weapon of Parjanya and pierces the earth at Bhishma's right side. "A clear, lovely jet of water" springs up, cool, "like the nectar of the gods", with a heavenly scent and taste (6.116.22–24). The CE does not say that Ganga herself rose to her son. See ledger L-200.
* **"Until the sun turns."** Bhishma says he will give up his life when the sun goes to the north, "the quarter that belongs to Vaishravana" (6.115.48–49; also 6.116.13). The CE's count of the nights he lay there comes much later, at 13.153.27: "fifty-eight nights". See ledger L-201.
* **Karna.** Bhishma tells Karna, alone, that he is Kunti's son, and that he knows it from Narada, Vyasa and Krishna (6.117.9). He says his harsh words were meant "to curb your fire" (*tejovadhanimittam*, 6.117.10) and because he feared "a split in the family" (6.117.13). Karna asks leave to fight, and Bhishma gives it (6.117.27–33). On the insult that kept Karna out of the war, see ledger L-184.
* **"Where dharma is, there is victory."** Bhishma ends with *yato dharmas tato jayaḥ* (6.117.33), the words Gandhari says to Duryodhana each day (ledger L-195).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*482–\*513. \*498 and \*499 are riddles on the word "cow" (*go*) that Bhishma speaks as he asks for water. \*505 has Bhishma say "Adhiratha is not your father; you are the son of the Sun". \*512–\*513 are the verses that praise the hearing of the Bhishma Parva.
