# The Pillow of Arrows

Dhritarashtra said, "How were the warriors then, Sanjaya, without Bhishma, the
strong one like a god, who lived as a celibate for his father's sake? I think
the Kurus and the other kings were killed at the moment when Bhishma would not
strike Drupada's son out of pity. What greater grief than this could come, now
that I, evil-minded as I am, have heard that my father has been killed? Surely
my heart is made of stone, Sanjaya, since it does not break into a hundred
pieces at hearing that Bhishma has been killed. Again and again I cannot bear
it, that Devavrata has been killed in battle, he whom Jamadagni's son could not
kill long ago with divine weapons. Tell me, Sanjaya, what the lion among men did
today, when he had been brought down in battle, Bhishma, who wanted victory."

"In the evening," said Sanjaya, "the old grandfather of the Kurus fell to the
ground, bringing despair to the sons of Dhritarashtra and joy to the Panchalas.
Bhishma lay on his bed of arrows, not touching the earth, fallen from his
chariot to the ground. A tumultuous cry of 'Alas!' arose among all beings when
that boundary tree of the Kurus fell in the slaughter of combat. Fear came over
the kshatriyas of both armies, king. Seeing Bhishma son of Shantanu, his armour
and banner shattered, the Kurus and the Pandavas turned back, lord of the
people. The sky was covered with darkness, the sun lost its light, and the earth
groaned when Bhishma son of Shantanu was killed. 'This is the best of those who
know brahman. This is the goal of those who know brahman,' beings said of the
bull of the Bharatas lying there. 'Long ago this bull among men, knowing that
his father Shantanu was sick with desire, made himself one who keeps his seed.'
So saying, the seers, with the siddhas and charanas, ran up to him on his bed of
arrows, by no means the least of the Bharatas.

"When Bhishma son of Shantanu, grandfather of the Bharatas, was killed, your
sons did not know what to do, Bharata. Their faces were pale, their glory gone,
Bharata, and they stood ashamed, their faces down in shame. But the Pandavas,
having won the victory, standing at the head of the battle, all blew their great
conches adorned with nets of gold. As the trumpets sounded loudly, faultless
one, we saw the very strong Bhimasena in battle, king, the Kaunteya, playing,
filled with great joy, having killed his very strong enemies in battle. A
tumultuous bewilderment came over the Kurus then. Karna and Duryodhana sighed
again and again. When Bhishma, who bore the Kauravas' yoke, had fallen like
that, everything became a cry of grief and went on without order.

"Seeing Bhishma fallen, your son Duhshasana went at top speed to Drona's army.
Sent by his brother, the hero, the tiger among men, in armour with his own
ranks, went urging on his own troops. Seeing him come, the Kurus surrounded
Duhshasana, great king, wondering, 'What will he say?' Then the Kaurava told
Drona that Bhishma had been killed. Hearing that unwelcome news, Drona fell from
his chariot at once. Recovering his senses, sir, the mighty son of Bharadvaja
held back his own ranks. Seeing the Kurus turned back, the Pandavas too had the
withdrawal made for their troops by messengers on swift horses.

"When the armies had turned back one after another on every side, all the kings
took off their armour and went to Bhishma. Ceasing from battle, the warriors in
hundreds of thousands came to the great-souled one as the immortals come to
Prajapati. Having reached Bhishma, the bull of the Bharatas, lying there, the
Pandavas with the Kurus greeted him and stood. Then the righteous Bhishma son of
Shantanu spoke to the Pandavas and the Kurus who stood before him, having bowed.
'Welcome, fortunate ones. Welcome, great chariot fighters. I am pleased by the
sight of you, you who are like immortals.'

"Having welcomed them so, with his head hanging, he said, 'My head hangs down
too much. Let a pillow be given.' Then the kings brought fine, soft, choice
pillows, but the grandfather did not want them. The tiger among men, as if
laughing, said to those kings, 'These are not fitting for a hero's bed, kings.'
Then, looking at the best of men, the Pandava Dhananjaya, long-armed, the great
chariot fighter of the whole world, he said, 'Dhananjaya, strong-armed one, my
head is hanging. Give me whatever pillow you think fitting here.'

"Laying down his great bow and greeting the grandfather, he said this, his eyes
full of tears. 'Command me, best of the Kurus, best of all who bear weapons. I
am your servant, unassailable one. What shall be done, grandfather?' Shantanu's
son said to him, 'My head is hanging, son. Bring me a pillow, best of the Kurus,
Phalguna. Give me quickly, hero, one that suits my bed. For you, strong-armed
Partha, are the best of all bowmen, and you know the dharma of the kshatriya,
and you have understanding, spirit and good qualities.'

"Phalguna said, 'So be it,' and, eager to act, took up Gandiva and arrows with
bent joints, having addressed them with mantras. Having asked leave of the
great-souled one, by no means the least of the Bharatas, he raised his head with
three sharp arrows of great speed. When the left-handed archer had understood
his wish, the righteous Bhishma, best of the Bharatas, who knew the truth of
dharma and of profit, was pleased. With the pillow given, he congratulated
Dhananjaya, the son of Kunti, the best of fighters, who increases the joy of his
friends. 'You have given a pillow that suits my bed, Pandava. If you had done
otherwise, I would have cursed you in anger. So it is, strong-armed one, firmly
set in dharma: a kshatriya who has gone to a bed of arrows in battle should
sleep like this.'

"Having said this to Bibhatsu, he spoke to all the kings and princes standing
there with the Pandava. 'I will lie on this bed until the sun turns. The kings
who come through to that time will see me. When the sun, blazing, scorching the
worlds with its chariot of the highest splendour, goes towards the quarter
Vaishravana rules, then I will let go of my life, dear as my friends are to me.
Let a trench be dug here round my dwelling, kings. So I will wait on the sun,
covered with hundreds of arrows. Cease from battle, kings, and give up your
enmities.'

"Then surgeons came, skilled in drawing out arrows, with all their instruments,
able and well trained. Seeing them, the son of Jahnavi said, 'Give the doctors
what is owed, honour them and send them away. Things being so, I have no need of
surgeons here now. I have reached the highest state, praised in the dharma of
the kshatriya. This is not dharma for me, now that I lie on a bed of arrows,
kings. I am to be burned at the end with these very arrows, lords of men.'
Hearing those words, your son Duryodhana sent away the surgeons, honouring them
as they deserved. Then the lords of many countries were filled with wonder,
seeing the highest constancy in dharma of Bhishma, of measureless splendour.

"Having given your father the pillow, lord of men, all the Pandavas together and
the Kauravas, the great chariot fighters, went up to the great-souled one lying
on that fine bed, greeted Bhishma and walked round him, keeping him on their
right. They all set a guard round Bhishma on every side. Then the heroes, deep
in thought and very distressed, went to their own camps in the evening to rest,
drenched in blood.

"When the great chariot fighters, the Pandavas, had settled down, glad at
Bhishma's fall and rejoicing, the Yadava came to them at the right moment and
said to Yudhishthira, son of Dharma, 'By good fortune you win, Kauravya. By good
fortune Bhishma has been brought down. That great chariot fighter, true to his
word, could not be killed by men, or by the gods, Partha, master of all arms and
weapons as he was. But having come up against you, whose look kills, he was
burned by your terrible eye.'

"So addressed, the King of Dharma answered Janardana, 'Victory comes from your
grace, and defeat from your anger. For you are our refuge, Krishna, who make
your devotees fearless. It is no wonder that they win whose protector in battle
you always are, Keshava, and who are always devoted to your good. Having come to
you, it is no wonder in any way. That is my mind.' So addressed, Janardana
answered, smiling, 'These words are fitting for you alone, best of kings.'"
