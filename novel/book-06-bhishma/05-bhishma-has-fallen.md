# Bhishma Has Fallen

Then wise Sanjaya, son of Gavalgana, came back from the battle, said
Vaishampayana. He saw everything before his own eyes, and knew past, present and
future. He came in haste to Dhritarashtra, who sat brooding, and in his grief he
told him that Bhishma, the first of the Bharatas, had been killed.

"I am Sanjaya, great king. I bow to you, bull among the Bharatas. Bhishma, son
of Shantanu, the grandfather of the Bharatas, has been struck down. The crown of
all warriors, the refuge of all bowmen, the grandfather of the Kurus lies today
on a bed of arrows. Bhishma, on whose valour your son relied when he played at
dice, lies struck down in battle by Shikhandi, king. The great chariot fighter
who alone, in one chariot, beat all the kings gathered in the great battle at
the city of Kashi, who was born of the Vasus, who fought Rama Jamadagnya in
battle and was not killed by Jamadagnya, has been killed today by Shikhandi. He
was like great Indra in courage, like the Himalaya in steadiness, like the ocean
in depth and like the earth in patience. With arrows for fangs, his bow for a
mouth and his sword for a tongue, no one could come near him. That lion among
men, your father, has been brought down today by the Panchala. When the great
Pandava army saw him rise in battle, it trembled in fear, like a herd of cattle
that sees a lion. For ten nights the destroyer of armies guarded your army. Then
he went to his setting like the sun, when he had done a very hard deed.
Unshakeable as Shakra, raining arrows by the thousand, he killed a hundred
million warriors in battle in ten days. Now he lies groaning on the ground like
a tree broken by the wind, king, because of your bad counsel, Bharata, as he did
not deserve."

"How was Bhishma, bull of the Kurus, killed by Shikhandi?" said Dhritarashtra.
"How did my father, who was like Vasava, fall from his chariot? How did my sons
fare without Bhishma, Sanjaya, that mighty, godlike man who lived chaste for his
father's sake? When that great spirit was killed, that great bowman of great
strength, great chariot fighter and tiger among men, what did they feel then?
The deepest pain comes over me, since you tell me he has been killed, the hero,
bull of the Kurus, unshakeable bull among men. Who followed him as he went? Who
went ahead of him? Who stood, who turned back, who pressed on, Sanjaya? What
brave men followed close behind that tiger among chariot fighters, the unshaken
bull among kshatriyas, as he plunged into the chariot ranks? The enemy's killer
drove off the enemy army as the sun drives off darkness. Like the thousand-rayed
sun, he struck fear into the enemy, and he did very hard deeds in battle at the
Kaurava's command. When he was devouring their ranks, who surrounded him? He was
skilled, and no one could come near him, as he came close in his own way. How
did the Pandavas hold Shantanu's son off in battle?

"He was cutting down their ranks, swift, with arrows for fangs, terrible, his
bow for a gaping mouth and his sword for a tongue, and no one could come near
him. He surpassed other tigers among men, he was modest, and he had never been
beaten. How did the son of Kunti bring him down, unconquered in battle? He was a
fierce bowman with fierce arrows, standing in his fine chariot, gathering the
enemy's heads with his sharp arrows. When the great Pandava army saw him rise in
battle, it always shrank from him, as from the fire at the end of time, which no
one can face. For ten nights the destroyer of armies harried my army. Then he
went to his setting like the sun, when he had done a very hard deed. Like Shakra
he loosed an endless rain of arrows and killed a hundred million warriors in
battle in ten days. He lies groaning on the ground like a tree broken by the
wind, the Bharata, through my bad counsel, as he did not deserve.

"How could the Pandava army bring itself to strike at Bhishma of terrible
valour, when it saw Shantanu's son there? How did the sons of Pandu fight
Bhishma? And how did Bhishma fail to win while Drona was alive, Sanjaya? When
Kripa was near, and Bharadvaja's son, how did Bhishma, best of fighters, meet
his death? How was Bhishma, an outstanding chariot fighter whom even the gods
could hardly face, killed in battle by Shikhandi of Panchala? He always rivalled
mighty Jamadagnya in battle, and Jamadagnya never beat him, and his valour was
like Shakra's. Tell me of that hero, Sanjaya, killed in battle, fit to face the
strength of great chariot fighters, without whom we know no peace. Which of my
great archers did not leave the unshaken one, Sanjaya? Which heroes surrounded
him at Duryodhana's order? When all the Pandavas came at Bhishma with Shikhandi
at their head, did the Kurus not leave the unshaken one in fear, Sanjaya?

"He rose like a great cloud, thundering with his bowstring, with arrows for
raindrops and the great roar of his twanging bow. He rained down on the sons of
Kunti, with the Panchalas and the Srinjayas, and killed the enemy's chariot
fighters as the bearer of the thunderbolt kills the danavas. He was a terrible
ocean of arrows and weapons, with arrows for crocodiles, bows for waves, maces
and swords for sea monsters and whirlpools, horses for alligators, full of
elephants, endless, with no island and no raft in battle, and no one could come
near him. He drowned horses, elephants, foot soldiers and chariots by the score
in battle, carrying off the enemy's heroes. He burned with anger and with
energy, that scorcher of enemies. Which heroes held him back, as the shore holds
back the home of sea monsters?

"When Bhishma, killer of enemies, did his deeds in battle for Duryodhana's good,
Sanjaya, who was in front of him then? Who guarded the right wheel of Bhishma,
whose power had no measure? Which heroes, strict in their vows, kept the enemy
off from behind? Who went in front, guarding Bhishma close? Which heroes guarded
the left wheel of the hero as he fought? Who, at his left wheel, killed the
Srinjayas, Sanjaya? Who guarded him at the head of the ranks, where no one could
come near him? Who kept at his sides, going the hard way? Who fought the enemy's
heroes in the press, Sanjaya? Guarded by heroes and guarding them, how did they
fail to beat quickly in battle the ranks of an enemy hard to beat? He was like
the supreme Prajapati, lord of all the worlds. How could the Pandavas even
strike at him, Sanjaya? You tell me that Bhishma, the tiger among men, has sunk,
the island the Kurus relied on when they fought the enemy, Sanjaya.

"My son, whose army is so strong, relied on his valour and did not count the
Pandavas for anything. How was he killed by the enemy? My father of the great
vow, mad for battle, was the one whose help the gods and Indra wanted long ago,
when they were killing the danavas. When he was born, mighty, bringing good to
the world, Shantanu put away sorrow, pain and misery in the fortune of a son. He
was wise, a last refuge, knowing, devoted to the true dharma, pure, knowing the
truth of the Vedas and their limbs. How can you tell me he has been killed? He
was trained in every weapon, self-controlled, calm and spirited. Hearing that
Shantanu's son has been killed, I think the rest of our army is killed too.

"I think adharma has grown stronger than dharma, when the Pandavas want the
kingdom and have killed their old teacher. Long ago Rama Jamadagnya, knower of
every weapon, unsurpassed, took up arms in battle for Amba's sake and was beaten
by Bhishma. You tell me that Bhishma has been killed, whose deeds were like
Indra's, the crown of all bowmen. What sorrow could be greater than this? The
killer of enemy heroes beat Rama Jamadagnya, who had beaten the hosts of the
kshatriyas in battle again and again. Surely, then, Shikhandi, son of Drupada,
has more energy, valour and strength than the mighty Bhargava, mad for battle,
since he has killed that brave and skilful bull among the Bharatas, learned in
all the teachings and knowing the supreme weapons. Which heroes followed that
killer of enemies in the enemy's midst? Tell me how Bhishma's battle with the
Pandavas went.

"My son's army is like a woman whose hero is dead, Sanjaya. My army is like a
herd of cows without a herdsman, running wild. When he was laid low, the man who
had the highest manhood of all the world in the great battle, what did you all
feel then? What strength is left in us, even alive, Sanjaya, when we have had
our mighty father killed, the most righteous man in the world? I think my sons
grieve bitterly now that Bhishma is dead, like people who want to cross a river
and see the boat sink in deep water. Surely my heart is made of the stuff of
mountains, very hard, since it does not break when I hear that Bhishma, tiger
among men, has been killed. The bull of the Bharatas, whom no one could
overcome, had weapons, wisdom and policy beyond measure. How was he killed in
battle? Not by weapons, not by courage, not by austerity, not by wisdom, not by
steadfastness, not even by renunciation is anyone freed from death. Surely time
is mighty, and no one in all the world can get past it, since you tell me
Bhishma, son of Shantanu, has been killed, Sanjaya.

"Burning with grief for my sons and thinking of the great sorrow to come, I
hoped before for protection from Bhishma, son of Shantanu. When Duryodhana saw
Shantanu's son fallen on the ground like the sun, Sanjaya, what did he do then?
Thinking it over in my mind, Sanjaya, I see nothing left of our own kings or of
the enemy's, in either army. This kshatriya dharma the seers have shown us is
harsh, in which the Pandavas want the kingdom and have killed Shantanu's son,
and we want the kingdom and have had the grandfather killed. The Parthas, who
hold to the kshatriya's dharma, do no wrong, nor do my sons. A noble man must do
this in hard straits, Sanjaya: show his valour with all his strength. And that
was firm in him.

"He was cutting down their ranks, modest and never beaten. How did the sons of
Pandu bring down Shantanu's son, my friend? How were the armies drawn up? How
did the great men fight? How was my father Bhishma killed by the enemy, Sanjaya?
When Bhishma was killed, what did Duryodhana and Karna say, and Shakuni, son of
Subala, and Duhshasana the gambler? Those foolish gamblers went into a hall in
battle where no one could come near, strewn with the bodies of men, elephants
and horses, terrible, with arrows, spears, maces, swords and lances for dice.
Which bulls among men played in that terrible game of lives? Who won there, and
who lost, their marks taken and struck down, apart from Bhishma, son of
Shantanu? Tell me, Sanjaya.

"I have no peace here, now that I have heard that my father Devavrata, of
terrible deeds, has been killed in battle. Grief has come over me. The great
pain rooted in my heart, which my sons have caused, you are kindling, Sanjaya,
as if you poured ghee on a fire. He had taken up a great burden, famous in all
the world. Seeing Bhishma killed, I think my sons are grieving. I shall hear of
the sorrows Duryodhana has made. So tell me everything that happened there,
Sanjaya. In that battle of the lords of the earth, which the fool's folly
brought about, tell me what was done badly or well, Sanjaya. Tell me all that
Bhishma did in battle, with his energy and his mastery of weapons, wanting
victory. Tell me how the battle went between the armies of the Kurus and the
Pandavas, in what order, at what time, and what happened, and how."

"This question is worthy of you, great king," said Sanjaya. "But you should not
fasten this fault on Duryodhana. A man who comes to harm by his own misconduct
should not suspect another of that sin. Great king, a man who does every
blameworthy thing among men deserves death from the whole world for doing what
is blamed. The Pandavas, who saw through the deceit, suffered humiliation with
their ministers out of regard for you, and they bore it for a long time in the
forest. What I saw with my own eyes and by the power of yoga, of horses and
elephants and heroes of boundless might, hear it, lord of the earth, and do not
set your mind on grief. Surely this was fated long ago to be so, lord of men.

"I bow to your father, the wise son of Parashara. By his grace I received the
highest divine knowledge. By that great man's boon I have sight beyond the
senses, king, and hearing from afar, knowledge of other minds and of past and
future, and knowledge of the source of what arises. I can go always through the
sky, and weapons do not touch me in battle. Hear me tell at length the great
battle of the Bharatas as it happened, varied and most wonderful, making the
hair stand on end.

"When the armies were ready and drawn up in due order, great king, Duryodhana
said to Duhshasana, 'Duhshasana, have the chariots that guard Bhishma yoked
quickly, and urge on all the divisions at once. The meeting I have been thinking
of for years has come to me, of the Pandavas and their armies with the Kurus. I
think nothing in the battle is more important than guarding Bhishma. If he is
guarded, he will kill the Parthas, the Somakas and the Srinjayas. That pure soul
has said, "I will not kill Shikhandi. He is said to have been a woman before, so
I must avoid him in battle." So I think Bhishma must be guarded above all. Let
all my men stand ready to kill Shikhandi. Let those of the east, the west, the
south and the north, skilled in every weapon, guard the grandfather. If it is
not guarded, even a mighty lion may be killed by a wolf. Let us not have the
lion killed by Shikhandi, as by a jackal. Yudhamanyu guards Phalguna's left
wheel and Uttamaujas his right. Those two guard Phalguna, and Phalguna guards
Shikhandi. See to it, Duhshasana, that Shikhandi, guarded by Partha and avoided
by Bhishma, does not kill Ganga's son.'

"Then, as the night turned to dawn, a great noise rose as the kings shouted,
'Yoke! Yoke!' With the roar of conches and drums and the lion roars of the men,
Bharata, with the neighing of horses and the rumble of chariot wheels, with the
trumpeting of elephants and the roaring of warriors, with war cries, the
slapping of arms and shouting, there was uproar everywhere. At sunrise, great
king, the whole great host of the Kuru and Pandava armies rose up, yoked and
ready, your sons', lord of kings, and the Pandavas'.

"There the elephants and chariots, adorned with Jambunada gold, shone like
clouds with lightning. The ranks of chariots looked like so many cities, and
your father shone there brightest of all, like the full moon. The warriors stood
in their ranks with bows, spears, swords, maces, javelins and lances, their
weapons gleaming. Elephants, chariots, foot soldiers and horses stood like nets,
lord of the people, by the hundred and the thousand. Banners of many shapes were
raised up, our own and the enemy's, shining by the thousand. The kings' banners,
golden and inlaid with gems, blazed like fires by the thousand, bright as
Indra's banners in Indra's halls. The heroes in their armour looked at them,
eager for battle. The lords of men stood at the head of the armies, bright with
their raised weapons, with leather guards on their arms and quivers on their
backs, and eyes like bulls.

"There were Shakuni, son of Subala, Shalya, Jayadratha of Sindhu, and Vinda and
Anuvinda of Avanti. There were Sudakshina of Kamboja, Shrutayudha of Kalinga,
King Jayatsena, Brihadbala of Kosala and Kritavarma the Satvata. These ten
tigers among men, brave, with arms like iron bars, were the lords of the
akshauhinis, men who had sacrificed with rich gifts. These and many others who
followed Duryodhana's will, kings and princes, wise in policy and mighty, were
seen in their armour, standing in their ranks. All of them wore black deerskins
and garlands of munja grass, and had their banners. They had given themselves up
for Duryodhana, consecrated for the world of Brahma, and they stood in command
of ten rich armies. The eleventh, the great army of the Kauravas,
Dhritarashtra's son's own, was in front of all the armies, where Shantanu's son
was the leader.

"We saw Bhishma, great king, with his white turban, his white horses and his
white armour, the unshaken one, like the moon when it has risen. The Kurus and
the Pandavas saw Bhishma, with his banner of the golden palm, standing in his
silver chariot, like the sun in a white cloud. When they saw Bhishma at the head
of the army, the Pandavas trembled, and so did the Srinjayas, great archers, led
by Dhrishtadyumna. As small beasts shrink when they see a great lion yawning,
all of them, led by Dhrishtadyumna, shrank again and again.

"Your eleven armies, Bharata, blessed with fortune, and the Pandavas' seven,
guarded by great men, looked like two oceans met at the end of an age, whirling
with maddened sea monsters and full of great crocodiles. We had never seen or
heard of such a gathering of armies before, king."

**Divergences.**

* **When Sanjaya tells the war.** In the CE the battle is not told as it happens. Sanjaya comes back "from the battle" after Bhishma has fallen, ten days in, and tells it all from the beginning, at Dhritarashtra's request (6.14.1–13; 6.15.75). The Gita is told within this account. A rejected line (\*59) has Sanjaya go to Kurukshetra at Dhritarashtra's word. See ledger L-190.
* **Sanjaya's powers.** Sanjaya says he saw the battle "with my own eyes and by the power of yoga", and names Vyasa's gifts: sight beyond the senses, hearing from afar, knowledge of other minds and of past and future, going through the sky, and freedom from weapons (6.16.5–9).
* **Who is to blame.** Sanjaya tells Dhritarashtra that he should not put the fault on Duryodhana alone, since a man who comes to harm through his own misconduct should not blame another (6.16.1–2). In 6.14.13 he has already said that Bhishma lies "because of your bad counsel".
* **Shikhandi.** Duryodhana repeats Bhishma's refusal to kill Shikhandi, "said to have been a woman before" (6.16.15), and sets guards against him (6.16.16–20). See ledger L-185.
* **The armies.** Ten lords of akshauhinis are named, and the eleventh army, Duryodhana's own, is led by Bhishma (6.16.33–39). The Pandavas have seven (6.16.44).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*59–\*66. Among them are Bhagadatta and Brihadratha of Kosala among the leaders (\*66).
