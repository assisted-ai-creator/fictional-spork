# The Lamp out of the Wind

"'One who does the work he should, without depending on its fruit,' said the
Blessed One, 'is both renouncer and yogin, not one who has no sacred fire or
does no rites. Know that what they call renouncing is yoga, Pandava, for no one
becomes a yogin without renouncing his purposes. For the sage who wants to climb
to yoga, action is said to be the means. For the same man when he has climbed to
yoga, calm is said to be the means. When he does not cling to the objects of the
senses or to actions, and has renounced all purposes, then he is said to have
climbed to yoga.

"'One should lift up the self by the self, and not let the self sink down. For
the self alone is the friend of the self, and the self alone is the enemy of the
self. The self is a friend to the self of one who has conquered the self by the
self. But for one who has not, the self behaves like an enemy, in enmity. The
highest self of one who has conquered himself and is at peace is steady in cold
and heat, pleasure and pain, honour and dishonour. The yogin whose self is
satisfied with knowledge and discernment, who stands firm like an anvil, his
senses conquered, to whom a clod, a stone and gold are the same, is called
disciplined. One whose understanding is the same towards friends, allies,
enemies, the indifferent, the neutral, the hateful and kin, towards the good and
the evil, is the best.

"'The yogin should always discipline himself, staying in a secret place, alone,
his thought and self controlled, without hope and without possessions. In a
clean place he should set for himself a firm seat, not too high and not too low,
covered with cloth, deerskin and kusha grass. There he should sit and make his
mind one-pointed. Controlling the working of his thought and senses, he should
practise yoga to purify the self. He should hold body, head and neck even and
still, steady, gazing at the tip of his nose and not looking about him. His self
at peace, free of fear, keeping the vow of chastity, controlling his mind, with
his thought on me, he should sit disciplined, intent on me. Always disciplining
himself like this, the yogin whose mind is controlled reaches peace, the highest
nirvana, which rests in me.

"'Yoga is not for one who eats too much, Arjuna, nor for one who does not eat at
all, nor for one who sleeps too much, nor for one who stays awake. Yoga, which
destroys sorrow, is for one who is measured in eating and recreation, measured
in his actions, measured in sleep and waking. When the controlled mind rests in
the self alone, free of longing for all desires, then he is called disciplined.
"As a lamp out of the wind does not flicker": that is the likeness remembered
for the yogin whose thought is controlled, who practises the yoga of the self.

"'Thought, held back by the practice of yoga, comes to rest. He sees the self by
the self and is content in the self. He knows that boundless happiness which is
grasped by the understanding and is beyond the senses, and standing there he
does not move from the truth. Having gained it, he thinks no other gain greater,
and standing in it he is not shaken even by heavy sorrow. Let that be known as
yoga, the unyoking from union with sorrow. That yoga should be practised with
resolve and a mind that does not lose heart.

"'He should give up without exception all desires born of purpose and control
the whole troop of the senses on every side with the mind alone. Then he should
slowly, slowly come to rest, with understanding held by steadiness. Making the
mind rest in the self, he should think of nothing at all. Wherever the restless,
unsteady mind wanders, from there he should hold it back and bring it under
control in the self alone. For the highest happiness comes to this yogin whose
mind is at peace, whose passion is calmed, who has become Brahman and is without
fault. Always disciplining himself like this, the yogin free of faults easily
reaches the boundless happiness of contact with Brahman.

"'One whose self is disciplined by yoga, who sees the same everywhere, sees the
self standing in all beings and all beings in the self. One who sees me
everywhere and sees everything in me, I am not lost to him, and he is not lost
to me. The yogin who, standing in oneness, worships me as standing in all
beings, lives in me, however he lives. One who, by comparison with himself, sees
the same everywhere, Arjuna, whether in pleasure or in pain, is thought the
highest yogin.'

---

"'This yoga you have taught as sameness, Madhusudana,' said Arjuna, 'I do not
see how it can stand firm, because the mind is restless. For the mind is
restless, Krishna, turbulent, strong and stubborn. I think it as hard to hold as
the wind.'

"'No doubt, strong-armed one,' said the Blessed One, 'the mind is restless and
hard to hold. But it is held by practice and by detachment, son of Kunti. Yoga
is hard to reach for one whose self is not controlled. That is my view. But it
can be reached by the right means by one who strives and whose self is under
control.'

"'Krishna,' said Arjuna, 'what end does one reach who has faith but does not
strive, whose mind has strayed from yoga, who has not reached perfection in
yoga? Surely he does not perish like a broken cloud, fallen from both, with no
foothold, strong-armed one, confused on the path of Brahman? You should cut away
this doubt of mine completely, Krishna. For no one but you can cut away this
doubt.'

"'Partha,' said the Blessed One, 'there is no ruin for him either here or
hereafter. For no one who does good, my son, goes to a bad end. The man who has
fallen from yoga reaches the worlds of those who have done good and lives there
for endless years. Then he is born in the house of the pure and fortunate. Or he
is born in a family of wise yogins, though such a birth as this is harder to
find in the world. There he regains the union of understanding from his former
body, and strives again from there for perfection, joy of the Kurus. By that
former practice he is carried on even against his will. Even one who only wants
to know yoga goes beyond the Brahman of words. But the yogin who strives with
effort, his faults cleansed, perfected through many births, then reaches the
highest end.

"'The yogin is greater than the ascetics, and thought greater than men of
knowledge, and greater than men of rites. So be a yogin, Arjuna. And of all
yogins, the one who worships me with faith, his inner self gone to me, I think
the most disciplined.'"
