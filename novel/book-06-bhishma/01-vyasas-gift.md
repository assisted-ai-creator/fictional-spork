# Vyasa's Gift

"How did the heroes fight," asked Janamejaya, "the Kurus, the Pandavas and the
Somakas, and the fortunate kings gathered from many lands?"

"Hear, lord of the earth," said Vaishampayana, "how the heroes fought, the
Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas, at Kurukshetra, the field of austerity.

"The Pandavas and the Somakas came down to Kurukshetra and advanced against the
Kauravas, mighty and eager to win. All of them were accomplished in the study of
the Veda, all delighted in war, and all of them hoped for victory in the battle,
or for death facing the enemy. They advanced on the army of Dhritarashtra's son,
which was hard to face, and camped with their soldiers in the western part,
facing east. Outside Samantapanchaka, Kunti's son Yudhishthira had camps built
by the thousand, in due form.

"The whole earth seemed empty. Only children and the old were left. There were
no horses and no men, no chariots and no elephants. As far as the sun warms the
circle of Jambudvipa, so far the army had gathered from, best of kings. People
of every class were in one place, covering a circle many yojanas across, and
they spread over the lands, the rivers, the mountains and the forests. King
Yudhishthira ordered the finest food for all of them and their animals, bull
among men. And Yudhishthira gave them all kinds of watchwords, so that 'one who
says this is to be known as a Pandava's man'. When the time of battle came, the
Kauravya gave them all badges, watchwords and ornaments to know one another by.

"When great-minded Duryodhana, son of Dhritarashtra, saw the tops of the
Parthas' banners, he drew up his army against the Pandavas with all the kings. A
white umbrella was held over his head, and he stood in the midst of a thousand
elephants, surrounded by his brothers. When the Pandava soldiers saw Duryodhana,
they were all glad. They blew great conches and beat drums by the thousand.
Then, seeing their army glad, the Pandavas were glad at heart, and so was mighty
Vasudeva.

"Then, to hearten the warriors, Vasudeva and Dhananjaya, tigers among men,
standing in their chariot, blew their divine conches. When they heard the roar
of Panchajanya and Devadatta, the warriors and their animals let loose dung and
urine. As other beasts tremble when they hear a lion roar, so was the army of
Dhritarashtra's son then. Dust rose from the earth, and nothing could be seen.
The sun vanished, covered by the army's dust. A cloud rained a rain of flesh and
blood there, and drenched all the divisions. It was like a marvel. Then a low
wind rose, dragging gravel along. It struck the divisions and blew away the
dust.

"Then both armies, king, eager for battle, stood ready at Kurukshetra, like
oceans stirred up. The meeting of those two armies was a marvel, like the
meeting of two oceans when the end of the age has come. The whole earth was
empty, with only children and the old left, because of that mass of armies the
Kauravas had brought together.

"Then the Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas made a compact and laid down the
laws of the fighting, bull among the Bharatas. 'When the fighting has stopped,
let there be friendship among us, as before, as is fitting, and let there be no
more deceit. When the fighting is with words, the answer must be with words
only. A man who has left the ranks must never be killed. A chariot fighter must
fight a chariot fighter, and a man on an elephant's neck must fight a man on an
elephant. A horseman must fight a horseman, and a foot soldier a foot soldier,
Bharata. Each must strike as is fitting, by his valour, his strength and his
age, and only after calling out. No one must strike a man who trusts him, or a
man in distress. A man fighting another, a man off his guard, a man who has
turned away, a man whose weapons are gone and a man without armour must never be
killed. No one must ever strike the charioteers, the draught animals, the men
who carry weapons, or those who play the drums and conches.'

"When the Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas had made this compact, they looked
at one another in the greatest wonder. Then those great men, bulls among men,
took up their camps, glad and cheerful, with their soldiers.

"Then the blessed seer Vyasa, son of Satyavati, the best of all who know the
Vedas, watched the twilights of dawn and dusk. He was the grandfather of the
Bharatas, and he sees things as they are, knowing past, present and future. He
saw that the battle would be terrible. So he spoke in private to King
Vaichitravirya, who was grieving and distressed, brooding over his sons' wrong
course.

"'King, your sons and the other kings: their time has come,' said Vyasa. 'They
will meet in battle and kill one another. When they perish, since their time has
come, Bharata, understand that this is the turning of time and do not set your
mind on grief. But if you wish to see this battle, lord of the people, I will
give you sight. Watch this battle.'

"'I do not want to see my kin killed, best of brahmin seers,' said
Dhritarashtra. 'But I would like to hear the whole of this battle, through your
power.'

"Since he did not want to see the battle but wanted to hear it, the lord of
boons gave a boon to Sanjaya.

"'This Sanjaya will tell you of this battle, king,' said Vyasa. 'Nothing in the
battle will be hidden from him. He will have a divine eye, king, and he will
tell you of the battle and know everything. Open or secret, by night or by day,
even what is thought in the mind, Sanjaya will know it all. Weapons will not
pierce him and weariness will not trouble him. This son of Gavalgana will come
out of this battle alive. And I shall spread the fame of these Kurus and of all
the Pandavas, bull among the Bharatas. Do not grieve. This was fated long ago.
You should not grieve over it. Nor can it be checked. Where dharma is, there is
victory.'

"When he had said this, the blessed great-grandfather of the Kurus spoke again
to strong-armed Dhritarashtra. 'In this battle there will be great slaughter,
great king, from the omens I see today, which bode fear. Hawks, vultures, crows
and herons fly down in flocks at the forest's edge and gather together. The
birds look forward with joy to a very terrible battle. The flesh-eaters will eat
the flesh of elephants and horses. Crying "khata, khata" fearfully, foretelling
fear, herons fly across the middle towards the south. At both twilights, dawn
and dusk, Bharata, I always see the sun, at its rising and its setting,
surrounded by headless bodies. Bars of cloud in three colours, edged with white
and red, with black necks and lightning, cover the sun at twilight. I have seen
day and night with sun, moon and stars all blazing, so that day and night were
no different. That will be for their destruction. On the full moon of Karttika
the moon could not be seen. It had lost its light and was the colour of fire, in
a sky of the same colour. Heroic kings will sleep slain, covering the earth,
kings and princes, brave men with arms like iron bars.

"'In the sky at night I always hear the fierce roar of a boar and a cat
fighting. The images of the gods tremble and laugh. They vomit blood from their
mouths, and they sweat and fall. Drums sound without being beaten, lord of the
people, and the great chariots of the kshatriyas move without being yoked.
Cuckoos, woodpeckers, jays, vultures, parrots, cranes and peacocks give fearful
cries. At dawn armoured men are seen on horseback by the hundred, carrying
weapons and ornaments, and swarms of locusts. Both twilights glow as if the
quarters were on fire. There has been a rain of blood and a rain of bones,
Bharata. She who is famous in the three worlds, king, honoured by the good,
Arundhati, has put even Vasishtha behind her. Saturn stands pressing on Rohini,
king, and the mark on the moon has turned aside. There will be great fear. From
a cloudless sky comes terrible thunder, without end. Tears fall from the animals
as they weep.

"'Asses are born of cows. Sons take their pleasure with their mothers. The trees
in the forest bear flowers and fruit out of season. Pregnant women, even
princesses, give birth to monsters, to flesh-eating birds, jackals and other
beasts. Evil animals are born with three horns, four eyes, five feet, two sexual
organs, two heads, two tails and fangs, with gaping mouths, making evil cries.
Crested birds are born with three feet, and Tarkshya birds with four fangs and
horns. And in your city other women, the wives of those who speak of Brahman,
are seen giving birth to Vainateya birds and peacocks. A mare bears a calf, lord
of the earth, and a bitch a jackal. Partridges, mynahs and parrots are born,
with evil voices. Some women give birth to four or five girls, and as soon as
they are born the girls dance and sing and laugh. In the houses of thieves the
babies of common people, still at the breast, dance and sing, telling of great
fear. Others, driven by time, draw figures carrying weapons. Children run at one
another with sticks in their hands, and in their eagerness to fight they build
cities and besiege them. Lotuses and water lilies grow on trees. Fierce winds
blow from every side, and the dust does not settle.

"'The earth trembles again and again, and Rahu has seized the sun. A white
planet has passed Chitra and stands there. It foresees above all the ruin of the
Kurus. A very terrible comet stands pressing on Pushya. This great planet will
do both armies terrible harm. Mars is turning back in the Maghas, and Jupiter is
in Shravana. Bhaga's star is pressed and troubled by the son of the sun. Venus
has climbed into the first Proshthapada, lord of the people, and circling round
in the second, it looks back in company. A dark planet, blazing, smoking and
fiery, stands pressing on Jyeshtha, Indra's bright star. The Pole Star blazes
and turns terribly to the left. A harsh planet stands between Chitra and Svati.
The red-bodied planet, bright as fire, has moved crookedly, turning back and
back again, and stands in Shravana, covering Brahma's stars.

"'The earth is covered with every kind of crop and garlanded with fruit. Barley
grows with five heads and rice with a hundred. The cows, which are first in all
the world and on which the world depends, give blood when their calves suck.
Swords leap from their scabbards, blazing fiercely. Plainly the weapons see the
battle at hand. Weapons, water, armour and banners shine the colour of fire.
There will be great slaughter. On every side beasts and birds with blazing
mouths cry out, showing disaster and telling of great fear. A bird with one
wing, one eye and one foot flies through the sky at night and cries out fiercely
in anger, vomiting blood again and again.

"'Two planets with copper-red crests stand blazing, covering the light of the
noble Seven Seers. The two planets that stay a year in a place, Jupiter and
Saturn, both blazing, stand near the Vishakhas. In the Krittikas, the first of
the stars, a fierce planet blazes, robbing the stars of their light, and stands
there like a comet. Mercury falls again and again into all three of the Purvas,
causing great fear. I have known a fortnight of fourteen days and of fifteen,
and before now of sixteen. But I have never known the new moon to come on the
thirteenth, as it has now. Both moon and sun have been eclipsed in one month, on
the thirteenth. Eclipsed out of season, these two will make an end of the
creatures.

"'All the quarters are covered with dust by showers of dust on every side.
Fierce clouds of ill omen rain blood at night. Again, on the fourteenth of the
dark fortnight, there was a fierce rain of flesh, very terrible, at midnight,
and the rakshasas there could not have enough. Rivers flow backwards, their
waters turned to blood. Wells foam and bellow like bulls. Meteors fall with a
crash of thunder, mixed with dry lightning. This very night, now ended, at
sunrise, the sun was struck on every side by four great blazing meteors. And the
great seers who attend on the sun said there, "The earth will drink the blood of
thousands of kings." From Kailasa, Mandara and the Himalaya, peaks fall by the
thousand with a great noise. In the earthquakes the great beings make each of
the four oceans overflow its shore, stirring them again and again. Fierce winds
blow, dragging gravel along and tearing up trees. Shrine trees fall in villages
and towns.

"'The fire into which the brahmins pour offerings burns yellow, red and blue.
Its flames lean to the left, it smells of corpses, it is mostly smoke and it
crackles harshly. Touch, smell and taste have turned into their opposites, lord
of the earth. The kings' banners smoke, trembling again and again, and drums and
tabors shed showers of coals. On the peaks of palaces and at the city gates
fierce vultures fly round, keeping to the left. Birds cry out loudly, "Ripe,
unripe!", and settle on the tops of the banners, for the destruction of the
kings. Horses and elephants by the thousand brood and tremble and scatter their
hair, and weep in misery. Having heard this, decide now what the time calls for,
Bharata, so that this world is not destroyed.'

"When he heard his father's words, Dhritarashtra said, 'I think this was fated
long ago. It will happen, no doubt of it. If kshatriyas are killed in battle by
the kshatriya's dharma, they will reach the world of heroes and find nothing but
happiness. These tigers among men will give up their lives in the great battle
and win fame here, and great happiness for a long time in the other world.'

"When his son Dhritarashtra had spoken to him so truly, best of kings, the sage,
lord of poets, went into deep meditation. Then the great ascetic, who speaks of
time, spoke again. 'No doubt of it, lord of kings, time destroys the world, and
creates the worlds again. Nothing here lasts for ever. Show the road of dharma
to your kin, the Kurus, your relations and your friends, for you are able to
stop this. They say the killing of kin is a mean thing. Do not do what I
dislike. This is time, born to you in the form of a son, lord of the people.
Killing is not praised in the Veda. It is never good. A man who would destroy
the dharma of his family would destroy his own body. Driven by time, you are
going off the road when you could keep to it. For the ruin of this family and of
the kings, give up this evil in the shape of a kingdom, which brings only
unhappiness. Another has robbed you of your judgement. Show your sons what
dharma is. What is a kingdom to you, invincible one, when it has brought you
sin? If you guard your good name, dharma and fame, you will reach heaven. Let
the Pandavas have the kingdom, and let the Kauravas have peace.'

"While the lord of brahmins was speaking, Dhritarashtra, son of Ambika, who was
skilled in speech, broke in and answered him.

"'As you know, so I know,' said Dhritarashtra. 'Being and not-being are known to
me as they are. People lose their heads over their own interest, sir. Know that
I am like other people. I beg you, you of matchless power. You are our refuge
and our wise guide. They are not under my control, great seer. You should not
lead me into wrong. For you are dharma, purity, fame, renown, steadfastness and
memory. You are honoured by the Kurus and the Pandavas, and you are their
grandfather.'

"'Vaichitravirya, king,' said Vyasa, 'say what is on your mind, as you please. I
will cut away your doubt.'

"'Lord,' said Dhritarashtra, 'I want to hear truly all the signs that are seen
in battle for those who are going to win.'

"'A fire of clear light, with its rays going upwards, its flame turning to the
right and without smoke, and a pure smell rising from the offerings: they say
this is the form of the victory to come,' said Vyasa. 'Where conches and drums
sound deep and loud, and the sun and the moon have clear rays: they say this is
the form of the victory to come. The cries of crows are welcome when they come
from behind those setting out and about to go. The ones behind urge them on,
king, and the ones in front hold them back. Where birds of good voice, royal
swans, parrots, cranes and woodpeckers keep to the right in battle, the brahmins
say victory is certain. Those whose army shines with ornaments, armour and
banners, and with the bright, golden faces of its men, so that it is hard to
look at, conquer their enemies. Where the warriors' voices and spirits are glad,
Bharata, and their garlands do not wither, they overcome their enemies in
battle. A wind is welcome behind a man who has gone in, and to his right as he
is about to go in. From behind it helps his purpose, and in front it hinders.
Where sounds, sights, tastes, touches and smells are pure and clear, and the
warriors are always glad, victory is certain. The winds blow after them, and so
do the clouds and the birds. The clouds follow them, and the rainbows too. These
are the signs of those who are winning, lord of the people. For those about to
die, they are the other way round.

"'In an army small or great, this is certain: the gladness of the warriors is
the one sign of victory. One man who breaks can break a very great army. When he
breaks, even the bravest break after him. Once a great army is broken, it is the
hardest thing to stop, like a great rush of water, or frightened herds of deer.
A great army cannot be put back together in the clash. Thinking "They are
broken", even the bravest break. And seeing the frightened and the broken, fear
grows greater still. When a great army is suddenly broken, king, and scattered
in all directions by the enemy, even heroes cannot make it stand.

"'A wise king who has gathered a great army of four parts should strive first,
always ready, by other means. They say that victory by peaceful means is the
best, and by dividing the enemy the middle one. Victory by battle is the lowest,
lord of the people. A clash of arms does great harm, and so it is called the
maimed way. Men who know one another, glad, free of other ties and resolved:
even fifty such heroes can crush a great army. Or five, six or seven who do not
turn back can win. Vinata's son Garuda does not think much of a great crowd,
Bharata, even when he sees a great gathering of birds. Victory does not come
from the size of an army, Bharata. Victory is never sure. Fate is the last word
in it. Even those who win in battle suffer loss.'"

**Divergences.**

* **The rules of war.** They are agreed by both sides before the battle (6.1.26–32). How they are kept or broken is told as the war goes on.
* **Sanjaya's divine eye.** Vyasa offers sight first to Dhritarashtra, who refuses because he does not want to see his kin killed (6.2.6–7). The eye is then given to Sanjaya, who will know even what is thought in the mind, will not be hurt by weapons and will come out of the battle alive (6.2.9–12). See ledger L-190.
* **"Where dharma is, there is victory"** (6.2.14, *yato dharmas tato jayaḥ*) is Vyasa's word here.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*1–\*19. Among them are the benediction verses (\*1–\*2), a line that the Pandavas stood in the eastern part facing west (\*5), and Vyasa's promise to hold back Yudhishthira and Bhima (\*16).

**Choices.**

The CE's own words are kept: "a white planet" (6.3.11), "a dark planet" (6.3.15), "a harsh planet" (6.3.16), "the red-bodied planet" (6.3.17, *lohitāṅga*), "the son of the sun" (6.3.14, Saturn). *Bhāgyaṃ nakṣatram* (6.3.14) is "Bhaga's star"; *brahmarāśi* (6.3.17) is "Brahma's stars".
