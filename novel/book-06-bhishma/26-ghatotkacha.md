summary: The afternoon of the eighth day. Seeing Iravan dead, Ghatotkacha roars until the earth shakes and the Kaurava soldiers' thighs go stiff with fear. # Ghatotkacha

"When the great chariot fighters, the Parthas, saw Iravan killed," said
Dhritarashtra, "what did they do in the battle? Tell me that, Sanjaya."

"When the rakshasa Ghatotkacha, Bhima's son, saw Iravan killed in the battle,"
said Sanjaya, "he gave a very great roar. At the sound of his roaring the earth,
clothed by the sea, with its mountains and forests, shook violently, king, and
so did the sky and all the quarters and the points between. When your army heard
that very great roar, Bharata, its thighs went stiff, and it trembled and
sweated. All your men, lord of kings, lost heart and writhed like snakes, like
elephants afraid of a lion.

"Roaring a very great roar like a thunderclap, the rakshasa raised a blazing
spear and took on a fearful form. Surrounded by chief rakshasas with terrible
weapons of many kinds, he came on in great anger, like Yama the Ender at the end
of time. Seeing him come on, angry and terrible to see, and seeing his own army
mostly turned away in fear of him, King Duryodhana charged Ghatotkacha. He took
his broad bow and roared again and again like a lion. The king of the Vangas
himself followed him with ten thousand elephants like mountains, streaming with
rut.

"Seeing your son coming on, great king, surrounded by the elephant division, the
night-walker grew angry. Then a tumultuous, hair-raising battle began, lord of
kings, between the rakshasas and Duryodhana's army. Seeing the elephant division
rising like a mass of clouds, the rakshasas charged in anger with weapons in
their hands. They roared many roars, like clouds with lightning, killing the
elephant fighters with arrows, spears, swords and iron arrows. They killed the
great elephants with short javelins, spears, hammers and axes, with mountain
tops and trees. We saw elephants with their temples split, bleeding, their limbs
broken, being killed by the night-walkers, great king.

"As the elephant fighters were destroyed and broken, great king, Duryodhana
charged the rakshasas. Overcome by anger, giving up his own life, the mighty man
loosed sharp arrows at the rakshasas. The great archer, your son Duryodhana, in
anger, killed chief rakshasas there, best of the Bharatas. The great chariot
fighter killed four of them with four arrows: Vegavan, Maharaudra, Vidyujjihva
and Pramathi. Then he whose spirit had no measure loosed a shower of arrows,
hard to withstand, at the army of the night-walkers, best of the Bharatas.

"Seeing that great deed of your son's, sir, mighty Bhimasena's son blazed up
with anger. Stretching his great bow, which sounded like Indra's thunderbolt, he
charged Duryodhana, the tamer of enemies, at speed. Seeing him coming like Death
sent by Time, great king, your son Duryodhana did not flinch. Then the cruel
one, his eyes red, said to him in anger, 'You have been cruel, and kept the
Pandavas in exile for a long time. You beat them, king, at a crooked game of
dice. You brought Draupadi Krishna, in one garment and in her season, into the
hall, evil-minded one, and tormented her in many ways. And the evil-hearted
Saindhava, wanting to please you, scorned my fathers and harassed her while she
was living in the hermitage. Today I will settle these insults and others,
lowest of your family, unless you leave the battle.'

"Having said this, Hidimba's son stretched his great bow. He bit his lip with
his teeth, licked the corners of his mouth and covered Duryodhana with a great
shower of arrows, as a cloud in the rains pours streams on a mountain.

"The lord of kings bore that shower of arrows in the battle, hard even for
danavas to bear, as a great elephant bears the rain. Then your son, bull of the
Bharatas, filled with anger, hissing like a snake, came into very great danger.
He loosed twenty-five sharp, keen iron arrows. They fell suddenly on that bull
among rakshasas, king, like angry venomous snakes on Mount Gandhamadana. Pierced
by them, bleeding like an elephant in rut, the eater of flesh set his mind on
destroying the king. He took up a great spear that could split even mountains,
blazing like a great meteor, like Maghavan's thunderbolt. The strong-armed one
raised it, wanting to kill your son.

"Seeing it raised, the king of the Vangas quickly drove his elephant, like a
mountain, at the rakshasa. On that fine, strong, swift elephant he took the path
in the battle where Duryodhana's chariot was, and shielded your son's chariot
with the elephant. Seeing the way blocked by the wise king of the Vangas,
Ghatotkacha, great king, his eyes red with anger, hurled that raised great spear
at the elephant. Struck by it as it flew from his arm, king, the elephant gushed
blood, fell and died. As the elephant fell, the mighty lord of the Vangas leapt
quickly down and reached the ground.

"Duryodhana, seeing the fine elephant brought down and his army broken, was in
very great pain. Holding to the dharma of kshatriyas and to his own pride, the
king stood like a mountain, though the time to retreat had come. In great anger
he fitted a sharp arrow, bright as the fire of Time, and loosed it at that
terrible night-walker. Seeing that arrow coming, bright as Indra's thunderbolt,
huge Ghatotkacha dodged it with his speed. Fierce, his eyes red with anger, he
roared again, terrifying all creatures like a cloud at the end of an age.

"Hearing that terrible roar from the fearful rakshasa, Bhishma, son of Shantanu,
went to the teacher and said, 'From this terrible roar that we hear from the
rakshasa, Hidimba's son must be fighting King Duryodhana. No being can beat him
in battle. Go there, bless you, and guard the king. The fortunate man has been
attacked by the evil-hearted rakshasa. This is the highest duty for all of us,
scorchers of enemies.'

"Hearing the grandfather's words, the great chariot fighters went quickly, at
their best speed, to where the Kaurava was. Drona, Somadatta, Bahlika and
Jayadratha went, and Kripa, Bhurishravas, Shalya, Chitrasena and Vivimshati,
Ashvatthama, Vikarna, the prince of Avanti and Brihadbala. The many thousands of
chariots that followed them went too, wanting to rescue your son Duryodhana, who
had been attacked. Seeing that unassailable division, guarded by the best men in
the world, coming at him to kill him, the strong-armed best of rakshasas did not
tremble, like Mount Mainaka. He took his broad bow, surrounded by his kin, who
held spears, hammers and weapons of many kinds.

"Then a tumultuous, hair-raising battle began between the chief of the rakshasas
and Duryodhana's army. The sound of twanging bows was heard on every side, great
king, tumultuous, like bamboos burning. The sound of weapons falling on the
armour of men's bodies was like mountains splitting, king. The javelins hurled
from heroes' arms, lord of the people, looked like snakes gliding in the sky.

"Then the strong-armed lord of rakshasas, in very great anger, stretched his
huge bow, roaring a fearful roar. In anger he cut the teacher's bow with a
crescent-headed arrow. He struck down Somadatta's banner with a broad arrow, and
roared. He pierced Bahlika in the middle of the chest with three arrows. He
pierced Kripa with one, and Chitrasena with three. With an arrow drawn full and
well aimed he struck Vikarna at the collarbone, and Vikarna sank down on the
floor of his chariot, drenched in blood. Then he whose spirit had no measure, in
anger, sent fifteen iron arrows at Bhurishravas, bull of the Bharatas. They
split his armour and went quickly into the ground. He struck the charioteers of
Vivimshati and of Drona's son, and they fell on the floors of their chariots,
letting go of the horses' reins. With a crescent-headed arrow he struck down the
Sindhu king's boar banner, adorned with gold, great king, and with a second he
cut his bow. His eyes red with anger, he killed the four horses of the great man
of Avanti with four iron arrows. With a yellow, sharp arrow drawn full he split
Prince Brihadbala, great king, and he sank down on the floor of his chariot,
badly wounded and in pain. Filled with great anger, standing in his chariot, the
lord of rakshasas shot sharp, keen arrows like venomous snakes. They split
Shalya, great king, skilled as he was in battle.

"Having turned all your men away in the battle, the rakshasa charged Duryodhana,
wanting to kill him, best of the Bharatas. Seeing him charging the king at
speed, your men, mad in battle, ran at him, wanting to kill him. Drawing bows as
tall as palm trees, the mighty men charged that one man, roaring like a pride of
lions. They surrounded him on every side with a shower of arrows, as clouds in
autumn cover a mountain with streams. Badly wounded, in pain like an elephant
pricked with goads, he flew up into the sky like the son of Vinata. He roared a
very great roar, like an autumn cloud, making the quarters, the sky and the
points between ring with his fearful voice.

"King Yudhishthira, best of the Bharatas, heard that roar from the rakshasa. He
said to Bhimasena, 'The rakshasa must be fighting the great chariot fighters,
the sons of Dhritarashtra, from the way we hear his roar, that fearful roaring.
And I see too great a load laid on him there, my son. And the grandfather, in
anger, is set on killing the Panchalas, and Phalguna is fighting the enemy to
protect them. Having heard this, strong-armed one, you see there are two things
to be done. Go and protect Hidimba's son, who has come into the greatest
danger.'

"Heeding his brother's words, Vrikodara went quickly, terrifying all the kings
with his lion roar, at great speed, king, like the sea at the full moon. After
him went Satyadhriti and Saucitti, mad in battle, Shreniman and Vasudana, and
the mighty son of the king of Kashi. The great chariot fighters led by Abhimanyu
went, the sons of Draupadi, brave Kshatradeva and Kshatradharma, and Nila, lord
of Anupa, with his own army. With a great line of chariots they surrounded
Hidimba's son. With six thousand elephants, always in rut, that struck hard,
they guarded Ghatotkacha, lord of rakshasas, together. With their great lion
roars, the rumble of their wheels and the sound of their hooves, they made the
earth shake.

"Hearing the noise of their coming, your army, in dread of Bhimasena, its faces
pale, turned back, great king, and left Ghatotkacha. Then battle began here and
there between the great men, yours and theirs, who never turned back in battle.
Loosing weapons of many kinds, the great chariot fighters charged each other and
fought, a tangled, very cruel battle that terrified the timid. Horses met
elephants, foot soldiers met chariot fighters, king, seeking great glory against
each other in the battle.

"Suddenly, from the clash, a thick, great dust rose, stirred up by the feet and
wheels of chariots, horses, elephants and foot soldiers. The thick dust, smoky
red, covered the field. Neither our men nor theirs knew one another, king.
Father did not know son, nor son father, in that lawless, hair-raising
slaughter. The noise of weapons and of roaring men was very great, best of the
Bharatas, like bamboos burning. A river began to flow there of the blood of
elephants, horses and men, with entrails for waves and hair for weed and grass.
From the heads of men falling from their bodies in the battle a very great noise
was heard, like falling stones. The earth was strewn with headless men,
elephants with their limbs cut and horses with their bodies split.

"Loosing weapons of many kinds, the great chariot fighters charged each other
and fought. Horses driven by their riders met horses, struck each other in the
battle and fell lifeless. Men met men, their eyes red with anger, grappled chest
to chest and killed each other. Elephants, enemies of elephants, driven by their
riders, struck elephants in the battle with the points of their tusks. Streaming
blood, adorned with flags, locked together, they looked like clouds with
lightning. Some, split by the points of tusks, their temples split by javelins,
ran trumpeting like thundering clouds. Some fell in that tumult with their
trunks cut in two, others with their limbs cut, like mountains with their wings
cut off. Other fine elephants, their sides torn open by elephants, poured out
blood as mountains pour out red ores. Others, struck by iron arrows and pierced
by javelins, their riders killed, looked like mountains without their peaks.
Some, filled with anger, blind with rut, out of control, crushed chariots,
horses and foot soldiers by the hundred in the battle. In the same way horses,
struck by horsemen with lances and javelins, ran this way and that, throwing the
quarters into confusion.

"Chariot fighters, sons of good families, ready to give up their bodies, fought
chariot fighters, using their greatest strength and doing deeds without fear. In
that clash, as at a bridegroom choice, the men who gloried in battle struck at
each other, seeking glory, king, or heaven. As that hair-raising battle went on,
the great army of the sons of Dhritarashtra was mostly turned away."

**Divergences.**

* **Ghatotkacha's list of wrongs.** He names the exile, the crooked dice, Draupadi brought "in one garment and in her season" into the hall, and Jayadratha's attack on her in the hermitage (6.87.25–28; see Book 2 and Book 3). The rejected \*348 adds "Today I will pay my debt to my fathers and my mother."
* **The king of the Vangas.** He saves Duryodhana by driving his elephant into the path of Ghatotkacha's spear. The elephant dies, and the king leaps clear (6.88.6–10). He is not named.
* **Duryodhana stands.** "Holding to the dharma of kshatriyas and to his own pride", he stands his ground "though the time to retreat had come" (6.88.12). The rejected \*350 says he was "unable to fight back".
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*347–\*352.

**Choices.**

* 6.87.20: the four rakshasas are named as the text names them: Vegavan, Maharaudra, Vidyujjihva and Pramathi.
* 6.88.22 *āvantyaś ca bṛhadbalaḥ*: "the prince of Avanti and Brihadbala", two men, since at 6.88.36–37 "the great man of Avanti" and "Prince Brihadbala" are struck separately.
* 6.88.23 *maināka*: "Mount Mainaka".
* 6.89.3 *tālamātrāṇi cāpāni*: "bows as tall as palm trees".

* 6.89.40 *svayaṃvara ivāmarde*: "in that clash, as at a bridegroom choice".
