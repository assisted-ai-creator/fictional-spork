summary: The night of the ninth day. Yudhishthira, crushed by Bhishma's slaughter, talks of going back to the forest. Krishna offers to kill Bhishma himself if Arjuna will not. # How Bhishma Can Be Killed

"As they were fighting, the sun set," said Sanjaya, "and a terrible twilight
came on, and we could no longer see the battle. Then King Yudhishthira saw the
twilight, Bharata. He saw his army being killed by Bhishma, killer of enemies,
throwing away its weapons, turning back, bent on flight. He saw Bhishma in the
battle, in his fury, pursuing the great chariot fighters, and the great chariot
fighters of the Somakas beaten and without heart. He thought for a long time,
pondered, and chose to withdraw. Then King Yudhishthira withdrew the armies, and
in the same way your armies withdrew too. When they had withdrawn the armies,
the great chariot fighters settled there, best of the Kurus, wounded all over in
the battle.

"Thinking of Bhishma's deeds in the battle, the Pandavas found no peace, hard
pressed by Bhishma as they were. And Bhishma, having beaten the Pandavas with
the Srinjayas in the battle, honoured and saluted by your sons, Bharata, settled
with the glad Kurus all about him. Then night came on, which bewilders all
creatures.

"At the terrible mouth of the night the Pandavas with the Vrishnis and the
unassailable Srinjayas sat down to take counsel. The mighty men, skilled in
deciding on counsel, calmly considered what was best for them now that the time
had come. Then King Yudhishthira, having taken counsel for a long time, king,
looked at Vasudeva and said this.

"'Look, Krishna, at great Bhishma of terrible valour, crushing my army as an
elephant crushes reed beds. We cannot even bear to look at the great man as he
licks up the armies like a growing fire. Like the terrible great serpent
Takshaka, swollen with poison, so is Bhishma in battle, Krishna, fierce, with
his sharp weapons. With his bow in hand, loosing sharp arrows in battle, he
cannot be beaten. Yama in his anger can be beaten, and the king of the gods with
the thunderbolt in his hand. So can Varuna with his noose, or the lord of wealth
with his mace. But Bhishma, in his great anger, cannot be beaten in the great
battle.

"'So, Krishna, as things are, I am sunk in an ocean of sorrow, because of the
weakness of my own mind, having come up against Bhishma in battle. I will go to
the forest, unassailable one. It would be better for me to go there. I have no
taste for war, Krishna, for Bhishma is always killing us. As a moth running at a
blazing fire goes to death alone, so I have come against Bhishma. I have been
brought to ruin for the sake of a kingdom, Varshneya, for all my valour. And my
brave brothers are hard pressed by arrows. For my sake, out of love for their
brother, they lost their kingdom, and for my sake Krishnaa was tormented,
Madhusudana. I think much of life, for life is hard to keep today. With what is
left of my life I will practise the highest dharma. If you mean to show me and
my brothers favour, Keshava, tell me what is best without going against my own
dharma, Keshava.'

"Hearing these many words of his, Krishna answered out of compassion, comforting
Yudhishthira. 'Son of Dharma, do not despair, you who are true to your word.
Your brothers are brave, hard to beat and killers of enemies: Arjuna and
Bhimasena, as fiery as the wind and fire, and the valiant sons of Madri, like
the lords of the thirty gods. Or set me to it, out of friendship, and I will
fight Bhishma, Pandava. Set on by you, king, what would I not do in the great
battle? I will challenge Bhishma, the bull among men, and kill him in battle
while the sons of Dhritarashtra watch, if Phalguna does not want to. If you see
victory for yourself, king, in Bhishma's death, I will kill the old grandfather
of the Kurus today with a single chariot.

"'Watch my valour in battle, king, like great Indra's. I will bring him down
from his chariot as he looses his great weapons. Whoever is the enemy of the
sons of Pandu is my enemy, no doubt of it. Those who are for me are for you, and
those who are mine are yours. Your brother is my friend, my kinsman and my
pupil. I would cut off my own flesh and give it for Arjuna's sake, lord of the
earth. And this tiger among men would give up his life for me. This is our
agreement, my friend: we will each bring the other through. So set me to it,
lord of kings, and I will be your island.

"'Partha promised long ago at Upaplavya, in Uluka's presence, "I will have
Ganga's son killed." I must keep that word of wise Partha's. If Partha gives me
leave, I must do it, no doubt of it. Or else this is Phalguna's burden in the
battle, a measured one. He will kill Bhishma, conqueror of enemy cities, in the
battle. For Partha, once he rises to it, can do the impossible in battle. Arjuna
could kill the thirty gods in battle, joined with the daityas and danavas, lord
of men, let alone Bhishma. Bhishma, son of Shantanu, has turned the wrong way.
His courage is gone, his life is short, and surely he does not know what he
should do.'

"'It is as you say, strong-armed Madhava,' said Yudhishthira. 'All of them
together are not enough to hold back your force. I will surely get all I wish,
tiger among men, since I have you, mighty one, for my protector. With you for my
protector, Govinda, I could beat even the gods with Indra in battle, best of
victors, let alone Bhishma in the great battle. But I cannot make you false for
the sake of my own advantage. Help me without fighting, Madhava, as you said you
would.

"'Bhishma made an agreement with me, Madhava: "I will give you counsel for your
good, but I will not fight for you in any way. I will fight for Duryodhana. That
is the truth, lord." He is the giver of the kingdom to me, Madhava, and of
counsel. So let us all go with you, Madhusudana, and ask Devavrata again for the
means of his own death. Let us go together quickly to Bhishma, the best of men,
if it pleases you, Varshneya, and ask the Kaurava for counsel. He will tell us
words that are good and true, Janardana. As he tells it, Krishna, so I will do
it in the battle. He is the giver of victory and of counsel to us, that man firm
in his vows. He brought us up when we were children and had lost our father. And
I want to kill that old grandfather, Madhava, my father's own father, whom I
love. Curse the kshatriya's way of life!'

"Then, great king, the Varshneya said to the joy of the Kurus, 'I always like
what you say, strong-armed one. Devavrata Bhishma is skilled. He could burn a
man up with a look. Let us go to the son of the ocean-going river and ask him
the means of his death. He is bound to tell the truth, above all when you ask
him. Let us go there to ask the grandfather of the Kurus. We will bow our heads
to him and ask for counsel, Madhava. Whatever counsel he gives us, with it we
will fight the enemy.'

"When they had agreed on this, elder brother of Pandu, the heroic Pandavas and
mighty Vasudeva went together to Bhishma's tent, with their weapons and armour
put off. When they had gone in, great king, the Pandavas bowed their heads to
Bhishma and honoured him, bull of the Bharatas. Bowing their heads to Bhishma,
they went to him for refuge.

"Strong-armed Bhishma, grandfather of the Kurus, said to them, 'Welcome,
Varshneya. Welcome, Dhananjaya. Welcome to the son of Dharma, to Bhima and to
the twins. What can I do for you today to increase your happiness? I will do it
with my whole self, even if it is very hard to do.'

"When Ganga's son spoke like that, full of affection, again and again, the son
of Dharma, Yudhishthira, spoke with a heavy heart. 'You know dharma. How can we
win? How can we gain the kingdom? How can the destruction of the people be
stopped? Tell me that, lord. You yourself should tell us the means of your
death. How can we bear you in battle, king? There is not the smallest opening in
you, grandfather of the Kurus. In battle you are always seen with your bow drawn
to a circle. We never see you taking an arrow, fitting it or drawing your bow,
strong-armed one. You stand in your chariot like the sun. Killer of enemy
heroes, you kill men, horses, chariots and elephants. What man could dare to
kill you, bull of the Bharatas? Raining great showers of arrows, best of men,
you have brought my great army to destruction. Tell me, grandfather, how I may
beat you in battle, how the kingdom may be mine, or how my army may have peace.'

"Then Shantanu's son said to the Pandavas, elder brother of Pandu, 'While I
live, son of Kunti, there will be no victory for you in battle in any way. I
tell you the truth. But once I am beaten in battle, you will surely beat the
Kauravas. Strike me quickly, if you want victory in battle. I give you leave,
Parthas. Strike as you please. I think this is the right thing, for you know me.
When I am killed, all is killed. So let it be done this way.'

"'Tell us, then, the means by which we may win in battle,' said Yudhishthira,
'against you, angry in battle like Death with his staff in his hand. The Wielder
of the Thunderbolt can be beaten, and Varuna and Yama too. But you cannot be
beaten in battle even by the gods and asuras with Indra.'

"'It is as you say, strong-armed Pandava,' said Bhishma. 'I cannot be beaten in
battle even by the gods and asuras with Indra, when I have my weapons in hand,
am on my guard in battle and hold my fine bow. But when I have laid down my
weapons, king, the great chariot fighters could kill me in battle. I do not like
to fight one who has laid down his weapons or fallen, one whose armour and
banner have gone, or one who is running away, or afraid, or says, "I am yours".
Nor do I like to fight a woman, one with a woman's name, a cripple, a man with
only one son, a man with no son, or one who is wretched to look at.

"'Hear this too, Partha, a resolve I made long ago. If I see an inauspicious
banner, I will not fight at all. This son of Drupada in your army, king, the
great chariot fighter Shikhandi, eager for battle, brave, a winner of battles,
was a woman before and later became a man. You all know it just as it happened.
Let heroic Arjuna, in his armour, put Shikhandi in front in the battle and come
at me quickly with arrows. Where the banner is inauspicious, and above all where
the man was once a woman, I will never want to strike, even with an arrow in my
hand. Let Dhananjaya the Pandava take that opening and quickly have me killed
with arrows on every side, bull of the Bharatas. I see no one in the worlds who
could kill me when I am ready to fight, except Krishna, the fortunate one, or
Dhananjaya the Pandava. So let Bibhatsu put someone else in front of me and
bring me down. Then you will have the victory. Do this, son of Kunti, as I have
told you. Then you will beat the sons of Dhritarashtra gathered in the battle.'

"Given leave, the Parthas greeted great Bhishma, grandfather of the Kurus, and
went to their camp. Ganga's son had spoken like this, dedicated to the other
world. Arjuna, burning with grief, said in shame, 'How can I fight the
grandfather in battle, Madhava, my elder, the eldest of my family, wise and of
perfect understanding? When I was a child, Vasudeva, playing, my body grimy with
dust, I soiled that great man. I climbed on to his lap as a boy, elder brother
of Gada, and called him "Father", the father of my great father Pandu. "I am not
your father, I am your father's father, Bharata," he said to me when I was a
child. How can I kill him? Let him kill my army if he likes. I will not fight
that great man, whether it brings me victory or death. What do you think,
Krishna?'

"'You promised long ago to kill Bhishma in battle, Jishnu,' said Krishna.
'Standing by the dharma of kshatriyas, Partha, how can you not kill him? Bring
him down from his chariot, Partha, like a tree struck by a thunderbolt. Without
killing Ganga's son in battle, you will have no victory. This was fixed long ago
by the gods, and it will happen whether you will or not: "The killer of Bhishma
is Indra's son." It is so and not otherwise. No one but you could kill
unassailable Bhishma, who is like Death with his mouth open, not even the
Wielder of the Thunderbolt himself. Kill Bhishma, strong-armed one, and hear
these words of mine, which wise Brihaspati spoke to Shakra long ago. "Shakra, if
even an elder, even one full of virtues, comes at you as an assassin, you may
call on him and kill him as a killer." This is the eternal, settled dharma of
kshatriyas, Dhananjaya: to fight, to protect and to sacrifice, without envy.'

"'Shikhandi will surely be Bhishma's death, Krishna,' said Arjuna. 'For whenever
Bhishma sees the Panchala, he always turns back. So we will put Shikhandi in
front of him and bring Ganga's son down by this means. That is my thought. I
will hold back the other great archers with my arrows. Let Shikhandi, the best
of fighters, go against Bhishma himself. You heard the chief of the Kurus say,
"I will not kill Shikhandi. He was born a girl long ago, and became a man."'

"Having decided on this," said Sanjaya, "the Pandavas with Madhava went, those
bulls among men, each to his own bed."

**Divergences.**

* **Bhishma tells them himself.** The Pandavas and Krishna go unarmed to Bhishma's tent, and Bhishma tells them how he can be brought down: Arjuna should put Shikhandi in front and shoot from behind him (6.103.65–82). The plan to use Shikhandi is Bhishma's own, and Arjuna repeats it (6.103.97–100). See ledger L-185.
* **"I am your father's father."** Arjuna remembers sitting in Bhishma's lap as a boy and calling him "Father", and Bhishma saying, "I am not your father, I am your father's father" (6.103.86–88). See ledger L-197.
* **Bhishma's list.** Those he will not fight include "a woman, one with a woman's name, a cripple, a man with only one son, a man with no son, or one who is wretched to look at", and anyone under "an inauspicious banner" (6.103.72–74). The rejected \*429 adds men "ruled by women".
* **Krishna's offer.** Krishna offers to kill Bhishma himself, but Yudhishthira will not let him break his word not to fight (6.103.28–43).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*423–\*434. \*426 has Bhishma embrace the Pandavas and take them on his lap in tears.
