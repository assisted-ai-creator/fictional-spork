# Arjuna's Despair

"Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "my sons and the sons of Pandu met on the field
of dharma, the field of the Kurus,[^b6c7-1] ready to fight. What did they do?"

"When King Duryodhana saw the Pandava army lined up for battle," said Sanjaya,
"he went to his teacher, Drona, and spoke to him.

"'Teacher, look at the huge army of the sons of Pandu. Your own clever pupil,
Drupada's son, has set it out. Here are heroes and great archers, as good in
battle as Bhima and Arjuna. There is Yuyudhana, and Virata, and Drupada, a great
chariot fighter. There are Dhrishtaketu, Chekitana and the brave king of Kashi,
Purujit, Kuntibhoja and Shaibya, a bull among men. There are brave Yudhamanyu
and strong Uttamaujas, Subhadra's son and the sons of Draupadi. All of them are
great chariot fighters.

"'Now, best of brahmins, know the best men on our side, the leaders of my army.
I will name them for you. There is you yourself, and Bhishma, and Karna, and
Kripa, who wins his battles. There are Ashvatthama, Vikarna and Somadatta's son.
And many other heroes are ready to die for me. They carry all kinds of weapons,
and all of them know war well. Our army, guarded by Bhishma, has no measure.
Their army, guarded by Bhima, can be measured. So all of you, stand in your
places at every opening in the lines, and above all, guard Bhishma.'

"Then old Bhishma, the grandfather of the Kurus, gave a loud lion's roar and
blew his conch, to cheer Duryodhana. At once conches and drums, tabors,
kettledrums and horns all sounded together. The noise was huge.

"Then Krishna and Arjuna, standing in their great chariot pulled by white
horses, blew their divine conches. Krishna blew his conch, Panchajanya. Arjuna
blew Devadatta. Bhima, who does terrible deeds, blew his great conch, Paundra.
King Yudhishthira blew Anantavijaya. Nakula and Sahadeva blew Sughosha and
Manipushpaka. The king of Kashi, a very fine archer, blew his conch, and so did
Shikhandi, the great chariot fighter. Dhrishtadyumna, Virata and Satyaki, who
had never been beaten, blew theirs. Drupada, the sons of Draupadi and
strong-armed Subhadra's son all blew their own conches. That roar filled the sky
and the earth, and it tore at the hearts of Dhritarashtra's sons.

"Arjuna, whose banner carries a monkey, looked at Dhritarashtra's sons standing
in their lines. The arrows were about to fly. He lifted his bow and spoke to
Krishna.

"'Krishna, drive my chariot into the space between the two armies. I want to
look at these men who are standing here, eager for battle. I want to see who I
have to fight. I want to see the men who have come here to please
Dhritarashtra's wicked son in this war.'

"So Krishna drove that fine chariot into the middle, between the two armies, in
front of Bhishma and Drona and all the kings. 'Arjuna,' he said, 'look at all
the Kurus gathered here.'

"Arjuna looked. On both sides he saw fathers and grandfathers, teachers and
uncles. He saw brothers, sons, grandsons and friends, fathers-in-law and dear
companions.[^b6c7-2] When he saw all his own family standing there, a deep pity
came over him, and in his sorrow he spoke.

"'Krishna, when I see my own people standing here, ready to fight, my legs go
weak and my mouth goes dry. My body shakes and my hair stands on end. My bow,
the Gandiva, is slipping from my hand, and my skin is burning. I cannot stand
steady. My mind is spinning. I see bad omens, Krishna. I cannot see any good in
killing my own family in battle.

"'I do not want to win, Krishna. I do not want a kingdom, or pleasures. What use
is a kingdom to us, or enjoyment, or even life? The very people we wanted the
kingdom for, and the pleasures and the comforts, are standing here in battle,
ready to lose their lives and their wealth. They are our teachers, fathers, sons
and grandfathers, uncles, fathers-in-law, grandsons, brothers-in-law and other
relations. I do not want to kill them, even if they kill me. I would not do it
even to rule the three worlds, let alone this earth. What happiness could we
have if we killed Dhritarashtra's sons? We would only be stained with sin for
killing them, even though they have wronged us and would kill us. So we must not
kill Dhritarashtra's sons. They are our own family. How could we be happy after
killing our own people?

"'Greed has blinded them. They cannot see the harm in destroying a family, or
the crime in betraying friends. But we can see that harm, Krishna. So why should
we not know enough to turn away from this wrong? When a family is destroyed, its
age-old ways are lost. When those ways are lost, lawlessness takes over the
whole family. When lawlessness takes over, the women of the family go astray.
When the women go astray, the classes get mixed.[^b6c7-3] That mixing drags both
the family and those who destroyed it down to hell. Their ancestors fall too. No
one is left to give them the offerings of rice and water.[^b6c7-4] Through these
wrongs, the ways of the classes and the ways of the family are wiped out for
ever. We have heard it said, Krishna, that people whose family ways are wiped
out must live in hell.

"'Oh, what a terrible wrong we are about to do! Out of greed for a kingdom and
its pleasures, we are ready to kill our own people. It would be better for me if
Dhritarashtra's sons came at me with weapons and killed me in battle, while I
stood unarmed and did not fight back.'

"When he had said this, there in the middle of the battlefield, Arjuna sat down
in his chariot. He let his bow and arrows fall. His heart was full of grief.

"Arjuna sat there, full of pity and sorrow, his eyes filled with tears. Then
Krishna spoke to him.

"'Where has this weakness come from, Arjuna, at a time like this?' said Krishna.
'Noble people do not act like this. It does not lead to heaven. It only brings
shame. Do not be a coward, Arjuna. It does not suit you. Throw off this small
weakness of heart and stand up.'

"'Krishna,' said Arjuna, 'how can I shoot arrows at Bhishma and Drona in battle?
I should honour them.[^b6c7-5] It would be better to live in this world by
begging than to kill my teachers, who are great men. Even if they have taken the
enemy's side for the sake of wealth, if I kill them, every pleasure I enjoy here
will be stained with their blood. And we do not even know which is better for
us: to win, or for them to win. Dhritarashtra's sons stand in front of us, and
if we killed them, we would not want to go on living.

"'My mind is weak with pity, and I am confused about what my dharma is.[^b6c7-6]
So I ask you: tell me clearly what is best. I am your pupil. I have come to you
for help. Teach me. Even if I won a rich kingdom on earth with no rival, or even
rule over the gods, I cannot see anything that would take away this grief. It is
drying up all my senses.'"

"When Arjuna had said this to Krishna," said Sanjaya, "he said, 'I will not
fight,' and fell silent. There, between the two armies, Krishna, almost smiling,
spoke to him in his sorrow.

"'You are grieving for people you should not grieve for,' said Krishna, 'and yet
you talk like a wise man. The wise do not grieve for the dead, or for the
living. There was never a time when I did not exist, or you, or these kings. And
there will never be a time when any of us stops existing.

"'Inside this body lives the self.[^b6c7-7] It passes through childhood, youth
and old age in this body. In the same way, it passes into another body.[^b6c7-8]
A steady person is not troubled by this. When the senses meet the things of the
world, they bring cold and heat, pleasure and pain. These come and go. They do
not last. Put up with them, Arjuna. The person who is not shaken by them, who
stays the same in pain and in pleasure, and stays steady, is ready for the life
that does not die.

"'What is not real never comes to be. What is real never stops being. People who
see the truth have seen where the line between the two lies. Know that the thing
that fills this whole world can never be destroyed. No one can destroy what
cannot perish. These bodies come to an end. But the one who lives in the body is
eternal. It cannot be destroyed, and it cannot be measured. So fight, Arjuna.

"'If someone thinks the self kills, or thinks the self is killed, he does not
understand. The self does not kill, and it is not killed. It is never born, and
it never dies. It did not come into being, and it will not come into being
again. It is unborn, eternal, always there, and very old. When the body is
killed, it is not killed. If a person knows that the self cannot be destroyed,
that it is eternal, unborn and never changes, how can that person kill anyone,
or make anyone kill?

"'A person throws away old, worn-out clothes and puts on new ones. In the same
way, the self leaves worn-out bodies and goes into new ones. Weapons cannot cut
it. Fire cannot burn it. Water cannot wet it. Wind cannot dry it. It cannot be
cut or burned, wetted or dried. It is eternal. It is everywhere. It is fixed and
does not move. It is for ever. It cannot be seen. It cannot be imagined. It
never changes. That is what they say of it. So, now that you know this, you
should not grieve.

"'And even if you think the self is born again and again and dies again and
again, you still should not grieve for it. Whoever is born is sure to die, and
whoever dies is sure to be born. So you should not grieve over what cannot be
avoided. Beings cannot be seen before they are born. They can be seen in the
middle, while they live. And they cannot be seen again after they die. So what
is there to cry about?

"'One person looks at the self as a wonder. Another speaks of it as a wonder.
Another hears of it as a wonder. And even after hearing about it, no one really
knows it. The self in everyone's body is eternal. It can never be killed. So you
should not grieve for any living being.

"'Think, too, of your own dharma, and do not waver. For a kshatriya, nothing is
better than a just war.[^b6c7-9] Lucky are the kshatriyas who get a battle like
this. It has come to you by itself, like an open door to heaven. But if you will
not fight this just war, you will throw away your own dharma and your good name,
and you will do wrong. People will talk about your shame for ever. For a man who
has been honoured, shame is worse than death. The great chariot fighters will
think you ran from the battle out of fear. Those who thought so highly of you
will think little of you. Your enemies will say things that should never be
said, and mock your strength. What could hurt more than that? If you are killed,
you will go to heaven. If you win, you will enjoy the earth. So stand up,
Arjuna, and make up your mind to fight. Treat pleasure and pain the same, gain
and loss the same, winning and losing the same. Then get ready for battle. In
this way you will do no wrong.

"'So far I have taught you this understanding in the way of Sankhya, the way of
knowledge. Now hear it in the way of yoga, the way of discipline.[^b6c7-10] If
you hold to this understanding, Arjuna, you will break the chains of
action.[^b6c7-11] On this path, no effort is ever wasted, and it does no harm.
Even a little of it saves a person from great fear. On this path the mind that
has made up its mind is single. But the minds of those who have not made up
their minds go off in many directions, without end.

"'There are some unwise people who make pretty, flowery speeches. They love the
words of the Veda and say there is nothing else.[^b6c7-12] They are full of
wanting, and heaven is their goal. Their speech promises a good rebirth as the
reward for rituals. It is full of special ceremonies for getting pleasure and
power. People who cling to pleasure and power have their minds carried off by
such words. Their minds are never steady and settled.

"'The Vedas deal with the three strands of nature.[^b6c7-13] Rise above the
three strands, Arjuna. Rise above the pairs of opposites. Always hold to what is
true. Do not worry about getting and keeping things. Be master of yourself. When
water floods everywhere, a small well is of little use. In the same way, all the
Vedas are of little use to a wise brahmin who knows.

"'Your right is to the work alone, never to its results. Do not work for the
sake of the result. And do not become attached to doing nothing.[^b6c7-14] Stay
steady in yoga and do your work, Arjuna, without clinging. Stay the same whether
you succeed or fail. This sameness of mind is called yoga. Work done for a
result is far below work done with a steady mind, Arjuna. Take shelter in that
steady mind. Pitiful are the people who work only for results. A person with a
steady mind lets go of both good and bad deeds, here and now. So give yourself
to yoga. Yoga is skill in action. Wise people with a steady mind give up the
fruit of their work. They are set free from the chain of birth, and they go to
the place where there is no suffering. When your mind has got through the thick
forest of confusion, you will no longer care about what you have heard or what
you have still to hear. Your mind is confused now by all the things you have
been told. When it stands still and steady, fixed in deep focus, you will have
reached yoga.'

"'Krishna,' said Arjuna, 'how would you describe a person of steady wisdom,
whose mind is fixed and still? How does such a person speak? How do they sit?
How do they walk?'

"'Arjuna,' said Krishna, 'a person may give up all the wants in the mind, and be
content in the self, by the self. Then that person is called a person of steady
wisdom.[^b6c7-15] When their mind is not upset by trouble, when they do not
crave pleasure, and when longing, fear and anger have gone from them, they are
called a sage whose mind is steady. When they are not attached to anything, and
do not jump for joy at good things or hate bad things, their wisdom is firm.
When they can pull their senses back from the things of the senses, as a
tortoise pulls in its legs, their wisdom is firm.

"'If a person stops feeding the senses, the things of the senses fall away. But
the taste for them stays. Even that taste goes, once they have seen the highest.

"'The senses are wild, Arjuna. They can drag away the mind even of a wise person
who is trying hard. So a person should hold them all in check, sit with a steady
mind, and keep me as their goal. When a person's senses are under control, their
wisdom is firm.

"'When a person keeps thinking about the things of the senses, they grow
attached to them. From attachment comes wanting. From wanting comes anger. From
anger comes confusion. From confusion, memory slips. When memory slips, good
sense is lost. And when good sense is lost, the person is ruined.[^b6c7-16]

"'But a person with self-control can move among the things of the senses with
the senses under control, free from liking and disliking. That person finds
peace. In that peace, all their sorrows come to an end. When the mind is at
peace, good sense soon stands firm. A person who has no discipline has no good
sense, and no power to reflect. Without reflection there is no peace. And
without peace, how can there be happiness? When the mind runs after the
wandering senses, it carries a person's wisdom away, as the wind carries away a
boat on the water. So, Arjuna, the person who holds back the senses from their
objects on every side has firm wisdom.

"'What is night for all other beings, the self-controlled person is awake to.
What all other beings are awake to is night for the sage who truly sees. Rivers
keep pouring into the ocean, yet the ocean stays full and still within its
shores. In the same way, desires may flow into a person, and yet that person
finds peace. The one who chases after desires does not. The person who gives up
all desires and lives without craving, without "mine" and without "I", finds
peace. This is the state of Brahman, Arjuna.[^b6c7-17] Whoever reaches it is
never confused again. Anyone who stays in it, even at the moment of death,
reaches nirvana in Brahman.'"

## Explanations

[^b6c7-1]: **The field of dharma, the field of the Kurus.** *What it means:*
    Kurukshetra, "the field of the Kurus", the plain where the war is fought. It
    is called the field of dharma because it was a holy place. *In the story:*
    the epic's opening tells how Rama Jamadagnya once filled five lakes there
    with the blood of kshatriyas, and how that holy ground, Samantapanchaka,
    became the place where the Kuru and Pandava armies fought (CE 1.2.3–9).

[^b6c7-2]: **Family on both sides.** *What it means:* the war is a family war.
    *In the story:* Bhishma is the grandfather of both sides, and Drona taught
    both the Kaurava and Pandava princes. Arjuna names these two when he says he
    cannot shoot them, because he should honour them (CE 6.24.4).

[^b6c7-3]: **Classes, and their "mixing".** *What it means:* the text speaks of
    four classes (*varṇa*): brahmins (priests and teachers), kshatriyas (rulers
    and warriors), vaishyas (farmers and traders) and shudras (servants and
    workers). Arjuna fears that if the men of a family die in war, its customs
    will break down, and the order of the classes will break down with them.
    *Note:* this is Arjuna's worry, spoken in his grief. Krishna does not answer
    it point by point. He answers with a different teaching.

[^b6c7-4]: **Offerings of rice and water to the ancestors.** *What it means:*
    people offered balls of rice and water to their dead parents and
    grandparents. It was believed that the dead needed these offerings, and that
    only their descendants could give them. *In the story:* early in the epic,
    the sage Jaratkaru finds his ancestors hanging upside down in a pit,
    clinging to a clump of grass, because their family line is dying out. He has
    no son to carry it on (CE 1.13.11–16).

[^b6c7-5]: **Why Arjuna will not fight.** *What it means:* Arjuna is not afraid
    for himself. He does not want to kill people he loves and respects. His
    problem is not a lack of courage. He is torn between two duties: to fight,
    and to honour his elders.

[^b6c7-6]: **Dharma.** *What it means:* the right way to live. It is duty, law
    and goodness together. Each person also has "their own dharma"
    (*svadharma*): the duty that fits their place in life. *Everyday example:* a
    doctor's duty is to treat the sick, even ones they do not like. *In the
    story:* long before, Arjuna broke an agreement with his brothers so that he
    could get his weapons and save a brahmin's cattle from thieves. Then he went
    into exile as he had promised. When Yudhishthira told him he did not have to
    go, Arjuna said, "I have heard from you that one should not practise dharma
    by a trick. I will not move from the truth" (CE 1.205.20–30).

[^b6c7-7]: **The self (*ātman*).** *What it means:* the true "I" in each living
    being. The body changes and dies. The self, Krishna says, does not.
    *Everyday example:* you are the same person you were as a child, though your
    body has changed completely. Krishna uses this very example: childhood,
    youth, old age (CE 6.24.13).

[^b6c7-8]: **Rebirth.** *What it means:* the belief that when a body dies, the
    self moves on into a new body, as a person changes clothes (Krishna's own
    example, CE 6.24.22). *In the story:* Amba, whom Bhishma wronged, dies in a
    fire and is born again in Drupada's family as Shikhandi, who remembers the
    past life (CE 5.188.12–18; 5.193.60).

[^b6c7-9]: **A just war.** *What it means:* the Sanskrit is *dharmya*, "in
    keeping with dharma": a war fought for a right cause and by the rules. *In
    the story:* before the fighting began, both sides agreed on rules: no
    striking a man who has lost his weapons, or who has turned away, or
    charioteers or drummers (CE 6.1.26–32). On the first morning Bhishma told
    the kings, "This great door to heaven stands open for you … for a kshatriya
    it is wrong to die of sickness at home" (CE 6.17.8–11). Krishna uses the
    same picture of an open door to heaven (CE 6.24.32).

[^b6c7-10]: **Sankhya and yoga.** *What they mean:* two ways to the same goal.
    *Sankhya* ("reckoning") is the way of knowledge: understanding clearly that
    the self is different from the body. *Yoga* here does not mean exercises. It
    means "joining" or "discipline": doing your work with a steady mind. Krishna
    says more about both in the next chapters.

[^b6c7-11]: **Karma and its chains.** *What it means:* *karma* means action, and
    also the results that action brings back. People believed that every action
    ties the doer to its results, like a chain, and so to more births. Krishna
    teaches a way to act without being tied.

[^b6c7-12]: **The Veda and its "flowery speech".** *What it means:* the Vedas
    are the oldest sacred texts. Part of them is about rituals that promise
    rewards: wealth, children, heaven. Krishna is criticising people who think
    these rituals and rewards are all there is. He is not throwing away the Veda
    itself. *Everyday example:* someone who does good deeds only to be praised
    has missed the point of goodness.

[^b6c7-13]: **The three strands (*guṇa*).** *What it means:* everything in
    nature is made of three "strands" twisted together, like a rope. *Sattva* is
    goodness and clearness. *Rajas* is passion and restlessness. *Tamas* is
    darkness and dullness. Krishna explains them fully later in the Gita
    (chapter 12 of this Book). Here he says: do not be ruled by them.

[^b6c7-14]: **Work without craving the result.** *What it means:* do your duty
    as well as you can, but do not let the result be the reason you do it, and
    do not let the fear of failure stop you. This is the heart of the Gita's
    "yoga of action". *Everyday example:* a farmer ploughs and sows with care,
    but he cannot command the rain. He does his part and accepts what comes. *In
    the story:* Vyasa told Dhritarashtra before the war, "Victory is never sure.
    Fate is the last word in it. Even those who win in battle suffer loss" (CE
    6.4.35).

[^b6c7-15]: **A person of steady wisdom (*sthitaprajña*).** *What it means:*
    someone whose mind stays calm and clear whatever happens, good or bad.
    Krishna describes such a person in the verses that follow: content within,
    not greedy, not afraid, not angry, and in control of their senses, like a
    tortoise that can pull in its legs.

[^b6c7-16]: **How wanting leads to ruin.** *What it means:* Krishna gives a
    chain: thinking about something, then clinging, then wanting, then anger,
    then confusion, then forgetting, then loss of good sense, then ruin. *In the
    story:* at Yudhishthira's great sacrifice, Duryodhana saw the Pandavas'
    wealth and glory and could not stop thinking about it. He went pale, and "an
    evil thought was born" in him (CE 2.43.12–16). That thought led to the dice
    game, and in the end to this war.

[^b6c7-17]: **Brahman and nirvana.** *What they mean:* *Brahman* is the highest
    reality, the one thing behind everything. *Nirvana* means "blowing out", as
    a flame goes out: here it means perfect peace, where desire has ended.
    "Nirvana in Brahman" is the peace of being one with that highest reality.

**Divergences.**

* **The Gita in the CE.** The Bhagavad Gita is CE 6.23–40, eighteen adhyāyas. Sanjaya tells it to Dhritarashtra after Bhishma's fall (see ch 5; ledger L-35, L-190). This chapter is Gita chapters 1 and 2.
* **Speakers.** The text's tags are *dhṛtarāṣṭra uvāca*, *saṃjaya uvāca*, *arjuna uvāca* and *śrībhagavān uvāca*, "the Blessed Lord said". The narration calls him Krishna, as the Style Guide asks.
* **The meditation verses.** App. I No. 2, after 6.23.1, is a later devotional preface to the Gita (its "seed", "power", hand gestures and meditation verses). It is not in the CE and is not narrated. The opening salutation (\*85) is also rejected.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*85–\*88 and App. I No. 2. \*87 adds lines on Arjuna's bewilderment after 6.24.11; \*88 adds a verse on the true renouncer after 6.24.48.

**Choices.**

* The Gita is told in very simple English at the author's request, every verse kept, with footnotes (Style Guide §13). The footnotes explain; the text itself adds nothing. Each "In the story" example in the footnotes was checked against the CE passage cited.
* 6.23.10 *aparyāptam … paryāptam*: "Our army … has no measure. Their army … can be measured." The words can also mean "not enough" and "enough"; the verse is famously ambiguous.
* 6.23.11 *ayaneṣu*: "at every opening in the lines".
* 6.23.36 *ātatāyinaḥ*: "even though they have wronged us and would kill us". The word means an aggressor who may lawfully be killed.
* 6.23.40–43 *kuladharmāḥ*, *jātidharmāḥ*, *varṇasaṃkaraḥ*: "the family's age-old ways", "the ways of the classes", "the classes get mixed". *Adharma* is "lawlessness".
* 6.24.5 *arthakāmān*: "Even if they have taken the enemy's side for the sake of wealth". The compound can also mean "who wish us well" or "greedy for gain"; this is a cautious reading.
* 6.24.14 *mātrāsparśāḥ*: "when the senses meet the things of the world".
* 6.24.16: "What is not real never comes to be. What is real never stops being."
* 6.24.39 *buddhi*: "understanding", "mind" or "good sense", as the context needs in simple English.
* 6.24.45 *nityasattvasthaḥ*: "Always hold to what is true"; *niryogakṣemaḥ*: "Do not worry about getting and keeping things".
* 6.24.47: "Your right is to the work alone, never to its results."
* 6.24.53 *śrutivipratipannā*: "confused … by all the things you have been told".
* 6.24.66 *bhāvanā*: "power to reflect".
* 6.24.70: the ocean image is kept: "desires may flow into a person, and yet that person finds peace", as rivers flow into a still ocean.
* 6.24.72 *brāhmī sthitiḥ*: "the state of Brahman"; *brahmanirvāṇam*: "nirvana in Brahman".
