# The Rules of War

"How did the heroes fight," said Janamejaya, "the Kurus, the Pandavas and the
Somakas, and the fortunate kings gathered from many lands?"

"Hear, lord of the earth," said Vaishampayana, "how the heroes fought, the
Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas, at Kurukshetra, the field of austerity.

"The Pandavas with the Somakas came down to Kurukshetra and advanced on the
Kauravas, mighty and wanting victory. All of them were rich in the study of the
Veda, all delighted in battle, hoping for victory in battle or for death facing
the enemy. They came up to the army of Dhritarashtra's son, which was hard to
withstand, and camped with their soldiers in the western part, facing east.
Outside Samantapanchaka, Kunti's son Yudhishthira had camps made by the rules in
thousands. The whole earth seemed empty, left only with children and the old,
without horses or men, without chariots or elephants. All the strength of the
lands over which the sun shines in the circle of Jambudvipa had gathered there,
best of kings. All the classes were in one place, over a circle many yojanas
wide, and they spread over countries, rivers, mountains and forests. King
Yudhishthira ordered the finest food and drink for all of them, bull among men,
and their animals. Yudhishthira gave them passwords of many kinds, so that
whoever spoke so could be known as one of the Pandavas' men. When the time of
battle came, the Kauravya fixed tokens, passwords and ornaments for all of them.

"Seeing the tops of the Parthas' banners, high-minded Dhritarashtra's son drew
up his army against the Pandavas with all the kings. When they saw Duryodhana,
with a white umbrella held over his head in the middle of a thousand elephants,
surrounded by his brothers, all the Pandava soldiers were glad. They all blew
great conches and beat drums by the thousand. Seeing their own army so glad, the
Pandavas were glad at heart, and so was mighty Vasudeva. Then, cheering the
fighters, Vasudeva and Dhananjaya, tigers among men, standing in their chariot,
blew their heavenly conches. When they heard the roar of Panchajanya and
Devadatta, the fighters and their animals let go dung and urine. As other beasts
are terrified when they hear a lion roar, so was the army of Dhritarashtra's son
then.

"Earthly dust rose, and nothing could be made out. The sun was hidden, covered
by the dust of the army. The rain god poured down a rain of flesh and blood,
wetting all the ranks, and it was a wonder. Then a wind arose, low, dragging
gravel along, beating at those ranks and driving away the dust. Then, king, the
two armies stood at Kurukshetra, eager for battle, ready, like stirred-up
oceans. The meeting of those two armies was wonderful, like that of two oceans
when the end of the age has come. The whole earth was empty, left only with
children and the old, because of the mass of armies the Kauravas had brought
together.

---

"Then the Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas made an agreement and set down the
rules of the fighting, bull of the Bharatas. 'When the fighting is over, let
there be friendship between us again, as before and as is fitting, and let there
be no trickery. When the fight is with words, the answer must be with words. One
who has left the ranks must never be killed. A chariot fighter must be fought by
a chariot fighter, one on an elephant's neck by an elephant, a horseman by a
horseman and a foot soldier by a foot soldier, Bharata. Each must strike as is
fitting, by strength, spirit and age, after calling out, and not one who trusts
or is in distress. One engaged with another, one who is careless or has turned
his face away, one whose weapons are gone and one without armour must never be
killed. There must never be any striking at charioteers, at draught animals, at
those who carry weapons, or at those who play drums and conches.' When they had
made this agreement, the Kurus, the Pandavas and the Somakas looked at one
another in the greatest wonder. When they had taken their places, those great
men, bulls among men, with their soldiers, were glad in form and happy at heart.

---

"Then the lord and seer Vyasa, son of Satyavati, watched both twilights. He was
the best of all who know the Veda, the grandfather of the Bharatas, who sees
what is before his eyes and knows what has been, is and will be. Now that the
terrible battle was coming, he spoke secretly to King Vaichitravirya, who was
grieving, in pain and brooding on his sons' wrong policy.

"'King, your sons and the other kings have come to their time. They will meet in
battle and kill one another. When they have come to their time and are
perishing, Bharata, understand that it is the turning of time, and do not set
your mind on grief. But if you want to see this battle, lord of the people, I
will give you sight. Come, watch this war.'

"'I do not want to see the killing of kin, best of brahmin seers,' said
Dhritarashtra. 'But by your power let me hear this war, all of it.'

"Since he did not want to see the battle but wanted to hear it, the lord of
boons gave a boon to Sanjaya.

"'King, this Sanjaya will tell you of this war,' said Vyasa. 'Nothing in the
battle will be out of his sight. With divine sight, king, Sanjaya will tell you
of the war and will know everything. Open or secret, by night or by day, even
what is thought in the mind, Sanjaya will know it all. Weapons will not cut him,
and weariness will not trouble him. This son of Gavalgana will come out of this
war alive. And I will spread the fame of these Kurus and all the Pandavas, bull
of the Bharatas. Do not grieve. This was fated long ago, and you should not
grieve over it. It cannot be held back. Where dharma is, there is victory.'

"When he had said this, the lord, the great-grandfather of the Kurus, spoke
again to strong-armed Dhritarashtra. 'There will be great slaughter in this war,
great king, to judge by the signs of danger I see today. Hawks, vultures, crows
and herons gather in flocks and come down at the forest's edges. The birds watch
this very fierce war with joy. The flesh-eaters will eat the flesh of elephants
and horses. Screaming terribly, foretelling danger, the kahva birds fly across
the middle towards the south. At both twilights, morning and evening, Bharata, I
always see the sun at its rising and setting surrounded by headless trunks. At
twilight, bars of three colours, white and red at the edges, black at the neck,
with lightning, cover the sun. I have seen day and night with the sun, moon and
stars blazing, with no difference between day and night. That will bring
destruction. On the full-moon night of Karttika the moon could not be seen,
without its light, the colour of fire in a sky of one colour. Heroes, kings and
princes, brave men with arms like iron bars, will lie killed, covering the
earth.

"'Every night I hear the fierce roar of a boar and a cat fighting in the sky.
The images of the gods tremble and laugh, vomit blood from their mouths, sweat
and fall down. Drums sound without being struck, lord of the people, and the
great chariots of the kshatriyas move without being yoked. Cuckoos, woodpeckers,
jays, vultures, parrots, cranes and peacocks cry out terribly. Swarms of locusts
are seen in hundreds at dawn, holding weapons and ornaments, in armour, riding
on horses' backs. Both twilights shine with the quarters burning. There has been
a rain of blood and a rain of bones, Bharata. Arundhati, famous in the three
worlds and honoured by the good, has put Vasishtha behind her. Saturn stands
afflicting Rohini, king, and the mark on the moon has turned. There will be
great danger. Very terrible thunder is heard all the time from a cloudless sky,
and drops of tears fall from the animals as they weep.'"
