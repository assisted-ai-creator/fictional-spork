# Bhishma Takes Command

When the night was over, King Duryodhana divided those eleven armies, Bharata.
The king arranged the best, the middling and the weakest of the men, elephants,
chariots and horses in all the divisions. They had spare axles and quivers,
guard rails and lances, sheaths, spears, arrow cases and clubs. They had banners
and flags, bows and lances, bright ropes and nooses, and coverings. They had
hair-grappling hooks, oil, molasses and sand, pots of snakes, lac and dust,
bells and shields, axes and billhooks. They were covered in tiger skins and
leopard skins. They had leather guards and horns, spears and weapons of many
kinds, hatchets and spades, and oil, linen and butter. Bright in their ranks,
handsome, they blazed like fires. The warriors too were in armour, brave and
trained in weapons.

Men of good family who knew horses were set as charioteers. They wore amulets
and girths, and flew their banners and flags. All the chariots were drawn by
four horses, all were fitted with weapons, all had cheerful horses, and each
carried a hundred bows. One of the charioteers drove the two pole horses, and
two others drove the outer horses. They too were fine chariot fighters, and the
chariot fighter knew horses as well. The chariots, by the thousand, hung with
gold, were like guarded cities that no enemy could take. The elephants, like the
chariots, were girthed and well adorned, and each carried seven men, like
mountains full of jewels. Two held the goads, two were fine archers, two held
fine swords, and one carried a spear and a flag. The Kaurava's army was thick
with thousands of elephants in rut, with armour, weapons and quivers. There were
horses by the ten thousand, with riders in bright armour, with flags and fine
trappings, well trained and well kept, with golden harness, many hundreds of
thousands of them, obedient to their riders. There were foot soldiers of many
shapes and kinds, with all sorts of armour and weapons, wearing gold garlands.

To each chariot there were ten elephants, to each elephant ten horses, and to
each horse ten men as foot guards all round. Or, to each chariot there were
fifty elephants, to each elephant a hundred horses, and to each horse seven men,
to close the gaps that opened. A sena is five hundred elephants and as many
chariots. Ten senas make a pritana, and ten pritanas a vahini. Vahini, pritana,
sena, dhvajini, sadini, chamu, akshauhini and varuthini are used for one
another. That is how the wise Kaurava arrayed his divisions. Eleven akshauhinis
were counted there, and seven too. The Pandavas' force was seven akshauhinis,
and the Kauravas' force was eleven. A patti is fifty-five men. Three of these
are a senamukha, which is also called a gulma. Ten gulmas make a gana, and there
were ganas by the ten thousand in Duryodhana's armies, fighters ready to strike.

Then strong-armed King Duryodhana looked over the brave and wise men and made
his commanders. He brought together the kings, the best of men, as leaders of
the separate akshauhinis, and consecrated them in due form. They were Kripa,
Drona and Shalya, the great chariot fighter Saindhava, Sudakshina of Kamboja and
Kritavarma, Drona's son, Karna and Bhurishravas, Shakuni son of Subala and the
great chariot fighter Bahlika. Every day and at every hour, Bharata, he gave
them many kinds of signals face to face, again and again. So all of them were
appointed, and so were their followers, and the soldiers wanted to please the
king.

Then the son of Dhritarashtra joined his palms and, with all the kings, spoke to
Bhishma, son of Shantanu.

"Without a commander even a very great army breaks apart when it meets battle,
like an ant-hill. Two men's minds are never the same, and the leaders are rivals
in their courage. We hear, very wise one, that once all the brahmins went
against the Haihayas, whose power had no measure, raising banners of kusha
grass. The vaishyas and shudras followed them, grandfather, three classes on one
side and the bulls among kshatriyas on the other. In the battles the three
classes were broken again and again, and the kshatriyas won, though the army on
the other side was large. Then the best of the brahmins asked the kshatriyas,
and they, knowing dharma, told them the truth, grandfather. 'In battle we listen
to one man of great wisdom, but each of you follows his own mind.' Then the
brahmins made one brahmin, skilled in policy and brave, their commander, and
beat the kshatriyas. So those who make a skilled, brave, blameless man, who is
set on their good, their commander, conquer their enemies in battle.

"You are like Ushanas, and you always want my good. No one can turn you, and you
stand in dharma. Be our commander. Like the sun among things that shine, like
the moon among plants, like Kubera among yakshas, like Vasava among the Maruts,
like Meru among mountains, like Suparna among birds, like Kumara among beings,
like fire among the Vasus, so shall you be to us. Guarded by you, as the gods
are by Shakra, we shall surely be beyond the reach even of the thirty gods. Go
before us as Agni's son goes before the gods, and we shall follow you as calves
follow a bull."

"It is as you say, strong-armed Bharata," said Bhishma. "But the Pandavas are as
dear to me as you are. I must speak for their good too, lord of men, though I
must fight for you, as I agreed. I see no fighter on earth equal to me except
that tiger among men, Dhananjaya, son of Kunti. The strong-armed one knows every
divine weapon. But the Pandava would never fight me openly in battle. He and I
in a moment could empty this world of men, with its gods, asuras and rakshasas,
by the strength of our weapons. But I must not destroy the sons of Pandu, lord
of men. So I shall kill ten thousand fighters every day by my skill. In this way
I shall bring about their death, joy of the Kurus, unless they kill me first
when we meet.

"I shall be your commander, king, as you wish, on one more condition. You should
hear it. Let Karna fight first, lord of the earth, or me. For the suta's son
always rivals me greatly in battle."

"While Ganga's son lives, king, I shall not fight at all," said Karna. "When
Bhishma is killed, I shall fight the Gandiva bowman."

Then the son of Dhritarashtra made Bhishma commander in due form, with rich
fees, and once consecrated he shone. At the king's command men played drums,
conches and hundreds of tabors, without pause. There were lion roars of every
kind and the cries of animals. From a cloudless sky came a rain of blood and
mud. There were thunderclaps and earthquakes and the trumpeting of elephants,
which cast down the spirits of all the fighters. Voices with no body were heard,
and meteors fell from the sky. Jackals howled loudly in shrill voices,
foretelling danger. When the king consecrated Ganga's son as commander, these
terrible things happened by the hundred, king.

When he had made Bhishma, the crusher of enemy armies, his commander, he had the
best of the brahmins speak blessings, and gave them gold coins and cows in
plenty. Strengthened by blessings for victory, he set out, surrounded by his
soldiers, with the son of Apaga in front and his brothers with him, and went
with his great camp to Kurukshetra. The Kaurava went round Kurukshetra with
Karna and had a camp measured out on level ground. On sweet, unsalted land, with
plenty of fodder and firewood, the camp shone like Hastinapura itself.

"Bhishma, son of Apaga, was great-souled, the best of those who bear weapons,"
said Janamejaya, "the grandfather of the Bharatas and the banner of all kings.
He was equal to Brihaspati in wisdom, to the earth in patience, to the ocean in
depth and to the Himalaya in firmness. He was like Prajapati in generosity and
the sun in splendour, and like great Indra in destroying enemies with showers of
arrows. When King Yudhishthira heard that he had been consecrated for a long
night in the terrible, hair-raising sacrifice of battle, what did the
strong-armed king, who knew all dharma, say? What did Bhimasena and Arjuna do,
or Krishna?"

"Wise Yudhishthira, skilled in the dharma and gain of hard times," said
Vaishampayana, "called together all his brothers and Vasudeva the Satvata. The
best of speakers spoke to them gently. 'Go round the armies. Stand ready in your
armour. Your first battle will be with the grandfather. So look to the leaders
of my seven armies.'

"'You have spoken as you should at such a time, bull among the Bharatas,' said
Vasudeva, 'and what you say is to the point. I like it, strong-armed one. Let
what comes next be done. Let seven leaders be consecrated for your army.'

"Then Yudhishthira sent for Drupada, Virata, the bull of the Shinis,
Dhrishtadyumna of the Panchalas, King Dhrishtaketu, Shikhandi of the Panchalas
and Sahadeva of Magadha. He consecrated these seven great archers, heroes who
delighted in war, as the leaders of his armies, in due form. As commander of the
whole army he named Dhrishtadyumna, who was born from the blazing fire to bring
about Drona's end. Over all these great ones together he made Dhananjaya,
Gudakesha, the leader of the leaders. And the leader of Arjuna, and the driver
of his horses, was glorious, wise Janardana, the younger brother of Samkarshana.

"When he saw that the war had come, near and very destructive, Halayudha entered
the Pandava king's dwelling. With him came Akrura and the rest, Gada, Samba and
Ulmuka, the son of Rukmini, the sons of Ahuka, and Charudeshna at their head,
with the chief Vrishnis around him like tigers, fierce in strength. The
strong-armed one was guarded like Vasava by the Maruts. He wore blue silk and
was like a peak of Kailasa. He had the playful stride of a lion, and his eyes
were red at the corners with wine. When they saw him, the Dharma King, splendid
Keshava, Partha Vrikodara of terrible deeds, the Gandiva bowman and all the
other kings there rose. They came to Halayudha and honoured him. The Pandava
king touched his hand with his own, and all of them, with Vasudeva at their
head, greeted him. Halayudha, tamer of enemies, greeted old Virata and Drupada
and sat down with Yudhishthira.

"When the kings were seated all around, Rauhineya looked at Vasudeva and spoke.
'This will be a terrible and cruel slaughter of men. I think it is fate, and
cannot be avoided. I hope to see you all come out of this war, with your
friends, healthy and unhurt. The kshatriyas of the earth who have gathered are
ripe for death, no doubt of it. There will be a great slaughter, a mud of flesh
and blood. I told Vasudeva again and again in private, "Treat our kinsmen alike,
slayer of Madhu. King Duryodhana is to us what the Pandavas are. Let him be
looked after too, as is fitting." I said it again and again. But for your sake
the slayer of Madhu did not do what I said. He has given himself wholly to it,
looking to Dhananjaya.

"'I am sure that the Pandavas' victory is certain, for that is Vasudeva's
resolve, Bharata. And I cannot bear to look on the world without Krishna. So I
follow what Keshava means to do. Both heroes, skilled in the mace fight, are my
pupils. So I love Bhima and King Duryodhana equally. So I shall go to visit the
bathing places on the Sarasvati. For I shall not be able to look on while the
Kauravas perish.' When the strong-armed one had said this, he took leave of the
Pandavas, sent the slayer of Madhu back, and Rama set out on his pilgrimage.

"At this very time came the son of great Bhishmaka, King Hiranyaloman, who was
Indra's friend, lord of the Akritis, the famous Bhoja, lord of the south. The
son was known in every quarter as Rukmin. He was the pupil of the lion of the
kimpurushas, who lives on Gandhamadana, and he had learned the whole four-footed
science of the bow. He had won the bow of great Indra, equal in power to the
Gandiva and matched with the Sharnga, divine and imperishable. There are only
these three divine bows of the dwellers in heaven: Varuna's, the Gandiva; great
Indra's, the Vijaya; and the Sharnga, which they call Vishnu's, the divine bow
made of splendour, which Krishna carried, bringing fear to the enemy's army. The
son of the chastiser of Paka got the Gandiva from Fire at Khandava. Splendid
Rukmin got the Vijaya from Druma. When Hrishikesha had cut the nooses of Muru,
killed Muru by his might, conquered Naraka, son of the earth, and brought back
the jewelled earrings, he gained sixteen thousand women and many kinds of
jewels, and the Sharnga, the best of bows.

"When Rukmin had got the Vijaya, whose sound was like a cloud, he came to the
Pandavas as if frightening the world. He was proud of the strength of his own
arms. Long ago the hero could not bear it when wise Vasudeva carried off
Rukmini. He had made a vow, 'I shall not come back until I have killed Keshava,'
and then he ran after the Varshneya, the best of all who bear weapons, with a
great four-part army that stretched far, with bright weapons and armour, like
the Ganga in flood. When he met the Varshneya, the lord and master of yoga, he
was beaten and shamed, king, and he did not go back to Kundina. Where Krishna
had beaten him in battle, the killer of enemy heroes built a fine city called
Bhojakata. With that great army, rich in elephants and horses, that city became
famous on earth by the name of Bhojakata, king.

"That mighty king of the Bhojas came to the Pandavas surrounded by a great army,
an akshauhini. Armoured, with sword, arrows, bow, arm guard and chariot, he
entered the great army with a banner the colour of the sun. He was known to the
Pandavas, and he came to please Vasudeva. King Yudhishthira went out to meet him
and honoured him. Honoured by the sons of Pandu, and well received as was right,
he honoured them in turn, and rested with his soldiers. Then he spoke to
Dhananjaya, son of Kunti, in the middle of the heroes. 'If you are afraid, son
of Pandu, I am here to help you in the war. I shall give you help in battle that
your enemies will not be able to bear. There is no man here equal to me in
courage. I shall kill your enemies in battle and give them to you, Phalguna.'

"He said this in the presence of the Dharma King and Keshava, while the kings
and all the others listened. Wise Kaunteya looked at Vasudeva and at the Dharma
King, the Pandava, laughed, and said as a friend, 'When I fought the mighty
gandharvas at the cattle tour, hero, what friend was there to help me? When I
fought at Khandava, in that terrible place full of gods and danavas, who helped
me then? When I fought the Nivatakavachas and the Kalakeyas, the danavas, who
helped me then? When I fought many Kurus in battle at Virata's city, my friend,
who helped me? I have looked to Rudra, Shakra, Vaishravana, Yama, Varuna and
Fire, to Kripa, Drona and Madhava in battle. I carry the divine Gandiva, the
strong bow made of splendour. I have arrows that never run out, and I am
strengthened with divine weapons. I was born in the family of the Kauravas, and
above all I am the son of Pandu. I call Drona my teacher, and I have Vasudeva as
my ally. How could a man like me say the shameful words, "I am afraid," tiger
among men, even to the thunderbolt-wielder himself? I am not afraid,
strong-armed one, and I do not need help. Go as you please, as suits you, or
stay.'

"Then Rukmin turned back his army, which was like the ocean, and went to
Duryodhana in the same way, bull among the Bharatas. When the king met him, he
said the same thing, and he was refused by Duryodhana too, who thought himself a
hero. So only two went away from that war, great king: Rauhineya the Varshneya
and King Rukmin. When Rama had gone on his pilgrimage, and Bhishmaka's son too,
the Pandavas sat down again to take counsel. The Dharma King's council, crowded
with kings, shone like the sky bright with stars and the moon, Bharata."

**Divergences.**

* **Bhishma's terms.** Bhishma says the Pandavas are as dear to him as the Kauravas, that he will not destroy them, and that he will kill ten thousand fighters a day (5.153.16–22). See ledger L-182.
* **Karna and Bhishma.** Karna's refusal to fight while Bhishma lives is CE here too (5.153.25), as at 5.61.13. See ledger L-177.
* **Sixteen thousand women.** When Krishna defeated Naraka he gained sixteen thousand women and many jewels (5.155.9). The CE says no more here. The rejected line \*488 (at 5.128.44) says he married them. See ledger L-182.
* **Duryodhana at Bhishma's feet.** A rejected line (\*550) has Duryodhana fall at Bhishma's feet. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*543–\*549, \*551–\*554, among them more equipment for the chariots and Rukmin's offer to kill Drona, Kripa, Bhishma and Karna (\*552).
