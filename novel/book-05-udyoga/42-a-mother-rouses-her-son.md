# A Mother Rouses Her Son

Krishna went into her house, greeted her feet, and told her briefly what had
happened in the Kuru assembly.

"Many words were spoken, worth taking and full of reason," said Vasudeva, "by
the seers and by me, and he did not take them. All those who follow Duryodhana
are ripe for time. I take my leave of you, and I will go quickly to the
Pandavas. What should I tell the Pandavas from you? Tell me, very wise lady. I
want to hear your words."

"Keshava, tell King Yudhishthira, whose soul is dharma," said Kunti, "'Your
dharma is wasting away fast. Do not make it vain, my son. Like a dull, unwise
reader of the Veda, king, your understanding is stunned by repeating the
lessons, and it sees only dharma. Look, my son, at dharma as the Self-born made
it. The kshatriya was made from his chest, to live by the strength of his arms,
always for hard deeds and to protect the people.

"'Hear a parable that I heard from the old. Long ago Vaishravana, pleased, gave
this earth to the royal seer Muchukunda, and he did not accept it. "I want to
enjoy a kingdom won by the strength of my arms," he said. Then Vaishravana was
pleased and amazed. Then King Muchukunda ruled the earth well, won by the
strength of his arms, following the dharma of a kshatriya.

"'Of the dharma that people practise when they are well protected by the king,
the king gets a quarter, Bharata. If the king practises dharma, he is fit for
godhood. If he practises adharma, he goes to hell. The science of the rod, used
rightly by the ruler, holds the four classes to their own dharma and holds them
back from adharma. When the king keeps fully and rightly to the science of the
rod, then the best of times, the age called Krita, begins. Do not doubt whether
time is the cause of the king or the king the cause of time. The king is the
cause of time. The king creates the Krita age, and the Treta, and the Dvapara.
The king is the cause of the fourth age too. For causing the Krita, a king
enjoys heaven without end. For causing the Treta, he does not enjoy heaven
without end. For setting the Dvapara going, he enjoys his share. Then the
evil-doer lives in hell for endless years. For the world is touched by the
king's fault, and he by the world's.

"'Look to the dharma of kings that befits your father and grandfather. This is
not the conduct of a royal seer, the one you want to keep to. No king who
settled into kindness mixed with weakness ever won any fruit of protecting his
people. Neither Pandu nor I nor your grandfather ever wished for you the
blessing by which you now live with that wisdom of yours. I have always wished
you sacrifice, giving, austerity, courage, children, greatness, strength and
energy. The gods worshipped rightly, and men, always gave Svaha and Svadha, long
life, wealth and sons. Ancestors and gods always hope these from sons: giving,
study, sacrifice and protecting the people. Whether it is dharma or adharma, you
were born to it by your birth. You are learned, born in a good family, my son,
and tormented by want.

"'What dharma is greater than this, that hungry people of the earth come to a
generous hero and go away satisfied? A man of dharma who has won a kingdom here
should take others to himself on every side, one by giving, another by force,
another by kind words. A brahmin should live by begging, a kshatriya should
protect, a vaishya should earn wealth, and a shudra should serve them. Begging
is forbidden to you, and farming does not suit you. You are a kshatriya, a
protector from harm, who lives by the strength of his arms. Lift up again your
father's share that has sunk, strong-armed one, by conciliation, by gifts, by
division, by the rod and by policy. What could be more painful than this, that
I, without kin, look to others for my food, I who bore you, joy of your enemies?
Fight by the dharma of kings. Do not sink your grandfathers. Do not go with your
brothers, your merit spent, to an evil end.'

---

"On this," said Kunti, "they tell an old story, the dialogue of Vidura and her
son, scorcher of enemies. In it she says what is good, and more. She was famous
and proud, born in a good family, a light in the night, devoted to the dharma of
a kshatriya, fortunate, far-seeing, known in the assemblies of kings, learned, a
woman whose words were heard. This woman named Vidura, true to herself, blamed
her own son, who had been beaten by the king of Sindhu and lay there, sunk in
misery, bringing no joy, not knowing dharma, adding to his enemies' delight.

"'You are not my son, nor your father's. Where did you come from? You are
without anger, a mere offshoot, a man whose tools are a eunuch's. You are
hopeless for the rest of your life. Carry the yoke for good. Do not despise
yourself. Do not live on a little. Set your mind on good, do not be afraid, hold
yourself up. Get up, you coward. Do not lie there beaten, delighting all your
enemies, without pride, bringing grief to your kin. A little river is easily
filled. A mouse's paws are easily filled. A coward is easily contented. He is
pleased with very little.

"'Break the fang of your enemy, even if you go quickly to your death. Even if
your life is in danger, show your courage. Circle in the sky like a hawk,
looking for your enemy's weak point, crying out or silent, without fear. Why do
you lie here like a dead man, as if struck by lightning? Get up, you coward. Do
not lie there beaten. Do not sink out of sight, miserable. Make yourself heard
by your own deeds. Do not stay in the middle, or the lowest, or below. Stand up
and be strong. Blaze for a moment, like a firebrand of tinduka wood. Do not be
like a fire of chaff without flame, wanting to live like a crow. Better to blaze
for a moment than smoke for a long time.

"'Let no king's wife, in anyone's house, be as soft as a she-donkey. A man who
has done a man's work, and run the race of battle to the end, is free of his
debt to dharma and does not blame himself. The wise do not grieve whether they
win or not. They go straight on to the next thing and do not cling to their
lives. Show your courage, or go to that sure end. Putting dharma first, son, why
else do you live? Your sacrifices and good works, eunuch, and all your fame are
destroyed. The root of your enjoyment is cut. Why else do you live?

"'A man who is drowning, or about to fall, should seize his enemy by the leg.
Even if his roots are cut, he should never despair. He should lift the yoke and
pull, remembering what a thoroughbred does. Have spirit and pride. Know your own
manhood. Lift up the family that has sunk because of you. The man whose great
and wonderful deeds people do not talk of only adds to the heap. He is neither
woman nor man. The man whose fame is not known for giving, austerity, courage,
learning or wealth is only his mother's dung. The man who outshines others by
learning, austerity, fortune or courage, by his deeds, is a man.

"'You should not seek the beggar's life, mean, cruel, shameful and painful, fit
for a coward. Kin do not prosper when they come to such a kinsman, whom enemies
welcome, thin, despised by the world, eating and dressing poorly, delighted by
small gains, wretched, of mean life and little worth. We will perish for lack of
a living, driven from the kingdom, without any of our pleasures, fallen from our
place, with nothing. Sanjaya, I bore you as a son, but you are strife itself,
bringing disgrace among the good and destroying the family line. Let no woman
ever bear a son like this, without anger, without effort, without courage, a joy
to his enemies. Do not smoke. Blaze fiercely. Attack and kill your enemies.
Blaze on your enemies' heads, if only for a moment or an instant.

"'A man is just this: impatient and unforgiving. The patient man without anger
is neither woman nor man. Contentment kills fortune, and so does pity. Sloth and
fear both do too. The man without effort wins nothing great. Free yourself by
your own strength from these sins that bring defeat. Make your heart iron and
seek your own again. He is called *purusha*, a man, because he bears up against
the city, *pura*. They say the man who lives here like a woman bears his name in
vain. Even when a brave hero of mighty spirit, who walks with a lion's stride,
has met his fate, his people delight in his leavings. The man who gives up his
own pleasure and comfort and seeks fortune soon brings joy to his counsellors.'

"'What use is the whole earth to you without me?' said the son. 'What use are
ornaments, pleasures or life?'

"'Let our enemies win the worlds of those who live only for today,' said the
mother, 'and let our friends go to the worlds of the self-respecting. Do not
follow the way of the wretched and spiritless, who are abandoned by their
servants and live on others' food. Let brahmins and friends live on you, my son,
as creatures live on the rain cloud and the gods on Shatakratu. The man on whom
all beings live, Sanjaya, as on a tree with ripe fruit, has a life worth living.
The man by whose courage his kin thrive happily, as the thirty gods thrive by
Shakra's, lives well here. The man who lives by the strength of his own arms
wins fame in the world and a good end hereafter.

---

"'And now, in this state, you want to give up your manhood. You will soon go the
road the lowest go. A kshatriya who wants to live and does not show his fire by
his courage, as far as he can, they call a thief. Words full of sense, fitting
and good, do not reach you, as medicine does not reach a dying man. There are
many people in the king of Sindhu's kingdom who are not content. They sit by,
foolish, out of weakness, waiting for his misfortunes. When they see your
manhood, others will turn against him too. Gather allies, make them resolute
here and there, and roam the mountain strongholds with them, waiting for his
misfortune in time. He does not live for ever, free of age and death.

"'You are called Sanjaya, the victorious, and I do not see it in you. Be true to
your name, my son, and do not bear it in vain. When you were a child, a very
wise brahmin who saw clearly said of you, "This boy will meet great trouble and
rise again." Remembering his words, I hope for your victory. That is why I speak
to you, my son, and I will speak again and again. The man whose success gladdens
others, who follows his aim by good policy, is sure of success. Whether it is
prosperity or want, Sanjaya, knowing this, set your mind on war. Do not hold
back.

"'Shambara said there is no worse state than one in which no meal can be seen
today or tomorrow. He said it is greater pain than the death of a husband or
son. What is called poverty is a kind of death. I was born in a great family,
and came like a stream from one lake into another. I was a queen, with every
good thing, greatly honoured by my husband. Once my friends saw me with costly
garlands and ornaments and fine clothes. Now they see me in misery. When you see
me and your wife very weak, Sanjaya, you will have no use for life. When you see
the slaves, workers and servants, the teachers, priests and chaplains leaving us
for want of a living, what use is life to you?

"'If I do not see you do some deed today, as before, praiseworthy and glorious,
what peace will my heart have? If I had to say "No" to brahmins, my heart would
break. Neither I nor my husband ever said "No" to a brahmin. We were the ones
others took shelter with, not those who took shelter with others. If I have to
live by sheltering with others, I will give up my life. Be the far shore for us
who have none. Be the boat for us who have no boat. Make a place for us who have
no place. Bring us back to life, for we are dead. You can bear all your enemies,
if you do not want to live. But if you take up such a eunuch's life, give it up,
weary and broken in spirit, this wicked living. A hero becomes famous by killing
a single enemy. Indra became great Indra by killing Vritra alone. He won the
great Indra's cup and became lord of the worlds.

"'When a hero proclaims his name in battle, or challenges his enemies in armour,
or routs the front of an army, or kills a great man, when he wins great fame by
fighting well, then his enemies tremble and bow to him. Cowards, helpless, fill
the hero who has risked his life skilfully in battle with every wish and every
wealth. The good finish off a deceived enemy, whether the cost is a kingdom
fiercely lost or doubt about one's own life. Thinking a kingdom like the door of
heaven, or like amrita, blocked on a single road, fall on your enemies like a
firebrand. Kill your enemies in battle, king. Keep your own dharma. Never let a
prosperous enemy see you so wretched. Let me not see you wretched, surrounded by
our people grieving and by our enemies shouting, while I am wretched too.

"'Live with the girls of Sauvira and boast of your wealth as before. Do not fall
into the power of the girls of Sindhu. When a man like you, young, handsome,
learned, of good family, famous and known in the world, behaves basely, like an
ox that will not bear the yoke, I think it is death. If I see you speaking
kindly to another, or walking behind him, what peace will my heart have? No one
born in this family would walk behind another. You should not live, my son,
carrying another's yoke. I know the heart of the kshatriya, which is eternal, as
it was told by those before and those before them, and by those after and those
after them. A kshatriya born here who knows the dharma of a kshatriya should
never bow to anyone here out of fear or for a living. He should stand up and not
bow, for effort is manhood. He may break at the wrong joint, but he should not
bow to anyone here. Like a great-minded elephant in rut he should go about. He
should bow only to brahmins, Sanjaya, and for dharma. Holding the other classes
in check and striking down all evil-doers, with allies or without, he should be
like that all his life.'

---

"'Your heart is made of black iron, beaten hard, mother,' said the son,
'pitiless, set on enmity, impatient. Oh, the way of kshatriyas, that you should
speak like this to me, your only son, as if I were someone else! What use is the
whole earth to you without me? What use are ornaments, pleasures or life?'

"'All the undertakings of the wise, my son, are for the sake of dharma and
purpose,' said the mother. 'Looking to those, Sanjaya, I have spurred you on.
The chief time has come, the time that calls for action. If you do not do your
work now that it has come, you will do a very cruel thing, and lose all respect.
If I did not speak to you, Sanjaya, when you are touched by shame, they would
call it a she-donkey's love, without strength or reason. Give up the road the
good condemn and fools follow. There is a great ignorance that people take
shelter in. If you behave well, you will be dear to me for that, with conduct
joined to dharma, purpose and virtue, with the divine and the human, as the good
have practised it, and in no other way. A man who delights in a son or a
grandson without manners or effort has the fruit of his offspring in vain. The
lowest of men, who do no deeds, or do blameworthy ones, find no happiness here
or hereafter.

"'The kshatriya was made for war here, Sanjaya, and for victory, always for hard
deeds and to protect the people. Winning or being killed, he reaches the world
of Indra. There is no happiness in Shakra's holy dwelling in heaven like the
happiness a kshatriya enjoys when he has brought his enemies under his power. A
proud man, burning with anger, often wronged, wanting to beat his enemies, must
either give up his life or bring down his enemies. How else could he have peace?
A wise man here wants little that is unpleasant. A man for whom little in the
world is pleasant surely has little that is unpleasant. And without what is
pleasant, a man wins nothing fine. He surely goes to nothing, as the Ganga goes
to the ocean.'

"'You should not speak like this, mother, least of all to your son,' said the
son. 'See only pity here, and be dumb as a stone.'

"'That gives me all the more joy, that you see it like this,' said the mother.
'You urge me to what should be urged, and I urge you all the more. Then I will
honour you, when you have killed all the men of Sindhu. I see your whole victory
to come.'

"'How could I win, without treasure or allies?' said the son. 'Knowing my own
terrible state, my thoughts have turned from the kingdom, as an evil-doer's
thoughts turn from heaven. If you see any such way, you whose wisdom has
ripened, tell me plainly, since I ask. I will do all of it, just as you
command.'

"'Son, do not despise yourself because of past failures,' said the mother.
'Wealth that was not comes to be, and wealth that was is lost. Nor should great
undertakings be started by fools out of mere impatience. The fruit of every
deed, my son, is always uncertain. Some know it is uncertain and still succeed,
or do not. But those who do nothing never succeed at all. Without effort there
is one result, that nothing comes of it. With effort there are two: the fruit
comes or it does not. A man who knows beforehand that all things are uncertain
can drive off growth and prosperity in adversity, prince. A man should rise,
keep awake and set to the work of prosperity, always untroubled, with his mind
set on "It will be," putting lucky things first, with brahmins and lords. A wise
king soon prospers, my son. Fortune turns to him as the sun turns to the east.

"'You have been shown examples, means and many encouragements. I see that you
take them in. Show your manhood. You should gather in the human aim you want.
Carefully take to yourself the angry, the greedy, the ruined, the cast off and
the insulted, and any who are rivals. In this way you will break up great bands,
as a wild, mighty wind scatters the clouds. Give them the first share. Rise
early, and speak kindly. They will do what pleases you and surely put you first.
When an enemy knows his rival has given up his life, he trembles before him as
before a snake in the house. If, knowing him to be bold, he does not bring him
under his power, he should make peace with him by negotiation. In the end that
will come. Once you have a footing through negotiation, your wealth will grow.
Friends serve the rich and lean on them. But when a man's wealth has slipped, my
son, even his kin desert him. Even those who lean on him despise such a man. A
man who makes his enemy his ally and trusts him may well win a kingdom.

"'A king must never show fear, in any trouble. Even if he is broken, he should
not act broken. When they see the king broken, everyone breaks after him, the
kingdom, the army and the counsellors, and they each go their own way. Some go
over to the enemy. Others desert. Others, who were insulted before, try to
strike. Only the closest friends wait on him, powerless, wishing him well, like
cows with tethered calves. They grieve for him as he grieves, as for kin they
trust. You have honoured them before, and you know them as friends, those who
care for the kingdom when the king has met misfortune. Do not frighten your
friends. Do not let them leave you, broken.

"'I said all this to test your power, your manhood and your wisdom, cheering you
as the strong cheer the weak. If you understand it, if I speak rightly, make
yourself fierce, Sanjaya, and rise for victory. We have a great store of
treasure that you do not know of. I know it, and no one else. I will hand it to
you. And you have many hundreds of friends, Sanjaya, heroes who bear happiness
and sorrow, worth a hundred each, who will not turn back. Such allies and
counsellors, slayers of enemies, belong to a man who wants to prosper and rises
up even a little.'

"'Hearing words like these, so wonderful in meaning, words and syllables,' said
the son, 'whose darkness would not be driven away, however small his mind? I
must bear this yoke in the water, and run down the slope, since you are my
guide, who see what is to come and what has been. I wanted to hear more and more
of your words, and answering a little now and then, I kept silent again and
again. Unsatisfied, as with amrita hard won from a kinswoman, I rise up to hold
down my enemies and win.'"

---

"Like a fine horse struck," said Kunti, "goaded by the arrows of her words, he
did everything just as she told him. This fierce, rousing story, which adds to a
man's fire, should be told by a counsellor to a king who is sinking, pressed by
his enemies. This story is called Victory, and anyone who wants to conquer
should hear it. When he has heard it, he soon conquers the earth and crushes his
enemies. It brings sons and heroes to birth. A pregnant woman who hears it again
and again will surely bear a hero: a hero of learning, of austerity, of
self-control, an ascetic, blazing with the splendour of Brahman, praised by the
good, bright, strong, fortunate, a great chariot fighter, bold, unassailable, a
conqueror never conquered, a curb on the wicked and a guardian of those who keep
dharma. For that a kshatriya woman bears a hero, truly brave."
