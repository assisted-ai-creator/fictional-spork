# Bhishma and Rama

"Then Rama all but laughed and said to me," said Bhishma, "'By good fortune you
want to fight me in battle, Bhishma. Here I go to Kurukshetra, Kauravya, with
you. I shall do what I have said. Come there, scorcher of enemies. There let
your mother Jahnavi see you killed by me, Bhishma, covered with hundreds of
arrows, food for vultures, herons and badas. Let the goddess, whom the siddhas
and charanas serve, weep today, lord of the earth, when she sees you wretched
and killed by me. The fortunate river, Bhagiratha's daughter, who bore you, a
fool and sick with a lust for war, does not deserve it. Come, go with me,
Bhishma. Let the battle be today. Take everything, Kauravya, your chariot and
the rest, bull among the Bharatas.'

"When Rama, conqueror of enemy cities, said this, I bowed my head, king, and
said, 'So be it.' When he had said this, Rama went to Kurukshetra, wanting to
fight, and I went into the city and told Satyavati. Then, when the rites for my
safety had been done and my mother had blessed me, I had the brahmins say the
blessings for a holy day and for my welfare, splendid one. I mounted a lovely
silver chariot with white horses, well fitted, with a good seat and a tiger-skin
covering, furnished with great weapons and all its gear, king. It was yoked by a
heroic suta of good family who knew the science of horses, well trained and
often tested. I was armoured in fine white armour, and I took a white bow and
set out, best of the Bharatas, with a white umbrella held over my head, fanned
with white yak-tail fans, lord of men, wearing white clothes and a white turban
and ornaments all white. Praised with blessings for victory, I went out of
Gajasahvaya to Kurukshetra, the field of battle, bull among the Bharatas. Urged
on by that suta, the horses, as swift as mind and wind, carried me hard to the
great battle, king.

"When I had come to Kurukshetra, and mighty Rama too, king, we came at each
other at once for battle. I stood in sight of Rama, the great ascetic, took up
the best of conches and blew it loudly. The brahmins, king, and the ascetics who
live in the forest, and the gods with the hosts of seers, watched that divine
battle. Divine garlands appeared again and again, and divine music and masses of
cloud. All the ascetics who followed the Bhargava became onlookers and stood
around the field.

"Then the goddess who wishes the good of all beings, my mother, in her own form,
king, said to me, 'What is it you mean to do? I shall go to Jamadagnya, best of
the Kurus, and beg him again and again, "Do not fight Bhishma, your pupil." Do
not be so stubborn, my son, lord of the earth, as to fight the brahmin
Jamadagnya in battle.' So she scolded me. 'Do you not know, my son, that Rama,
the destroyer of the kshatriyas, is equal in courage to Hara, that you want to
fight him?'

"Then I greeted the goddess with joined palms, best of the Bharatas, and told
her everything as it had happened at the bridegroom choice, and how I had tried
to calm Rama before, king of kings, and of the old wish of the daughter of the
king of Kashi. Then my mother, the great river, went to Rama. For my sake the
goddess asked the seer, the Bhargava, to forgive, and said, 'Do not fight
Bhishma, your pupil.' And he said to her as she begged, 'Turn Bhishma back. He
does not do what I want.' And so I came to him."

"Then Ganga, out of love for her son," said Sanjaya, "came to Bhishma again. But
he, his eyes rolling with anger, did not do what she said. Then the righteous
best of the Bhrigus, the great ascetic, appeared, and the best of brahmins
challenged him to battle again."

"I all but smiled," said Bhishma, "and spoke to him as he stood ready for
battle. 'I cannot fight you from a chariot while you stand on the ground. Mount
a chariot, hero, and put on armour, strong-armed Rama, if you want to fight me
in battle.'

"Then Rama smiled and said to me on the field, 'The earth is my chariot,
Bhishma, the Vedas are my horses, like good horses. The wind is my charioteer,
and the mothers of the Veda are my armour. Well covered by them, I shall fight
in battle, joy of the Kurus.' As he said this, son of Gandhari, Rama, whose
courage was true, surrounded me on every side with a great mass of arrows.

"Then I saw Jamadagnya standing in a divine chariot, glorious, wonderful to look
at, carrying every weapon, holy, broad as a city, made by the mind, yoked with
divine horses, fitted out and adorned with gold, with a banner marked with the
moon as its sign, strong-armed one. He held a bow, his quiver was tied on, and
he wore the lizard-skin finger guards. Akritavrana drove for him as he fought,
his friend, a knower of the Veda, very dear to the Bhargava. Challenging me to
battle, the Bhargava seemed to gladden my heart, calling out again and again,
'Come on!'

"I went to meet Rama alone, like the rising sun, whom no one could touch, the
mighty destroyer of the kshatriyas, alone. Then, three arrow-shots away, I
reined in my horses, got down, laid my bow aside, and went on foot to the best
of seers, to honour the best of brahmins. I greeted him as was right and said
the best of words: 'I shall fight you in battle, Rama, my superior and my
better, my teacher, whose nature is dharma. Wish me victory, my lord.'

"'That is how it should be done, best of the Kurus, by one who wants to
prosper,' said Rama. 'For this is the dharma of those who fight with their
betters, strong-armed one. I would have cursed you if you had not come like
this, lord of the people. Fight, and be careful in battle. Hold fast to your
courage, Kaurava. But I do not wish you victory, for I stand here to beat you.
Go and fight by dharma. I am pleased with your conduct.'

"Then I bowed to him, mounted my chariot quickly and blew my gold-adorned conch
again in the battle. Then the battle began between him and me, Bharata, for many
days, king, each wanting to beat the other. First he struck me in that battle
with nine hundred and sixty heron-feathered arrows, bright as fire. My four
horses and my charioteer were held back, lord of the people, and so was I,
standing armoured in the battle. I bowed to the gods and the brahmins, Bharata,
and all but smiled and spoke to him where he stood on the field. 'I have
honoured you as my teacher, though you have no bounds. But hear me again,
brahmin, on the rule of dharma. I do not strike the Vedas in your body, nor your
great brahminhood, nor the great austerity you have practised. I strike the
kshatriya's dharma, which you have taken up, Rama. For a brahmin becomes a
kshatriya by raising weapons. See the strength of my bow and the power of my
arms. I shall cut your bow in two, hero, with its arrow.'

"I shot a sharp broad-headed arrow at him, bull among the Bharatas, and it cut
off the tip of his bow, and it fell to the ground. And I shot nine hundred
straight heron-feathered arrows at Jamadagnya's chariot. They stuck in his body,
and driven by the wind they moved, dripping blood, like snakes. With every limb
wet with blood, and blood dripping from his wounds, Rama shone then, king, like
Meru pouring out its minerals, or like an ashoka tree at the end of winter, hung
with red clusters, or here and there like a kimshuka.

"Then Rama took up another bow in his anger and rained sharp arrows with golden
feathers. They reached me, fierce, piercing my vital parts in many places, very
swift, like snakes, fire and poison, and they shook me. Then I steadied myself
again in the battle and in my anger covered Rama with arrows by the hundred.
Pierced by those sharp arrows like fire and the sun, like poisonous snakes, Rama
seemed to lose his senses. Then I was filled with pity and blamed myself. 'Shame
on war, and shame on the kshatriyas,' I said, bull among the Bharatas. Again and
again, king, flooded with grief, I said, 'Alas, I have done an evil thing by the
work of a kshatriya, that my teacher, a brahmin whose nature is dharma, should
be hurt like this by arrows.' After that I did not strike Jamadagnya again,
Bharata. Then the thousand-rayed sun, having scorched the earth, set at the end
of the day, and the battle stopped.

"Then my charioteer, whom the skilled respected, drew out the arrows from
himself, from the horses and from me, lord of the people. When the sun had risen
at dawn, and the horses had been bathed and rolled and watered and were fresh,
the battle began. When mighty Rama saw me coming quickly, armoured and standing
in my chariot, he made his chariot ready. When I saw Rama coming, eager for
battle, I put down my great bow and got down quickly from my chariot. When I had
greeted him in the same way, I mounted my chariot, Bharata, and stood fearless
before Jamadagnya, wanting to fight.

"Then he covered me with a great shower of arrows, and I covered him as he
rained them. In his anger Jamadagnya sent more arrows at me, king, like snakes
with blazing mouths. I cut them down with sharp broad-headed arrows by the
hundred and the thousand, suddenly, in mid-air, king, again and again. Then
mighty Jamadagnya loosed divine weapons at me, and I held them off with weapons,
strong-armed one, wanting to do better. Then a great noise rose in the sky on
every side. I used the wind weapon against Jamadagnya, and Rama struck it down
with the weapon of the guhyakas, Bharata. Then I charged the fire weapon with
mantras and used it, and the lord Rama held it off with Varuna's. So I held off
Rama's divine weapons, and splendid Rama, tamer of enemies, who knew the divine
weapons, held off mine.

"Then the best of brahmins, mighty Jamadagnya, in anger, moving to my left,
king, pierced me in the chest. Then I sank down in my fine chariot, best of the
Bharatas, and my charioteer quickly carried me off, a good way, overcome and
hurt by Rama's arrows. When they saw me go, badly wounded and senseless, all
Rama's followers cried out for joy, with Akritavrana at their head, and the girl
of Kashi too, Bharata.

"When I came to, I knew my charioteer and said, 'Drive to where Rama is, suta. I
am ready. The pain is gone.' Then my charioteer carried me with the finest
horses, which seemed to dance and were as swift as the wind, Kauravya. I reached
Rama and in fury covered him in his fury with a net of arrows, Kaurava, wanting
to win. As my straight arrows came at him, Rama cut them down quickly with
arrows in the battle, each with three. All my sharp arrows were crushed, cut in
two by Rama's arrows by the hundred in the great battle. Then I loosed another
blazing, shining arrow like death at Jamadagnya Rama, wanting to kill him.
Struck hard by it, and overcome by the force of the arrow, Rama suddenly fainted
and fell to the ground.

"When Rama fell to the earth there was an outcry, and the world was shaken,
Bharata, as if the sun had fallen. Then all the ascetics, and the girl of Kashi,
ran in alarm to the son of Bhrigu. They embraced him and slowly revived him,
Kaurava, with hands cool with water and with blessings for victory. Then Rama,
still reeling, rose and said to me, fixing an arrow on his bow, 'Stand, Bhishma,
you are dead.' It was loosed and fell quickly on my left side in the great
battle, and I reeled from it, badly shaken, like a tree. Then he killed my
horses with his quick weapon in the great battle, king, and fearlessly covered
me with his feathered arrows. Then I too loosed the quick weapon, which nothing
can stop in battle, strong-armed one. Our arrows, Rama's and mine, stood in
between and quickly covered the sky on every side. The sun gave no heat, covered
by the net of arrows, and the wind roared in the space between, as if blocked by
clouds. Then from the shaking of the wind, the rays of the sun and the heat,
fire was born of itself. The arrows, set ablaze by the fire they had made, all
fell to the ground as ash, king. Then Rama in his anger quickly let fall on me
hundreds of thousands, millions and hundreds of millions of arrows, tens of
thousands, and more beyond counting, Kaurava. I cut them down in battle with
arrows like poisonous snakes and brought them to the ground like snakes, king.
So that battle went, best of the Bharatas. When the twilight had passed, my
teacher withdrew.

"The next day there was a terrible, tumultuous battle again with Rama, best of
the Bharatas. The righteous hero who knew the divine weapons, the lord, used
many divine weapons, day after day. I destroyed those weapons with weapons that
countered them in that tumultuous battle, Bharata, giving up the life that is so
hard to give up. When his weapons had been destroyed by mine in many ways, the
splendid Bhargava was angry, and he gave no thought to his life in the battle.

"Then great Jamadagnya, held back by my weapons, hurled a spear of terrible
shape, like a blazing meteor loosed by Time, its point aflame, covering the
worlds with its splendour. I cut it in three with blazing arrows as it came,
bright as the sun at the end of time, and brought it to the ground. Then a wind
blew with a holy scent. When it was cut, Rama, blazing with anger, hurled twelve
more terrible spears. Their form cannot be told, Bharata, for their splendour
and their speed. I was dismayed when I saw them coming from every quarter, like
great meteors of fire, of many shapes and blazing with fierce splendour, like
the twelve suns at the destruction of the world. Then I spread a net made of
arrows, saw them, pierced them with a net of arrows, and loosed twelve arrows in
the battle, and so I destroyed the terrible spears.

"Then great Jamadagnya hurled other terrible spears with golden shafts, bright,
bound with golden bands, blazing like great meteors. Those fierce ones too I
warded off with my shield and brought down with my sword in battle, lord of men.
And I rained divine arrows on Jamadagnya's divine horses and his charioteer.
When the great one, the crusher of the lord of the Haihayas, saw the golden
spears, like snakes that have shed their skins, cut down, in his anger he
brought out a divine weapon. Then rows of blazing arrows fell like fierce swarms
of locusts. They covered my body, my horses, my charioteer and my chariot. My
chariot was covered with arrows on every side, and so were my horses and my
charioteer, king. The yoke, the chariot pole and the wheels were covered too,
and the axle was cut by arrows and broke. When that rain of arrows was over, I
rained a flood of arrows on my teacher. That heap of Brahman, wounded by the
arrows, poured out a great deal of blood from his body without pause. As Rama
was scorched by the net of arrows, so I too was deeply pierced. Then the battle
stopped in the afternoon, as the sun made for the western mountain.

"At dawn, king of kings, when the sun had risen clear, the battle began again
between the Bhargava and me. Then Rama, the best of fighters, standing in his
wheeling chariot, rained showers of arrows on me, as Shakra does on a mountain.
My charioteer, my friend, struck by that shower of arrows, fell on the chariot
seat, and my heart sank. Then my charioteer fell into a deep faint. He fell to
the ground from the blows of the arrows and lost his senses. Then, hurt by
Rama's arrows, my charioteer gave up his life in a moment, king of kings, and
fear came over me.

"When my charioteer was killed, king, and I was shooting arrows with my mind
distracted, Rama loosed arrows at me like death. As I reeled, stricken by my
charioteer's death, the Bhargava drew his strong bow hard and struck me deeply
with an arrow. That blood-drinking arrow fell between my collarbones, king, and
went with me to the ground, king of kings. Thinking me dead, bull among the
Bharatas, Rama roared aloud like a cloud and rejoiced again and again. When I
had fallen like that, king, Rama, full of joy, gave a great shout with his
followers. But the Kauravas who stood beside me, and the people who had come to
see the battle, were deeply distressed when I fell.

"Then, as I lay fallen, lion among kings, I saw eight brahmins bright as the sun
and fire. They stood all round me and held me up in their arms in the middle of
the battle. Guarded by those brahmins, I did not touch the ground. I stayed in
the air, held by those brahmins as if by kinsmen, as if asleep in the air,
sprinkled with drops of water. Then the brahmins, holding me, said all together,
'Do not be afraid,' and again and again, 'May it go well with you.' Refreshed by
their words, I rose at once and saw my mother, the best of rivers, standing in
my chariot. The great river had held my horses in the battle, lord of the
Kauravas. I honoured my mother's feet and mounted my chariot. She guarded my
chariot, my horses and my gear. I joined my palms and sent her away again.

"Then I drove those wind-swift horses myself and fought Jamadagnya as the day
was ending, Bharata. Then, best of the Bharatas, I loosed at Rama a swift,
mighty arrow that pierced the heart. Overcome by the force of the arrow, Rama
fell to the ground, dropping his bow, on his knees, and fainted. When Rama, the
giver of many thousands, had fallen, clouds covered the sky, pouring much blood.
Meteors fell by the hundred, with thunderclaps and quakes, and Svarbhanu
suddenly covered the blazing sun. Harsh winds blew and the earth shook.
Vultures, badas and herons flew about gladly. In the burning quarter a jackal
howled terribly again and again. Drums sounded loudly without being struck.
These dreadful portents came when great Rama lay on the ground, all but
senseless, best of the Bharatas.

"Then the sun, its circle of rays grown dim, set, deep in a mass of dust. Night
came on, with pleasant, cool winds, and we withdrew from the battle. So there
was a pause, king, and then at first light the battle began again, terrible.
Morning after morning for twenty days, and then three days more."

**Divergences.**

* **The length of the fight.** The CE says the fight went on "morning after morning for twenty days, and then three days more" (5.183.27). See ledger L-187.
* **Rama's return to the fight.** A rejected passage (\*582) has Rama rise at once, take up his bow again, and be stopped by the seers with "No, Rama!". Not narrated here; the CE ends the day with sunset (5.183.26).
* \*581 is not narrated.

**Choices.**

* Bhishma's narration is in double quotes, and the speech in it in single quotes.
* 5.179.3 *baḍa*: a kind of carrion bird, left as a name.
* 5.180.3–4: Rama's chariot of the earth, the Vedas, the wind and "the mothers of the Veda" is his image. The "mothers of the Veda" are taken as the sacred metres.
* 5.180.8 *baddhagodhāṅgulitravān*: "he wore the lizard-skin finger guards".
* 5.180.12 *bāṇapāteṣu triṣu*: "three arrow-shots away".
* 5.180.20: the number of arrows is the text's, *ṣaṣṭyā śataiś ca navabhiḥ*, read as nine hundred and sixty.
* 5.180.23 *nirmaryāde hy api tvayi*: "though you have no bounds".
* 5.180.25: Bhishma's saying that a brahmin becomes a kshatriya by raising weapons is his.
* 5.181.2 *snātopavṛttaiḥ*: "bathed and rolled".
* 5.181.11 *guhyakāstreṇa*: "the weapon of the guhyakas".
* 5.181.15 *gorutam*: "a good way". *goruta* is a measure of distance, how far a cow's call carries.
* 5.181.32: the fire born of the wind, the sun and the heat is the text's.
* 5.181.34: the number words for the arrows (*śatasahasra*, *prayuta*, *arbuda*, *ayuta*, *kharva*, *nikharva*) are given in plain English.
* 5.182.12 *haihayeśapramāthī*: "the crusher of the lord of the Haihayas", Rama, who killed Kartavirya.
* 5.182.15 *brahmarāśiḥ*: "that heap of Brahman", Rama.
* 5.183.8 *rudhirāśanaḥ*: "blood-drinking".
* 5.183.12: the eight brahmins are not named here. See the next chapter.
* 5.183.16 *tathārṣṭiṣeṇaṃ ratham abhyaroham*: "and mounted my chariot". *ārṣṭiṣeṇam* is obscure, and is not rendered.
* 5.183.21 *bhūrisahasrade*: "the giver of many thousands", for Rama's gifts to the brahmins.
* 5.183.22: Svarbhanu is Rahu, who swallows the sun in eclipse.
