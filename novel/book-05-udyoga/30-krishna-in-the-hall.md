# Krishna in the Hall

When all the kings were seated and silent, Krishna, with his fine teeth and his
voice like a drum, began to speak. Like a cloud at the end of the hot season,
Madhava made the whole hall hear him. He looked at Dhritarashtra and spoke.

"I have come to try for peace between the Kurus and the Pandavas, Bharata,
without the heroes' having to strive for it. King, there is nothing else I need
tell you for your good, for you know all there is to know, tamer of enemies.

"Today this family is the best among all the kings, lord of the earth. It is
rich in learning and conduct, and it has every virtue. Pity, compassion,
kindness and gentleness, Bharata, honesty, patience and truth: these stand out
among the Kurus. In so great a family as this, king, it would be wrong for
anything unworthy to happen, and above all through you. For you are the best one
to restrain the Kurus, best of the Kurus, when they go wrong, my friend, whether
against outsiders or their own.

"Your sons, Kauravya, with Duryodhana at their head, have put dharma and gain
behind them and act cruelly. They are ill-bred and they know no limits. Greed
has robbed them of their minds, and they behave like this towards their own
leading kin. You know this, bull among the Bharatas. This terrible danger has
risen among the Kurus themselves, Kauravya. If it is ignored, it will destroy
the earth. It can be calmed, Bharata, if you want it to be. I do not think peace
is hard to win here, bull among the Bharatas.

"Peace rests with you, king, and with me, lord of the people. Hold back your
sons, Kauravya, and I shall hold back the others. Your sons and their followers
must do as you command, king of kings. It is greatly for their good to stay
under your rule. It is for your good too, king, and for the good of the
Pandavas, while I try for peace, if they follow my advice. See for yourself that
this is flawless, lord of the people, and bring it about. Then the Bharatas, all
united, will be yours, lord of men.

"Stand by dharma and gain, king, protected by the Pandavas. When they are like
that, lord of men, no one could overcome them, however hard he tried. Not even
Indra with the gods could beat you, if you were guarded by the great-souled
Pandavas, let alone the kings. Think who would stand together: Bhishma, Drona,
Kripa, Karna, Vivimshati, Ashvatthama, Vikarna, Somadatta and Bahlika, the
Saindhava, the Kalinga and Sudakshina of Kamboja, Yudhishthira, Bhimasena,
Savyasachin and the twins, mighty Satyaki and the great chariot fighter Yuyutsu.
What man out of his mind would fight them, bull among the Bharatas?

"With the Kurus and the Pandavas together you would be lord of the world again,
and no enemy could touch you, killer of enemies. Kings who are your equals, lord
of the earth, and kings greater than you, will make alliances with you, scorcher
of enemies. Guarded on every side by your sons and grandsons, your brothers,
your fathers and your friends, you will be able to live in happiness. If you put
these men in front, and honour them as you did before, you will enjoy the whole
earth, lord of the earth. With all of these, the Pandavas and your own, Bharata,
you will conquer other enemies. This is your whole interest. And you will enjoy
the land they win for you, scorcher of enemies, if you are at one with your sons
and ministers, lord of men.

"In battle, great king, one sees great slaughter. In slaughter on both sides,
king, what dharma do you see? If the Pandavas are killed in battle, or your
mighty sons, what happiness could you find, king? Tell me that, bull among the
Bharatas. They are all brave and skilled in weapons, and all eager for battle,
the Pandavas and yours. Save them from a great danger. Let us not see all the
Kurus and the Pandavas in battle, the heroes on both sides worn away, chariot
fighters struck down from their chariots by chariot fighters. The kings of the
earth are gathered, best of kings. Once they fall under the sway of anger, they
will destroy these people.

"Save this world, king. Let these people not perish. If you come back to your
true nature, joy of the Kurus, something will be left. They are pure and
generous, modest and noble, and of good birth, and they are each other's allies,
king. Protect them from a great danger. Let these kings meet each other in
friendship, eat and drink together, and go home each to his own house, well
dressed and garlanded and honoured, bull among the Bharatas, their resentments
and their feuds put away, scorcher of enemies. Let the love you had for the
Pandavas be yours again now, bull among the Bharatas, now that the end of your
life has come, and let it last.

"They were children when they lost their father, and you brought them up. Care
for them, bull among the Bharatas, as is right, and for your sons. You above all
must protect them in trouble. Do not let your dharma and your gain be lost, bull
among the Bharatas.

"The Pandavas greet you, king, and ask for your favour, and they say this to
you. 'At your command we have suffered, with our followers. We have lived these
twelve years in the forest, and the thirteenth year among people, unknown. We
were sure our father would keep to our agreement. We did not break it, sir, and
the brahmins know it. Keep to the agreement with us, bull among the Bharatas,
for we have kept to it. We have always suffered, king. Let us have our share of
our own kingdom. You know dharma and gain. You should protect us properly. We
look to you as our elder, and we endure many hardships. Be to us as a mother and
a father. The conduct of a pupil to a teacher is weighty, Bharata. If we have
gone astray, our father should set us right. Set us back on the road, king, and
keep to your own path.'

"And your sons, bull among the Bharatas, say this to the assembly. 'In an
assembly of men who know dharma, nothing unworthy should happen. Where dharma is
killed by adharma, and truth by falsehood, while the members of the assembly
look on, it is they who are killed. When dharma, pierced by adharma, comes into
an assembly, and they do not pull out the dart, the members themselves are
pierced. Dharma tears them down, as a river tears down the trees on its banks.
Those who see dharma and sit in silent thought have spoken truth and dharma and
what is right, bull among the Bharatas.'

"What else can be said to you, lord of men, but that you should give? Or let the
lords of the earth who sit in the assembly speak, when they have weighed dharma
and gain, if what I say is true. Free these kshatriyas from the noose of death,
bull among the kshatriyas. Be calm, best of the Bharatas. Do not give way to
anger. Give the Pandavas their fair share of their father's kingdom. Then enjoy
your pleasures with your sons, scorcher of enemies, with all your aims achieved.

"You know that Ajatashatru always stands in the dharma of the good. You know how
he behaves towards you and your sons, lord of men. He was burned and thrown out,
and still he came back to you for refuge. You and your son sent him to live at
Indraprastha. Living there, he brought all the kings under his power and made
them look to you, king, and he never went beyond you. While he was behaving like
this, Saubala, who wanted to take his kingdoms, his wealth and his grain, used
the greatest of tricks against him. He came to that state, and he saw Krishnaa
brought into the hall, and still Yudhishthira, whose soul is beyond measure, did
not waver from the kshatriya's dharma.

"I want what is good for you and for them, Bharata. Do not destroy your people's
dharma, gain and happiness, king. Restrain your sons, lord of the people, who
have gone too far in greed and who think harm is gain and gain is harm. The sons
of Pritha, tamers of enemies, are ready to serve you, and ready to fight. Stand
by what is best for you, king, scorcher of enemies."

All the kings honoured his words in their hearts. No one there dared step
forward to speak.

When the great Keshava had spoken, all those in the assembly sat still, the hair
standing on their bodies. All the kings wondered in their minds, "What man could
dare to answer this?"

While all the kings sat silent, Rama, son of Jamadagni, spoke in the Kuru
assembly.

"Hear this one example, king. It is true, and you need not doubt it. When you
have heard it, take what is good, if you think it right.

"There was once a king named Dambhodbhava, who ruled the whole world. He enjoyed
all the earth, so we have heard. Every day, when the night was over, the brave
man rose in the morning, and the great chariot fighter sat and asked brahmins
and kshatriyas, 'Is there anyone better than me, or my equal in battle, whether
shudra, vaishya, kshatriya, or a brahmin who bears arms?' He went about this
earth saying this, drunk with great pride, and thought of no one else.

"Learned brahmins who were not afraid of anything, and not mean, told the king
again and again to stop boasting. But though they told him again and again, he
went on asking the twice-born, proud and drunk with his fortune. So the
brahmins, great ascetics bound by the Vedic vows, burning with anger, said to
the boastful king, 'There are two lions among men whose friendship has lasted
through many births. You will never be their equal, king.'

"When the king heard this, he asked the brahmins again, 'Where are those two
heroes? Where were they born? What do they do, and who are they?'

"'We have heard that they are the ascetics Nara and Narayana,' said the
brahmins. 'They have come to the world of men. Fight them, king. We hear that
the great Nara and Narayana are practising a terrible austerity beyond words on
Gandhamadana.'

"The king could not bear it. He gathered a great army of six divisions and set
out to where the two unbeaten ones were. He went to Gandhamadana, the rough and
terrible mountain, searching, and he found the two unbeaten ascetics. When he
saw the two best of men, thin with hunger and thirst, their veins standing out,
worn by cold, wind and sun, he went up to them, clasped their feet and asked
after their health. They honoured him with roots and fruits, a seat and water,
and asked the king what they could do for him.

"'I have conquered the earth with my arms,' said Dambhodbhava, 'and killed all
my enemies. I have come to this mountain wanting to fight you. Give me this
welcome. I have wanted it for a long time.'

"'This ashram is free from anger and greed, best of kings,' said Nara and
Narayana. 'There is no fighting in this ashram. How could there be weapons here,
or anything crooked? Look for a fight somewhere else. There are many kshatriyas
on earth.'

"But though they said this, he went on talking, Bharata. Though they bore with
him again and again and spoke gently to him, Dambhodbhava wanted a fight and
went on challenging the ascetics. Then Nara took a handful of reeds, Kaurava,
and said, 'Come on, then, fight, you kshatriya who longs for a fight. Take up
all your weapons and draw up your army. I shall cure you of your faith in war
from now on.'

"'If you think that weapon fit for us, ascetic,' said Dambhodbhava, 'I shall
fight you even with that, for I have come for a fight.'

"When he had said this, Dambhodbhava and his soldiers, wanting to kill the
ascetic, covered him on every side with a shower of arrows. But the sage made
nothing of the terrible arrows he shot, which could cut through an enemy's body,
and drove them off with his reeds. Then the unbeaten one let loose at him the
terrible reed weapon, which nothing can counter. It was like a wonder. With his
reeds the sage, who could hit any mark, pierced by his power the eyes, the ears
and the noses of the soldiers. When the king saw the white sky filled with
reeds, he fell at Nara's feet and said, 'Let me be safe!'

"Nara, the refuge of those who seek refuge, said to the king, 'Honour the
brahmins and hold to dharma, and never do this again. Never, in your pride,
insult anyone, lesser or greater, king. That is what is best for you. Be wise,
free from greed and self-importance, self-possessed, self-controlled, patient,
gentle and kind, and protect your people, lord of the earth. You have our leave.
Go in peace, and never behave like this again. Ask the brahmins how they are,
and do it often, at our word.'

"Then the king bowed at the feet of the two great ones and went back to his own
city. And he gathered great merit in dharma. That was a great deed that Nara did
long ago. And Narayana is greater still, by many more virtues.

"So, king, as long as no weapon is set on Gandiva, best of bows, give up your
pride and go to Dhananjaya. Kakudika, Shuka, Naka, Akshisamtarjana, Samtana,
Nartana, Ghora and Asyamodaka, the eighth: all men struck by these die. They
lose their senses and their minds, and they thrash about like madmen. They
sleep, they leap about and they vomit. They pass water again and again, and they
weep and laugh.

"The son of Pritha has virtues beyond counting, and Janardana is greater than he
is. You yourself know Dhananjaya, son of Kunti, better than anyone. Know, great
king, that those two heroes, bulls among men, who were Nara and Narayana, are
Arjuna and Keshava. If you know this and do not doubt me, then set your mind on
what is noble, Bharata, and make peace with the Pandavas. If you think it best
that there should be no split among you, make peace, best of the Bharatas, and
do not set your mind on war. Your family, best of the Kurus, is greatly honoured
on earth. May it stay so. Good fortune to you. Think of your own good."

When he had heard Jamadagnya's words, the blessed seer Kanva also spoke to
Duryodhana in the Kuru assembly.

"Brahma, grandfather of the worlds, does not decay or pass away, and nor do the
blessed seers Nara and Narayana. Of all the Adityas, Vishnu alone is eternal,
unconquerable and undecaying, everlasting, the lord and master. Others die when
their cause comes: the moon and the sun, the earth and the water, the wind, the
fire and space, the planets and the hosts of stars. At the end of the world they
always leave the three worlds, and they all go to destruction, and they are made
again and again. Others die at any moment: men, beasts and birds, and those born
as animals, and all the others said to move in the world of the living. Most
kings, when they have enjoyed their fortune, meet death at the end of their
lives, to reap the fruit of their good and bad deeds.

"So you should make peace here with the son of Dharma. Let the Pandavas and the
Kurus protect the earth. Do not think, Suyodhana, 'I am strong.' The strong are
seen to meet men stronger than they are, bull among men. Among the strong,
strength is not strength, Kaurava. And all the Pandavas are strong, with the
courage of gods.

"There is an old story they tell about this, of Matali, who wanted to give away
his daughter and was looking for a husband for her.

"The king of the three worlds had a charioteer named Matali, whom he valued. In
his family there was one daughter, famous in the world for her beauty. Her name
was Gunakeshi, and she looked like a goddess. In grace and in beauty she
surpassed all other women.

"When Matali and his wife knew that the time had come to give her away, king, he
thought it over, anxious about it. 'Shame on it,' he thought, 'for a daughter to
grow up in the family of men who are proud, famous, rich and strong, and not
light in character. A daughter puts three families of good people in doubt, her
mother's family, her father's, and the one she is given to. I have searched both
the world of gods and the world of men with the eye of the mind, going deep into
them, and no husband pleases me. I have not liked any of the gods, nor of the
sons of Diti, nor the gandharvas, nor men, nor even the many seers, as a
husband.'

"At night Matali took counsel with his wife Sudharma, and set his mind on going
to the world of the nagas. 'Among gods and men I see no husband equal to
Gunakeshi in beauty. There will surely be one among the nagas.'

"When he had said goodbye to Sudharma and walked round her keeping her on his
right, he kissed his daughter on the head and went down into the earth."

**Divergences.**

* **Arjuna's weapons as passions.** Rama names eight weapons of Arjuna's (5.94.38). A rejected line (\*434) explains them as desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride, arrogance, envy and self-importance. The CE gives only the names and their effects (5.94.38–40). The rejected gloss is not narrated.
* **Krishna as maker of the worlds.** A rejected passage (\*435) adds that Krishna is the maker of all worlds, and asks who could beat Arjuna, whose kinsman he is. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*424–\*433, which repeat or add courtesies, give Nara more words, and have the Pandavas offer the same conduct to Dhritarashtra as he to them (\*428).
