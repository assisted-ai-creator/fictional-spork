# Krishna Will Go

When Sanjaya had gone back, Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, spoke to the
Dasharha, the bull of all the Satvatas.

"Janardana, the time of need for my friends has come. I see no one but you who
could carry us through our troubles. Relying on you, Madhava, we have no fear in
asking proud, deluded Dhritarashtra's son and his counsellors for our own share.
As you protect the Vrishnis in every trouble, tamer of enemies, so you must
protect the Pandavas. Save us from this great fear."

"Here I am, strong-armed one," said the Lord. "Tell me what you want to say. I
will do everything you tell me, Bharata."

"You have heard what Dhritarashtra and his son mean to do," said Yudhishthira.
"All that Sanjaya told me is Dhritarashtra's view, Krishna. It is his very self,
laid open. An envoy says what he was told to say. If he said otherwise, he would
deserve death. Greedy, with an evil mind, behaving unequally to his own, he
seeks peace with us without giving back the kingdom. We lived twelve years in
the forest and one year in disguise at Dhritarashtra's command, lord, believing
that Dhritarashtra would keep to the agreement with us. We did not break the
agreement, Krishna. Our brahmins know that.

"The old king Dhritarashtra does not see his own dharma. Or if he sees it, out
of greed for his son he follows the command of that fool. Following Suyodhana's
opinion, the king behaves falsely to us, Janardana, being greedy and acting as
if for his own good. What could be more painful than this, that I cannot provide
for my mother, Janardana, or for my friends?

"With the Kashis, the Chedis, the Panchalas and the Matsyas, and with you as our
protector, Madhusudana, I asked for five villages: Kushasthala, Vrikasthala,
Asandi and Varanavata, Govinda, and for the fifth any one at all. 'Give us five
villages, or towns, my son, where we may live together, and let the Bharatas not
perish.' But the evil-minded son of Dhritarashtra will not agree even to those.
He thinks he is the owner of it all. What could be more painful than that?

"When an old man born in a good family grows greedy for others' wealth, greed
kills his wisdom. Wisdom, when it is killed, kills shame. Shame, when it is
killed, attacks dharma. Dharma, when it is killed, kills fortune. And fortune,
when it is killed, kills the man. For a man, having nothing is death. Kinsmen,
friends and priests turn away from a man who has nothing, as birds turn away
from a tree without flowers or fruit. This too is death, my son, that kinsmen
turn away from him as if he had fallen, as the breaths leave a dead body.

"Shambara said there is no worse state than one in which no meal can be seen,
not today and not tomorrow. They say wealth is the highest dharma. Everything
rests on wealth. Men with wealth live in the world, and men without wealth are
dead. Those who rely on their own strength and drag a man down from his wealth
destroy his dharma, his gain and his pleasure, and the man himself. Some people
who come to this state have chosen death. Some have left for a village, some for
the forest, some for their own ruin. Some go mad. Others fall into the power of
their enemies. Some go into slavery to others for the sake of wealth.

"For a man, misfortune is worse than death, for the loss of fortune is the loss
of the means of dharma and pleasure. The death that is right by dharma for him
is the eternal road of the world, all around all beings. No one passes beyond
it. A man who is poor by nature does not suffer so much, Krishna, as a man who
has known good fortune and lived in comfort and then lost it. Then, come into
great trouble through his own fault, he blames the gods and Indra, and never
himself. All the teachings cannot get him past his complaint. He grows angry
with his servants and resents his friends. Then anger comes on him, and he is
more confused than ever. Fallen into the power of delusion, he turns to cruel
deeds. He breeds a mixing of classes that only leads to evil, and the mixing
leads to hell. That is the end of evil-doers. If he does not wake up, Krishna,
he goes to hell. His waking is wisdom alone. The man whose eye is wisdom is not
lost.

"When a man gains wisdom, he looks to the teachings. Keeping to the teachings,
he keeps to dharma, and shame is its best part. The modest man hates evil, and
his fortune grows. A man is a man only so long as he has fortune. He is always
devoted to dharma, calm in himself, always bearing the yoke of his work. He does
not set his mind on adharma or go into evil. The shameless man, or the deluded
one, is neither woman nor man. He has no right to dharma, any more than a
shudra. The modest man protects the gods, the ancestors and himself. By that he
goes to deathlessness. That is the end of those who do good.

"You have seen all this in me with your own eyes, Madhusudana, how I have fallen
from my kingdom and live in these lodgings. We cannot give up our fortune by any
fair means. If we are killed in trying for it, that too is good. Our first
choice, Madhava, is that we and they should be at peace, equal, and enjoy that
fortune together. The last extreme, which rises from terrible deeds and
destruction, is that we should kill the Kauravas and enjoy those kingdoms. Even
enemies who are no kin, Krishna, ignoble ones, should not be killed. How much
less people like these? Most of them are our kin, our allies and our elders.
Killing them would be very evil. What good is there in war?

"This dharma of the kshatriya is evil, and we are born kshatriyas. It is our own
dharma, whether it is dharma or adharma. Any other way of life is blamed. The
shudra serves, the vaishyas live by trade, and we live by killing. Brahmins have
chosen the begging bowl. Kshatriya kills kshatriya, fish lives on fish, and dog
kills dog, Dasharha. See how dharma has come down to us.

"In war, Krishna, there is always strife, and lives are lost in battle. Strength
is only a matter of policy. In force, victory and defeat lie. The life and death
of creatures do not come at their own will. Nor can happiness or sorrow be had
out of their time, best of the Yadus. One man kills many, and many kill one. A
coward kills a hero, a man without fame kills a famous one. Victory and defeat
are seen on both sides, and loss is seen too. In flight there is destruction and
waste. War is evil in every way. Who strikes and is not struck back? For the man
who is killed, Hrishikesha, victory and defeat are the same. I think defeat is
no better than death. And even the one who wins, Krishna, is sure to suffer
loss. In the end other men kill someone he loves. Then, Krishna, weakened, not
seeing his sons and brothers, he loses all heart for life.

"It is the heroes, the modest, the noble and the compassionate who are killed in
battle, and lesser men escape. Even after killing enemies, Janardana, regret is
always there. Evil follows it, and someone is always left. The one who is left
gathers strength and does not leave anyone left. He works to destroy everything,
to make an end of the enmity. Victory breeds enmity. The defeated man lives in
sorrow. The man at peace, who has put aside victory and defeat, sleeps happily.
But the man in whom enmity has been born always sleeps badly, with an unquiet
mind, like a man with a snake in the house. The man who destroys everything is
cut off from fame. He wins lasting ill fame among all beings.

"Enmities do not die down, even after a long time. There are always people to
tell the tale, and a man is born in the family to take it up. Nor is enmity
quieted by enmity, Keshava. It grows all the more, Krishna, like fire with
butter. So there is no peace any other way. There is always a gap to be found in
the end, and for those who look for a gap, this fault never ends. The strong
pain a man's heart feels is human. Peace comes by giving it up, or by turning
the mind away. Or else, Madhusudana, by cutting out the enemy at the root, the
fruit might come to full ripeness. But that would be more cruel.

"Peace by giving up would be no different from death, from the danger and from
the destruction of the enemy and of oneself. We do not want to give it up, and
we do not want the destruction of the family. So the peace that comes by bowing
down is the best. When men try every way, wanting not to fight, war follows when
conciliation is rejected. That is plain, and needs no courage. When conciliation
is rejected, something terrible begins. The wise have seen it in dogs in a cow
pen: wagging tails, growling, barking back, circling, baring teeth, snarling,
and then the fight begins. There, Krishna, the stronger one wins and eats the
meat. It is just the same among men. There is no difference. It is always
fitting for the strong to disregard and oppose the weak, for the weak are the
ones who bow down.

"A father, a king and an old man deserves honour in every way. So Dhritarashtra
is to be honoured and respected, Janardana. But Dhritarashtra's love for his son
is strong, Madhava. He has fallen under his son's control, and he will reject
our bowing down. What do you think is the right thing to do next, Krishna? How
can we not fall short of purpose and of dharma, Madhava? In such hard straits as
these, whom else could I ask, Madhusudana, but you, best of men? Who else is
there like you for us, Krishna, dear to us and wanting our good, knowing the way
of every act and the answer to every question, our friend?"

---

When he said this, Janardana answered the Dharma King. "I will go to the
assembly of the Kurus for the sake of you both. If I could make peace there
without giving up your cause, I would have done a holy deed, king, with great
fruit. I would free the angry Kurus and Srinjayas from the noose of death, the
Pandavas and the sons of Dhritarashtra, and this whole earth."

"It is not my wish, Krishna, that you should go to the Kurus," said
Yudhishthira. "Suyodhana will not do what you say, however well you say it. The
whole of the kshatriya class of the earth is gathered there, under Suyodhana's
control. I do not like the idea of you going down into the middle of them,
Krishna. If harm came to you, Madhava, no wealth would please us, no godhood,
let alone happiness, no, not lordship over all the immortals."

"I know the wickedness of Dhritarashtra's son, great king," said the Lord. "But
we will be beyond blame in the eyes of all the kings in the world. And all the
kings of the earth together are no match for me to stand before me when I am
angry, as other beasts cannot stand before a lion. If they should do anything
improper to me, I am minded to burn up all the Kurus. My going there will never
be in vain, Partha. Our aim may be won, and at the least we will be beyond
blame."

"If that is what you want, Krishna," said Yudhishthira, "go to the Kauravas, and
go safely. I shall see you come back safe, your aim fulfilled. Vishvaksena, go
to the Kurus and bring the Bharatas to peace, lord, so that we may all live
together with glad hearts and good minds. You are our brother and our friend,
dear to Bibhatsu and to me. Our friendship leaves no room for doubt. Go safely,
for our prosperity. You know us and you know the others. You know what we want,
and you know how to speak. Whatever is good for us, Krishna, say it to
Suyodhana. Whatever words are joined with dharma and would bring good, Keshava,
speak them, whether gentle or otherwise."
