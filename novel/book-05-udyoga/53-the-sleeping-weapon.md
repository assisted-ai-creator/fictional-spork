# The Sleeping Weapon

"Then at night, king of kings," said Bhishma, "I bowed my head to the brahmins,
the ancestors, the gods, the beings who roam at night and the night itself, lord
of the people. I lay down on my bed alone and thought in my mind, 'This most
terrible fight of mine with Jamadagnya, so destructive, has gone on for many
days now, and I cannot beat mighty Rama at the head of the battle, the brahmin
Jamadagnya of great strength. If I can beat mighty Jamadagnya, let the gods be
kind and show it to me tonight.'

"Then at night, king of kings, as I slept wounded by arrows on my right side,
towards dawn, those chief brahmins by whom I had been lifted up and held when I
fell from my chariot, and comforted with 'Do not be afraid', came to me in a
dream, great king. They stood round me and spoke. Hear it, upholder of the
Kurus.

"'Get up, son of Ganga, and do not be afraid. There is nothing for you to fear.
We protect you, tiger among men, for you are our own body. Rama Jamadagnya will
never beat you in battle. You will beat Rama in battle, bull among the Bharatas.
You will recognise this dearly loved weapon, for you knew it in an earlier body.
It is Prajapati's, made by Vishvakarman, and it is called Prasvapa, the Sleeper,
Bharata. Not even Rama knows it, nor any man on earth. Remember it, strong-armed
one, and use it with all your force. Rama will not be destroyed by that weapon,
lord of men, and you will never be touched by sin, you who give honour.
Jamadagnya will sleep, overcome by the strength of your arrows. Then, when you
have beaten him, you will wake him again, Bhishma, in battle, with the dear
weapon called Sambodhana, the Waker. Do this, Kauravya, in the morning, from
your chariot. Sleeping and dead are the same, we think. And Rama must never die,
king. So use this Prasvapa that has come to you.' When they had said this, king,
all the best of brahmins vanished, eight of them, all alike in form, all with
shining bodies.

"When the night had passed I woke, Bharata. When I thought of the dream, I was
filled with the greatest joy. Then the battle began between him and me, Bharata,
tumultuous, wonderful, making the hair of all beings stand on end. The Bhargava
rained a shower of arrows on me, and I held it off with a net of arrows,
Bharata. Then the great ascetic, in the greatest fury, with yesterday's anger
still in him, hurled a spear at me. It struck like Indra's thunderbolt, it shone
like Yama's rod, blazing like fire in the battle and licking on every side.
Then, tiger of the Bharatas, it struck me quickly on the shoulder, like a meteor
in the sky, Bharata. Then terrible blood flowed from me, like red earth from a
mountain, where Rama had wounded me, strong-armed one, red-eyed one.

"Then in great anger I loosed at Jamadagnya an arrow like death, like a snake's
poison. Struck by it on the forehead, the hero, the best of brahmins, shone,
great king, like a mountain with a peak. In his fury he turned and fitted an
arrow like Death the destroyer, terrible, a killer of enemies, drawing the bow
hard. The fierce arrow fell on my chest, hissing like a snake, and I fell to the
ground, king, covered in blood. When I came to again, I hurled at wise
Jamadagnya a clean spear, blazing like a thunderbolt. It fell between the arms
of the chief of brahmins, and he was shaken, king, and trembled. Then his friend
Akritavrana, the brahmin of great austerity, embraced him and comforted him in
many ways with kind words.

"When he had been comforted, Rama of the great vow, full of anger and
resentment, brought out the supreme brahma weapon. To counter it I used that
same best of weapons, the brahma, and it blazed as if showing the end of the
age. The two brahma weapons met between us, without reaching Rama or me, best of
the Bharatas. Then only splendour appeared in the sky, and all beings were
stricken, lord of the people. The seers, the gandharvas and the gods were in the
greatest torment, crushed by the splendour of the weapons, Bharata. Then the
earth shook with its mountains, forests and trees, and all beings, scorched,
fell into the deepest despair. The sky blazed, king, and the ten quarters
smoked. The creatures of the sky could not stay in the air. When the world, with
its gods, asuras and rakshasas, cried out, I thought, 'Now is the moment,' and I
wanted to use it, Bharata, the dear Prasvapa weapon, at the word of the speakers
of Brahman. As I thought of it, the weapon came into my mind.

"Then a great uproar rose in the sky, king: 'Bhishma, do not loose the Prasvapa,
joy of the Kurus!' But I fitted that weapon against the son of Bhrigu. As I was
about to use the Prasvapa, Narada said to me, 'Here stand the hosts of the gods
in the sky, Kauravya. They forbid you today. Do not use the Prasvapa. Rama is an
ascetic, devoted to Brahman, a brahmin and your teacher. Do not ever insult him,
Kauravya.' Then I saw those eight speakers of Brahman standing in the sky. They
smiled at me, king of kings, and said gently, 'Do as Narada says, best of the
Bharatas. That is the best thing for the worlds, bull among the Bharatas.'

"So I withdrew the sleeping weapon in battle and kindled the brahma weapon in
that battle, as the rule is. Then Rama, prince, angry at seeing that weapon
withdrawn, suddenly let out these words: 'I am beaten by Bhishma, fool that I
am.' Then Jamadagnya saw his father, and his father's father, and another before
him. They stood round him and spoke to him gently then.

"'Never do such a rash thing again, my son, as to go to battle with Bhishma,
least of all with a kshatriya. War is the dharma of the kshatriya, son of
Bhrigu. Study and the keeping of vows are the brahmins' greatest wealth. At one
time, for a certain reason, we told you to bear arms, a very fierce thing, and
you did that work. My son, enough of this struggle with Bhishma in battle,
strong-armed one. Go away from this battle. Enough of your carrying a bow, good
fortune to you. Put it aside, you whom no one can face, and practise austerity,
Bhargava. Here is Bhishma, son of Shantanu, held back by all the gods. They have
urged him, "Turn back from this battle," and told him again and again, "Do not
fight Rama, your teacher. You cannot rightly beat Rama in battle, upholder of
the Kurus. Pay honour to the brahmin on the battlefield, son of Ganga."

"'But we are your elders, and so we hold you back. Bhishma is one of the Vasus.
By good fortune you are alive, my boy. Ganga's son, the son of Shantanu, is a
famous Vasu. How could you beat him in battle, Rama? Turn back. Arjuna, the best
of the Pandavas, the mighty son of Purandara, Nara, the eternal god of old,
Prajapati, the hero famous in the three worlds as Savyasachin, has been
appointed by the Self-born to be Bhishma's death, when the time comes.'

"When his ancestors said this, Rama answered them, 'I will not turn back in
battle. That is the vow I have made. I have never turned back at the head of a
battle. Turn back the son of Apaga from the battle, if you like, grandfathers.
But I will not turn back from this battle in any way.'

"Then the sages, led by Richika, came together with Narada, king, and said to
me, 'Turn back from the battle, my friend. Honour the best of the twice-born.'
And I said 'No' to them, looking to the kshatriya's dharma. 'This is my vow in
the world, that I will never turn my face from battle and turn back, struck by
arrows from behind. Not from greed, nor weakness, nor fear, nor for gain would I
give up the eternal dharma. That is my firm resolve.'

"Then all the sages, led by Narada, king, and my mother Bhagirathi came into the
middle of the battle. With my arrow in hand and my bow, and my resolve as firm
as ever, I stood ready to fight in the battle. Then they came together again and
spoke to Rama, the son of Bhrigu, in the battle. 'The hearts of brahmins are not
made of fresh butter. Make peace, Bhargava. Rama, Rama, turn back from this
battle, best of the twice-born. Bhishma cannot be killed by you, nor you by
Bhishma, Bhargava.' Saying this, they all blocked the field of battle, and the
ancestors made the son of Bhrigu lay down his weapons.

"Then I saw those eight speakers of Brahman again, shining like eight planets
risen. They spoke to me with affection as I stood on the battlefield. 'Go to
Rama, strong-armed one, your teacher, and do the world good.' When I saw that
Rama had turned back at his friends' words, I too took their advice, for the
good of the worlds. Then I went to Rama and bowed to him, badly wounded as I
was. And Rama, the great ascetic, smiled with love and said to me, 'There is no
kshatriya like you on the earth, Bhishma. You may go. I am very pleased with you
in this battle.'

"Then, in front of me, the Bhargava called the girl and spoke to her sadly among
the ascetics. 'All the worlds have seen, spirited one, how I have shown great
manhood with all my might. I cannot beat Bhishma, the best of those who bear
weapons, in battle, even showing my best weapons. This is my utmost power, this
is my utmost strength. Go where you please, good lady. What else can I do for
you? Go to Bhishma himself for refuge. There is no other refuge for you. For I
have been beaten by Bhishma as he loosed great weapons.'

"When great-minded Rama had said this, he sighed and was silent. Then the girl
spoke to the son of Bhrigu. 'My lord, it is just as you say, my lord. This
Bhishma, of noble mind, cannot be beaten in battle, even by the gods. You have
done my business as far as you could, with all your effort, holding nothing back
in battle, and with every kind of weapon. But he cannot be beaten in battle in
the end. And I will never go to Bhishma again. I shall go, you who are rich in
austerity, where I myself shall bring Bhishma down in battle, upholder of the
Bhrigus.' When she had said this, the girl went away, her eyes rolling with
rage, resolved on austerity and thinking of my death.

"Then Rama, the best of the Bhrigus, took leave of me and went back to Mahendra
with those sages, as he had come, Bharata. Then I mounted my chariot, and
praised by the twice-born I went into the city and told my mother Satyavati
everything as it had happened, great king. She welcomed me. I ordered wise men
to see to the matter of the girl. Day after day they brought me news of where
she went, what she said and what she did, working for my good. As soon as the
girl went to the forest, set on austerity, I was troubled and wretched, as if my
mind had left me. For no kshatriya could beat me in battle by his courage, my
son, except a knower of Brahman, strict in his vows through austerity. I told
this to Narada too, king, and to Vyasa, out of fear. Both of them said to me,
'You should not despair over the daughter of Kashi, Bhishma. Who can turn back
fate by human effort?'

"That girl, great king, went into a circle of ashrams on the bank of the Yamuna
and practised austerity beyond the human. Without food, thin, rough, with matted
hair and caked with dirt, she lived six months on air, standing like a post,
rich in austerity. Reaching the bank of the Yamuna, the spirited girl then spent
another year living in the water without food. Another year she lived on a
single fallen leaf, standing on the tip of her big toe, fierce in her anger. So
for twelve years she heated heaven and earth. Her kin tried to turn her back,
but they could not.

"Then she went to Vatsabhumi, where siddhas and charanas live, to the ashram of
holy ascetics of great soul. There, in holy places, bathing her body day and
night, the girl of Kashi wandered as she pleased. At Nanda's ashram, great king,
then at Uluka's lovely ashram, at Chyavana's ashram and at Brahma's own place,
at Prayaga, the gods' place of sacrifice, in the forests of the gods, at
Bhogavati, king, and at Kaushika's ashram, at Mandavya's ashram, king, and at
Dilipa's ashram, at Ramahrada, Kauravya, and at Pailagargya's ashram: at these
bathing places the girl of Kashi bathed her limbs, lord of the people,
practising fierce austerity.

"My mother, rising from the water, said to her, Kauraveya, 'Why do you torment
yourself, good lady? Tell me the truth.' The blameless girl joined her palms and
said to her, king, 'Bhishma was not beaten by Rama in battle, lovely-eyed one.
Who else could dare to beat that king when he raises his arrow? So I shall
practise very terrible austerity for Bhishma's destruction. I roam the earth,
goddess, so that I may kill the king. Let the fruit of this vow be mine in
another body.'

"Then the goddess who goes to the ocean said, 'You walk a crooked path, spirited
one. You cannot win this wish, weak one, lovely one. If you practise this vow
for Bhishma's destruction, daughter of Kashi, and if you give up your body in
the middle of your vow, you will become a river, lovely one, crooked, with water
only in the rains, hard to cross and hard to know, flowing in the rains and not
for eight months, full of terrible crocodiles, dreadful and frightening to all
beings.' When she had said this, king, my fortunate mother, the spirited one,
turned away from the girl of Kashi, almost smiling.

"Sometimes in the eighth month, sometimes in the tenth, the lovely girl would
not even drink water. The daughter of the lord of Kashi, running about here and
there in Vatsabhumi in her longing for holy places, fell. She became a river in
Vatsabhumi, known as Amba, Bharata, flowing in the rains, full of crocodiles,
hard to cross and crooked. By that austerity, king, the girl was born with half
her share. She became a river among the Vatsas, and she remained a girl too."

**Divergences.**

* **Who won.** Rama himself says, "I am beaten by Bhishma" (5.186.8) and "I have been beaten by Bhishma as he loosed great weapons" (5.187.4). His ancestors and the sages stop the fight, and tell Rama that Bhishma cannot be killed by him, nor he by Bhishma (5.186.29). See ledger L-187.
* **Arjuna, Bhishma's death.** Rama's ancestors say that Arjuna "has been appointed by the Self-born to be Bhishma's death, when the time comes" (5.186.19–20). This is CE.
* **The river Amba.** Ganga warns Amba that if she dies in her vow she will become a crooked seasonal river (5.187.33–35). She does, in Vatsabhumi, but "by that austerity" only half of her; she remains a girl too (5.187.39–40). See ledger L-188.
* \*583, which says the weapon will come to Bhishma of itself, is not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.184.9 *svaśarīraṃ hi no bhavān*: "you are our own body". The eight brahmins are not named. Their words suggest the eight Vasus, of whom Bhishma is one (5.186.17–18), but the text does not say so here.
* 5.184.12 *prasvāpam*: the Prasvapa, "the Sleeper"; 5.184.15 *saṃbodhana*, the Sambodhana, "the Waker".
* 5.186.9: Rama's "father, and his father's father, and another before him". His father is Jamadagni; the sages who speak next are "led by Richika" (5.186.23). They are left unnamed here, as the text leaves them.
* 5.186.12 *idaṃ nimitte kasmiṃś cid asmābhir upamantritam*: "At one time, for a certain reason, we told you to bear arms". The reason is not given here. Book 3 tells of Rama's slaughter of the kshatriyas after his father was killed, and of Richika stopping him (3.117.6–10).
* 5.186.19 *naraḥ prajāpatir … pūrvadevaḥ*: "Nara, the eternal god of old, Prajapati", the ancestors' titles for Arjuna.
* 5.186.29 *nāvanītaṃ hi hṛdayaṃ viprāṇām*: "The hearts of brahmins are not made of fresh butter", read as a plea not to be hard-hearted; the sense is uncertain.
* 5.187.16 *bhayāt*: "out of fear", Bhishma's own word.
* 5.187.25–27: the ashrams and bathing places are named as the text names them.
* 5.187.33 *sāgaragā*: "the goddess who goes to the ocean", Ganga.
* 5.187.35 *nāṣṭamāsikī*: "not for eight months", that is, dry for most of the year.
* 5.187.40 *bhāgārdhena vyajāyata*: "was born with half her share".
