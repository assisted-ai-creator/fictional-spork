# The Swan and the Sadhyas

"On this," said Vidura, "they tell an old story, the talk of Atreya and the
Sadhyas, as we have heard it. Long ago the Sadhya gods questioned the great
seer, wise and firm in his vows, as he wandered in the form of a swan.

"'We are the Sadhya gods, great seer,' they said. 'When we look at you we cannot
guess what you are. We think you steady and wise with learning. Speak to us,
noble one, words of wisdom.'

"'This is the work I have heard of, immortals,' said the swan: 'firmness, calm,
and following the dharma of truth. Loosen every knot of the heart, and bring
what is pleasant and unpleasant under the self's control.

"'When a man is abused, he should not abuse in return. The anger he bears burns
up the abuser, and takes his good deeds for himself. He should not abuse, nor
scorn another. He should not betray his friend, nor wait on the low. He should
not be too proud, nor behave basely. He should avoid harsh and wounding speech.
Cruel words burn a man's vital parts, his bones, his heart and his life here. So
the lover of dharma should always avoid harsh and wounding words.

"'The man who stabs others with the thorns of his words, harsh, rough and cruel,
is the most unlucky of people. Know that he carries ruin tied to his mouth. If
another pierces him hard with sharp arrows blazing like fire and sun, the wise
man, cut down or raised up, should know: "He is adding to my good deeds."

"'If a man keeps company with the good or the bad, with an ascetic or with a
thief, he comes under their power, as cloth takes the colour of the dye. The man
who does not start a quarrel nor make another start one, who, when struck, does
not strike back nor make another strike, who wishes no harm even to one who
wants to kill him: the gods long for his coming.

"'They say silence is better than speech. To speak the truth is the second. To
speak kindly is the third. To speak what is right is the fourth. A man becomes
like those he argues with, like those he serves, and like what he wishes to be.
From whatever a man turns away, from that he is freed. By turning away from
everything he does not know the least sorrow.

"'He is not conquered, and he does not seek to conquer others. He makes no
enemies and does not strike back. He is the same in blame and praise. He does
not grieve, nor does he exult. He wishes all beings well, and never sets his
mind on their harm. He is truthful, gentle and self-controlled. That is the best
of men.

"'He does not comfort with empty words. He promises, and gives. He knows the
right and wrong of others. That is the middling man.

"'He is harsh and violent, and will not be taught. In his anger he does not turn
back. He is ungrateful. He is no one's friend, and his heart is wicked. These
are the marks of the lowest man here. He does not believe good even of others,
for he suspects himself. He drives his friends away. That is the lowest man.

"'A man who wants his own good should serve the best, and the middling when the
time comes, but should not serve the lowest. A man may get wealth by the power
of the bad, by constant effort, by wisdom and by manliness. But he never wins
true praise, nor the conduct of great families.'"

---

"The gods long for great families," said Dhritarashtra, "and so do those grown
old in dharma and profit, and the learned. I ask you this, Vidura: what are the
great families?"

"Austerity, self-control, knowledge of Brahman, sacrifices, pure marriages, and
the constant giving of food," said Vidura. "The families in which these seven
virtues are found, and whose conduct is good, are the great families. Those
whose conduct does not waver and whose birth does not trouble them, who practise
dharma with a glad heart, who want a fame that sets their family apart, and who
have given up lies: those are the great families.

"Families fall from being families by not sacrificing, by wrong marriages, by
neglecting the Veda, and by breaking dharma. Families fall from being families
by destroying what belongs to the gods, by stealing what belongs to brahmins,
and by going against brahmins. Families fall from being families by scorning and
slandering brahmins, Bharata, and by stealing what was left in trust. Families
rich in cattle, men and horses are not counted as families if their conduct is
poor. But families whose conduct is not poor, though they have little wealth,
are counted as families, and win great fame.

"Let no one in our family make enemies. Let no king's minister steal others'
goods. Let there be no one who betrays a friend, no cheat, no liar, no one who
eats before the Ancestors, the gods and the guests. Whoever among us kills a
brahmin, or hates brahmins, or ploughs in their fields, let him not come to our
gathering.

"Straw, earth, water, and fourth, kind words: these are never lacking in the
houses of the good. They are offered with the greatest faith, king, to honour
guests, by the righteous who do good deeds, wise one.

"A small chariot, king, can carry a load that other things made of wood from the
earth cannot. In the same way men of great families, so yoked, can bear the
load, and other men cannot.

"He is no friend whose anger you fear, or whom you must serve with suspicion.
The friend in whom you can trust as in a father is a friend. The others are only
acquaintances. Even a man who is no kin, if he behaves as a friend, is kin, and
friend, and refuge, and support.

"A man whose mind is fickle, who does not wait on the old, whose judgement
always wavers, never keeps his friends. Wealth passes by the fickle man who is
not his own master and follows his senses, as swans pass by a dry lake. It is
the nature of the wicked to be angry for no reason and pleased for no cause,
like a wandering cloud. Even the eaters of flesh will not eat the corpses of the
ungrateful, who are honoured by their friends and have their purposes served,
and yet do not stand by their friends.

"A man should go to his friends with requests, whether he has wealth or not.
Unless he asks, he does not learn whether they are solid or hollow.

"Grief takes away beauty. Grief takes away strength. Grief takes away knowledge.
Grief brings sickness. What cannot be had is not won by grieving. It only
torments the body, and enemies rejoice. Do not set your mind on grief.

"A man dies and is born again. A man falls and rises again. A man begs, and is
begged from again. A man grieves, and is grieved for again. Pleasure and pain,
being and not being, gain and loss, death and life, all touch us here in turn.
So a steady man should not exult, and should not grieve.

"These six senses are restless. Wherever any of them goes, a man's understanding
flows out through it, as water always runs from a cracked pot."

---

"The king is like a slender flame," said Dhritarashtra, "and I have dealt
falsely with him. He will make an end of my foolish sons in war. All this is
always troubled. My mind is always troubled. Tell me, wise one, of the place
that is free from trouble."

"I see no peace for you, blameless one," said Vidura, "except in learning and
austerity, in holding back the senses, and in giving up greed. A man drives away
fear by understanding. He wins greatness by austerity, knowledge by serving his
teacher, and peace by letting go. Those who seek freedom go about here without
depending on the merit of gifts or the merit of the Veda, free of love and hate.
At the end of good study, a good fight, a good deed and good austerity,
happiness grows.

"Those who are divided never find sleep, king, even in beds well spread. They
find no joy in women, nor in the praise of bards and heralds. The divided never
practise dharma. The divided never find happiness here. The divided do not
honour what deserves honour. The divided do not care for peace. Good advice does
not please them, and they cannot keep what they have or get what they lack. For
the divided, lord of men, there is no end but ruin.

"Wealth is to be looked for in cows, austerity in a brahmin, fickleness in
women, and danger from kin. Threads stretched out long, many threads together,
bear many strains by their number. That is the image of the good. Firebrands
smoke when they are apart, Dhritarashtra, and blaze when they are together. So
it is with kin, bull of the Bharatas. Those who are brave against brahmins,
women, kin and cows fall, Dhritarashtra, like ripe fruit from the stalk.

"A single tree, however great, strong and well rooted, can be forced down by the
wind, trunk and branches. But trees that stand together, many of them, well
rooted, bear the strongest winds, leaning on each other. In the same way enemies
think they can overcome one man alone, even with every virtue, as the wind
overcomes a tree that stands alone. Kin grow by upholding one another and
leaning on one another, like lotuses in a pool.

"These must not be killed: brahmins, cows, women, children, kin, those whose
food one has eaten, and those who come for refuge.

"Bless you: there is no virtue in a man, even a rich one, apart from health. The
sick are like the dead. Anger is a bitter headache that no sickness causes,
bound to sin, harsh, sharp and fierce. The good drink it down, and the bad do
not. Drink down your anger, great king, and be calm. Those racked by sickness
take no pleasure in fruits, and find no taste in the things of the senses. The
sick are always full of pain. They know neither wealth nor enjoyment nor
happiness.

"Long ago I told you, king, when I saw Draupadi won at the dice, and you did not
do as I said: 'Stop Duryodhana at the gaming board. The wise avoid gambling.'
That is not strength which fights against gentleness. Dharma mixed with force is
to be practised. Fortune gathered by cruelty perishes. Fortune grown by
gentleness passes to sons and grandsons.

"Let the sons of Dhritarashtra protect the Pandavas, and let the sons of Pandu
protect your sons. Let the Kurus have the same enemies and the same friends, and
one counsel, and live happy and prosperous, king. You are the pillar of the
Kauravas today, Ajamidha. The Kuru line depends on you. The Parthas are young,
and they have been scorched by their forest exile. Protect them, my father, and
guard your own good name. Make peace between the Kauravas and the sons of Pandu.
Do not let your enemies look for an opening. They all stand by the truth, god
among men. Hold back Duryodhana, lord of men."

---

"Manu, son of the Self-Existent, spoke of these seventeen men, king of kings,"
said Vidura, "son of Vichitravirya, who strike at the sky with their fists. He
said they are like men who try to bend Indra's bow, which cannot be bent, and
the rays of the sun, which cannot be bent.

"There is the man who instructs one who cannot be taught, and the man who is too
easily angry. There is the man who serves his enemy too much, and the man who
does not guard his women, bless you. There is the man who asks what should not
be asked, and the man who boasts. There is the well-born man who does what
should not be done, and the weak man always at war with the strong. There is the
man who speaks to one who has no faith, and the man who wants what should not be
wanted, lord of men. There is the father-in-law who makes jokes with his
daughter-in-law, and the man who lives on his daughter-in-law and still wants
honour. There is the man who sows his seed in another's field, and the man who
speaks too ill of a woman. There is the man who receives a gift and says, 'I do
not remember it,' and the man who gives and then boasts of it when asked. And
there is the man who courts the wicked with soft words. These follow the wind,
noose in hand.

"As a man behaves towards another, so should he be treated. That is dharma. The
deceitful should be met with deceit, and good conduct with goodness."

"Every Veda says that a man lives a hundred years," said Dhritarashtra. "Why
then does he not reach his full span here?"

"Excessive talk, excessive pride, and lack of generosity, king," said Vidura,
"anger, too much craving to know, and betrayal of friends: these six are sharp
swords that cut short the lives of embodied beings. These kill men, not death.
Bless you.

"These are all equal to the killers of brahmins: the man who goes to the wife of
one who trusted him, and the man who defiles his teacher's bed; the twice-born
man who marries a shudra woman, and the drinker, Bharata; and the man who kills
one who came for refuge. The scripture says one should do penance after meeting
them.

"The learned householder who is generous, whose words are not scorned, who eats
what is left after others have eaten, who harms no one, does nothing useless,
has given up quarrels, is grateful, truthful and gentle, goes to heaven.

"Men who speak pleasantly are always easy to find, king. But the man who says
what is unpleasant and wholesome, and the man who will hear it, are hard to
find. The man who stands on dharma and, setting aside what his master likes and
dislikes, tells him unpleasant things that are good for him, is the king's true
ally.

"Give up a man for the sake of the family. Give up a family for the sake of the
village. Give up a village for the sake of the country. Give up the earth for
the sake of your self. Keep wealth against misfortune. Guard your wife even with
your wealth. Guard your self always, even with your wife and your wealth.

"Even at the time of the dice I told you, king, that it was not right, son of
Pratipa's line. But like medicine to a sick man, it did not please you, son of
Vichitravirya. With crows, the sons of Dhritarashtra, you would beat these
peacocks with their bright tails, the Pandavas. You pass over lions and hide
jackals. When the time comes you will grieve, lord of men.

"My father, the master who is never angry with a loyal servant who works for his
good is trusted by his servants, and they do not leave him in misfortune. A man
should not cut off his servants' living to try to win over strangers. Ministers
who were fond of him, when they are held back from their due and lose their
comforts, leave him. First reckon up all the work to be done, and your income
and spending, and fix a fitting living. Then gather fitting allies, for hard
things are done with allies.

"The servant who knows his master's mind and does all his work tirelessly, who
says what is good for him, who is devoted and noble and knows his own strength,
should be cared for like one's own self. But the servant who ignores what he is
told when he is instructed, and argues back when he is given a task, proud of
his own wisdom and contrary in speech, should be dismissed at once.

"They say an envoy should have eight qualities: he should not be stiff, nor
cowardly, nor slow. He should be kind, smooth-spoken, not to be bought by
others, free of sickness, and noble in his words.

"A careful man should never go trustingly into another's house at the wrong
time. He should not stand hidden at a crossroads at night. He should not seek a
woman the king desires. He should not contradict a man who sits with a bad
council of ill-matched companions. Nor should he say, 'I do not trust you,' but
give some reason for going.

"A king who is too tender-hearted, a loose woman, a king's servant, a son, a
brother, a widow with a young child, a soldier, and a man whose living has been
cut off: these should be avoided in business dealings.

"Ten virtues attend a man who bathes: strength, beauty, a clear voice and
complexion, soft skin and sweet smell, purity, good fortune, delicacy, and the
finest women. Six virtues attend a man who eats in measure: health, long life,
happiness and strength. His children are sound, and no one mocks him as a
glutton.

"Do not keep these in your house: an idle man, a great eater, a man hated by the
world, a great deceiver, a cruel man, one who knows neither place nor time, and
one whose dress is unseemly. Never ask these for anything, even in great need: a
miser, an abuser, an unlearned man, one born of the low, one who honours those
who deserve no honour, a harsh man, a maker of enemies, and an ungrateful man.
Do not serve these six lowest of men: one whose deeds are troubled, one who
talks too much, one who always lies, one whose loyalty is weak, one whose love
has turned, and one who is too proud.

"Wealth is bound to allies, and allies are bound to wealth. The two are bound to
each other, and neither succeeds without the other.

"When a man has had sons and freed them from debt, and found them some living,
and married all his daughters well, he should go to the forest and live like a
sage. A man of power should do what is good for all beings and brings happiness
to himself. This is the root of success in dharma and profit. How can a man with
understanding, power, energy, strength, effort and resolve fear for his living?

"Look at the evils of a war with the Pandavas, in which even the gods with
Shakra would be troubled: enmity with your sons, a life of constant fear, the
loss of your good name, and your enemies' joy. The anger of Bhishma, of
yourself, who are like Indra, of Drona and of King Yudhishthira, once it grows,
could destroy this world, like a white comet falling slantwise across the sky.
Your hundred sons, Karna and the five Pandavas could rule the whole earth,
clothed in its ocean.

"The sons of Dhritarashtra are a forest, king, and the sons of Pandu are thought
of as tigers. Do not cut down the forest with its tigers. Do not drive the
tigers out of the forest. There would be no forest without tigers, and no tigers
without a forest. The forest is guarded by the tigers, and the woods guard the
tigers.

"The wicked do not want to know the virtues of others as much as they want to
know their faults. A man who wants the highest success in his affairs should
practise dharma from the start. Profit never leaves dharma, as nectar never
leaves the world of heaven. The man whose self has turned from evil and settled
in good has understood all this, nature and its changes. The man who follows
dharma, profit and pleasure each in its season finds the three joined, here and
hereafter. The man who holds back the rush of anger and joy when it rises, and
is not bewildered in misfortune, king, is a vessel for fortune.

"Hear from me the five kinds of strength that men always have. The strength of
the arms is called the least. The gaining of ministers, bless you, is called the
second. The gaining of wealth, those who want victory say, is the third. The
strength a man is born with, king, from his fathers and grandfathers, called the
strength of birth, is the fourth. But the strength by which all these are
gathered together, the best of all strengths, Bharata, is called the strength of
wisdom.

"When a man has made an enemy of one who can do him great harm, he should not
feel safe because he is far away. What wise man would put his trust in women,
kings, snakes, his own study, those who serve his enemies, pleasure, or life?
For a creature struck by the arrow of wisdom there are no doctors and no
medicines, no offerings or spells, no rites of good fortune, no Atharvan charms,
and no proven antidotes.

"A snake, a fire, a lion and a son of good family should not be despised by any
man, Bharata, for they all have great power. Fire, the great power in the world,
lies hidden in wood, and does not use up the wood until others kindle it. But
when it is drawn out of the wood by rubbing and blazes up, it quickly burns up
that wood, and the forest, and more, with its power. Just so, those born in good
families, with power like fire, lie forgiving and without sign, like fire in
wood.

"You and your sons are a creeper, and the sons of Pandu are thought of as shala
trees. A creeper never grows without leaning on a great tree. You and your sons
are a forest, son of Ambika, and know, my father, that the Pandavas are the
lions in it. Without lions the forest would perish, and without the forest the
lions would perish."

Not narrated.
