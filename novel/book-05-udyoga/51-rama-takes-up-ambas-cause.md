# Rama Takes Up Amba's Cause

"'You will see Rama Jamadagnya, my child, in the great forest,'" Bhishma went
on, "as Hotravahana told her, "'practising fierce austerity, true to his word
and very strong. On Mahendra, the best of mountains, seers who know the Veda,
gandharvas and apsarases always attend on Rama. Go there, good fortune to you,
and give him my message. First bow your head to him, old in austerity and firm
in his vows. Then tell him, good lady, what you have to do and what you want.
When you name me, Rama will do all of it for you. Rama, the son of Jamadagni,
the hero, the best of all who bear weapons, is my friend, my child. He loves me,
and he is my friend.'

"While King Hotravahana was saying this to the girl, Akritavrana, Rama's dear
companion, appeared. Then all the sages rose by the thousand, and so did the old
king, Hotravahana the Srinjaya. Then the forest dwellers asked each other's news
as was right, and sat down together around him, best of the Bharatas. They told
all kinds of stories, pleasant, lovely and divine, full of affection, gladness
and joy, lord of kings. At the end of the stories the great royal seer
Hotravahana asked Akritavrana about Rama, the best of great seers. 'Where is
mighty Jamadagnya now, strong-armed Akritavrana? Can the best of those who know
the Veda be seen?'

"'Rama is always talking of you, my lord,' said Akritavrana. 'He says, "The
Srinjaya, the royal seer, is my dear friend," king. I think Rama will be here
tomorrow morning. You will see him come here, wanting to see you. And why has
this girl come to the forest, royal seer? Whose is she, and what is she to you?
I want to know.'

"'This is my daughter's daughter, my lord,' said Hotravahana, 'the lovely
daughter of the king of Kashi. She stood at her bridegroom choice, the eldest,
with her two sisters, blameless one. She is known as Amba, the eldest daughter
of the lord of Kashi. Ambika and Ambalika are the two younger, you who are rich
in austerity. The kshatriyas of the earth gathered in the city of Kashi, and
there was a great festival for the girls, brahmin seer. Then, they say, mighty
Bhishma, son of Shantanu, splendid, threw back the kings and carried off the
three girls. When he had beaten the lords of the earth, Bhishma, whose soul is
pure, went to Gajahvaya with the girls, Bharata. He told Satyavati, and then the
lord gave orders for his brother Vichitravirya's wedding.

"'When this girl saw the wedding being prepared, she spoke to Ganga's son there,
among his ministers, bull among brahmins. "In my heart I chose the lord of the
Shalvas as my husband, hero. You who know dharma should not give me away when my
heart is another's." When Bhishma heard these words, he took counsel with his
ministers, and having decided, and following Satyavati's wish, he let her go.
With Bhishma's leave the girl gladly spoke to Shalva, lord of Saubha, in good
time, brahmin. "I have been let go by Bhishma. Grant me my dharma. I chose you
in my heart before, bull among kings." But Shalva refused her, for he suspected
her virtue. So she has come to the forest of austerities and is set on the
ascetic's life. I recognised her when she told her lineage. She thinks Bhishma
is the cause of this sorrow.'

"'Lord,' said Amba, 'it is just as the lord of the earth has said. Hotravahana
the Srinjaya is my mother's father, the maker of her body. I cannot bring myself
to go back to my own city, you who are rich in austerity, for fear of disgrace
and for shame, great sage. What the lord Rama tells me, best of the twice-born,
that I think is what I must most do, my lord.'

"'Good lady,' said Akritavrana, 'there are two sorrows here. For which do you
want to be avenged, weak one? Tell me, my child. If you think the lord of Saubha
should be pressed, good lady, great Rama will press him, wanting your good. Or
if you want to see Bhishma, the son of Apaga, beaten in battle by wise Rama, the
Bhargava might do that too. Now that you have heard what the Srinjaya said,
bright-smiling one, and what you have said yourself, let what should be done
next be thought over today.'

"'My lord, Bhishma carried me off without knowing,' said Amba. 'For Bhishma does
not know my heart went to Shalva, brahmin. Think it over in your mind and decide
for yourself, and let it be arranged as is right. Do what is fitting, brahmin,
with Bhishma, the tiger of the Kurus, or with the king of the Shalvas, or with
both. I have told you the root of my sorrow as it is. Make your arrangement
properly, my lord.'

"'What you say is fitting, good lady,' said Akritavrana, 'lovely one, since you
speak of dharma. But hear what I have to say. If the son of Apaga had not taken
you to Gajasahvaya, Shalva would have received you with a bow of his head, timid
one, at Rama's urging. But because you were carried off after he beat you,
spirited one, the king of the Shalvas has had his doubts about you,
slender-waisted one. Bhishma is proud of his manhood and flushed with victory.
So it is right that you should have him punished.'

"'That is the great wish that always stirs in my heart too, brahmin,' said Amba,
'that I might have Bhishma killed in battle. Bhishma or the king of the Shalvas,
whichever you find at fault, strong-armed one, punish the one who has made me
suffer so.'

"While they talked like this," said Bhishma, "the day went by, best of the
Bharatas, and the night, with its winds pleasant, cool and warm. Then Rama
appeared, blazing, as it were, with splendour, surrounded by his pupils, king, a
sage wearing matted hair and bark. He carried a bow in his hand, a sword and an
axe, with a spirit that never flagged, free of passion, tiger among kings, and
he came to King Srinjaya. When the ascetics saw him, and the king rich in
austerity, and the ascetic girl, they all stood with joined palms. They honoured
the Bhargava calmly with the honey mixture, and when he had been honoured as was
fitting, he sat down with them. Then the two of them, the royal seer Srinjaya
and Jamadagnya, talked of old times, Bharata.

"At the end of their talk the royal seer spoke in good time to the mighty Rama,
best of the Bhrigus, sweet and meaningful words. 'Rama, this is my daughter's
daughter, the daughter of the king of Kashi, my lord. Hear her business truly,
you who are skilled in business.'

"'Tell me all of it,' Rama said to her. Then she came to Rama, who blazed like
fire. The lovely girl bowed her head to Rama's feet, touched them with her
hands, which were like lotus petals, and stood before him. She wept in her
grief, her eyes clouded with tears, and took refuge with the son of Bhrigu, the
refuge of those who seek it.

"'As you are to this Srinjaya, princess, so you are to me,' said Rama. 'Tell me
the sorrow in your heart. I shall do what you say.'

"'My lord, I have come to you for refuge today, you of the great vow,' said
Amba. 'Lift me out of this terrible sea of the mud of grief, lord.'

"When Rama saw her beauty, her fresh youth and her great tenderness," said
Bhishma, "he fell into thought. 'What will she say?' the best of the Bhrigus
thought, and Rama pondered a long time, filled with pity. 'Tell me,' Rama said
to her again, and the bright-smiling girl told the Bhargava everything, just as
it was. When Jamadagnya had heard the princess's words, he decided what was to
be done and spoke to the lovely girl.

"'I shall send to Bhishma, the best of the Kurus, spirited one. When that lord
of men hears my words, he will do what is right. If Jahnavi's son does not do
what I say, I shall burn him up in battle, good lady, with his ministers, by the
fire of my weapons. Or if your mind turns that way, princess, I shall set the
brave lord of the Shalvas to this business first.'

"'Bhishma let me go as soon as he heard, son of Bhrigu,' said Amba, 'that my
heart had gone to the king of the Shalvas, as I had wished before. I went to the
king of Saubha and spoke words hard to speak. But he would not take me, for he
suspected my virtue. Think all this over with your own mind, son of Bhrigu, and
decide what is fitting to be done. The root of my disaster is Bhishma of the
great vow, who brought me into his power, lifting me up by force then. Kill
Bhishma, strong-armed one, for whose sake I have come to such sorrow. I wander
in the deepest misery, tiger of the Bhrigus. He is greedy and proud and flushed
with victory, Bhargava. So it is right that you should be avenged on him,
blameless one. This was the resolve in my heart, lord, when the Bharata carried
me off then: I would have the man of the great vow killed. So fulfil my wish
today, blameless Rama. Kill Bhishma, strong-armed one, as Purandara killed
Vritra.'

"When she said this to Rama, 'Kill Bhishma,' lord, again and again, weeping and
urging him," said Bhishma, "he answered the girl. 'Kashya, I do not take up a
weapon of my own will, lovely one, except for the sake of those who know
Brahman. What else can I do for you? By words Bhishma and Shalva will both do
what I tell them, queen, blameless one. I shall do that. Do not grieve. But I
will not take up a weapon in any way, spirited one, except at the bidding of
brahmins. That is the agreement I have made.'

"'You must take away my sorrow somehow, my lord,' said Amba. 'But it comes from
Bhishma. Kill him, lord, and quickly.'

"'Kashya, tell me again,' said Rama. 'Though Bhishma deserves to be honoured, he
will clasp both your feet with his head at my word.'

"'Kill Bhishma in battle, Rama, if you want to please me,' said Amba, 'and if
you want to make true what you promised.'

"While Rama and Amba were talking like this, king," said Bhishma, "Akritavrana
said to Jamadagnya, 'You should not abandon a girl who has come to you for
refuge, strong-armed one. Kill Bhishma in battle, Rama, as if he were a roaring
asura. If you challenge Bhishma to battle, Rama, great sage, he will either say
"I am beaten" or do what you say. Then this girl's business will be done, son of
Bhrigu, and your word will be made true, lord. And this was your vow too then,
Rama, great sage, which you promised the brahmins when you had beaten all the
kshatriyas: "If a brahmin, kshatriya, vaishya or shudra is an enemy of Brahman
in battle, I shall kill him," son of Bhrigu. "I can never, while I live, abandon
those who come to me for refuge, afraid and wanting to live. And I shall kill
the proud man who beats the whole kshatriya race gathered in battle," son of
Bhrigu. Bhishma, the upholder of the Kuru family, is such a victor, Rama. Meet
him and fight him in battle, son of Bhrigu.'

"'I remember the vow I made before, best of seers,' said Rama. 'I shall act so
that this will be won by conciliation alone. This is a great matter in the heart
of the girl of Kashi, brahmin. I shall go myself, with the girl, to where he is.
If Bhishma, who glories in battle, will not do what I say, I shall kill the
proud man. That is my firm resolve. For the arrows I let loose do not stick in
the bodies of living things. You know that from the war with the kshatriyas long
ago.'

"When he had said this, great-minded Rama set his mind on going, and rose with
the speakers of Brahman. The ascetics spent the night there, made their fire
offerings and said their prayers, and set out, wanting to kill me. Then Rama
went with those bulls among brahmins and the girl to Kurukshetra, great king,
Bharata. All the great ascetics, with the best of the Bhrigus at their head,
camped there, on the bank of the Sarasvati.

"On the third day he took his stand on level ground, king, and he of the great
vow sent me word: 'I have come.' When I heard that the mighty one, the treasure
of splendour, the lord, had come to the edge of my country, I went quickly and
gladly to meet him, putting a cow at the front, king of kings, surrounded by
brahmins, by priests like gods and by chaplains. When mighty Jamadagnya saw me
come, he accepted my honour and said this.

"'Bhishma, what were you thinking of, that you brought this daughter of the king
of Kashi here against her will and then let her go again? You have made her fall
from winning dharma, high or low. Who could come near her now that you have laid
hands on her? Shalva has refused her because you took her, Bharata. So take her
back, at my command, Bharata. Let the princess have her own dharma, tiger among
men. It is not right for you, a king, to insult her like this, blameless one.'

"When I saw that he was not in a good mood, I said, 'I would never give her to
my brother again, brahmin. She told me before, Bhargava, "I am Shalva's," and
with my leave she went to the city of Saubha. Neither for fear, nor pity, nor
greed, nor for the sake of gain, would I give up the kshatriya's dharma. That is
the vow I have taken.'

"Then Rama, his eyes rolling with anger, said to me, 'If you do not do what I
say, bull of the Kurus, I shall kill you today, with your ministers.' Rama said
it again and again in his fury, his eyes rolling with anger. Again and again I
begged that tamer of enemies, the tiger of the Bhrigus, with pleasant words, and
he did not calm down. Then I bowed my head again to the best of brahmins and
said, 'What is the reason you want to fight me? You yourself taught me the
fourfold art of the bow when I was a boy, strong-armed one. I am your pupil,
Bhargava.'

"Then Rama said to me, his eyes red with anger, 'You know I am your teacher,
Bhishma, and yet you will not take this daughter of Kashya, to please me,
Kauravya, lord of the earth. There is no peace for you any other way, joy of the
Kurus. Take her, strong-armed one, and save your family. You have made her fall,
and she does not find a husband.'

"When Rama, conqueror of enemy cities, said this, I said to him, 'It will not
happen like that again, brahmin seer. What is the use of your trouble? I look on
you as my teacher of old, Jamadagnya, and I beg you, my lord. I gave her up long
ago. Who, knowing the great faults of women, would keep in his house a woman
whose heart is another's, like a snake? Not even for fear of Vasava would I give
up dharma, splendid one. Be gracious, or do not, or do what you must do, and
quickly. And there is this verse we hear in the old tales, lord whose soul is
pure, sung by the great, wise Marutta: "Even a teacher who is proud, who does
not know what should be done and what should not, and who has taken the wrong
road, should be punished."

"'I have honoured you greatly with love as my teacher. But you do not know how a
teacher should behave, so I shall fight you. I would not kill a teacher in
battle, least of all a brahmin, and least of all one old in austerity. That is
why I have borne with you. But when a man sees a brahmin with his arrow raised,
fighting like a kshatriya and not running away, and kills him in anger in
battle, he does not incur the sin of killing a brahmin. That is settled in the
books of dharma. I am a kshatriya, standing in the kshatriyas' dharma, you who
are rich in austerity. A man who treats others as they treat him incurs no
adharma and finds good. A man able in gain or in dharma, who knows place and
time, is better off when he is free of doubt than when he is caught in the doubt
of harm. Since you act as is fitting in this doubtful matter, I shall fight you,
Rama, in a great battle. See the strength of my arms and my courage beyond the
human.

"'Even so, son of Bhrigu, I shall do what I can. I shall fight you at
Kurukshetra, brahmin, in single combat, Rama, as you wish. Make ready, great
sage. There, Rama, killed by me and covered with hundreds of arrows, you will
win the worlds you have conquered, purified by weapons in the great battle. So
go back to Kurukshetra, you who love battle. I shall come there to fight you,
strong-armed one, you who are rich in austerity. There, where you once made the
purifying offerings for your father, Rama, I too shall kill you and make the
offerings, Bhargava. Go there quickly, Rama, fierce in battle. I shall take away
your old pride, you who call yourself a brahmin.

"'And as for the boast you so often make in assemblies, Rama, that you alone
conquered the kshatriyas of the world, hear this. In those days Bhishma was not
born, nor any kshatriya like me, who could have destroyed your pride in war and
your desire. I, Bhishma, conqueror of enemy cities, am born now, strong-armed
one. I shall take away your pride in battle, Rama, no doubt of it.'"

**Divergences.**

* **What Amba asks for.** Amba asks Rama to kill Bhishma (5.176.39–42; 5.177.5, 7). She does not ask Bhishma to marry her. It is Rama who tells Bhishma to take her back, "at my command" (5.178.7, 18). Bhishma answers that he would never give her to his brother again, since she said she was Shalva's (5.178.9–11). See ledger L-186.
* **Rama's vow.** Rama says he takes up weapons only for brahmins (5.177.2–4). Akritavrana reminds him of his other vows: to protect those who come to him for refuge, and to kill any proud man who conquers the whole kshatriya race (5.177.12–16).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*575–\*580, among them the saying that dying in bed is a great adharma (\*579).

**Choices.**

* Bhishma's narration is in double quotes and the speech of the people in it in single quotes. Speech quoted inside those, such as Amba's words to Bhishma as Hotravahana repeats them, is in double quotes again.
* 5.175.7 *sṛñjayaḥ*: Hotravahana is of the Srinjaya line.
* 5.175.26 *cāritrasyābhiśaṅkitaḥ*: "he suspected her virtue".
* 5.175.28 *śarīrakartā mātur me*: "the maker of her body", her mother's father.
* 5.176.5 *avijānatā*: "without knowing", that is, without knowing her heart.
* 5.176.28–29: Rama's pity at her youth is the text's.
* 5.177.2 *kāśye*: "Kashya", daughter of the king of Kashi.
* 5.177.6: that Bhishma "will clasp both your feet with his head at my word" is Rama's promise.
* 5.178.3 *gāṃ puraskṛtya*: "putting a cow at the front", a mark of honour to a guest.
* 5.178.6 *dharmāvāpteḥ parāvarāt*: "from winning dharma, high or low". The phrase is obscure.
* 5.178.21 *vyālīm iva*: "like a snake". The sayings about women here are Bhishma's.
* 5.178.24: the verse of Marutta is Bhishma's quotation.
