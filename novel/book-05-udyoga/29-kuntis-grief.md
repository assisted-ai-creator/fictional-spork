# Kunti's Grief

In the afternoon, after seeing Vidura, Janardana Govinda, tamer of enemies, went
to his father's sister. When Pritha saw Krishna coming, bright as the clear sun,
she threw her arms round his neck and cried out, remembering the Parthas. Seeing
the Varshneya Govinda after so long, the companion of those strong sons of hers,
Pritha shed tears. When the lord of fighters had been given hospitality and was
seated, she spoke to Krishna, her mouth dry and her voice choked with tears.

"From childhood they have been devoted to serving their elders. They are friends
to one another, honoured, of one mind. They were thrown out of their kingdom by
a trick and went off to the empty forest, though they deserved to live among
men. They have mastered anger and delight. They honour brahmins and speak the
truth. The Parthas gave up their comforts and their pleasures, left me weeping,
and went to the forest, and took my heart with them, root and all. How did the
great Pandavas, who did not deserve it, Keshava, live in the great forest, my
son, full of lions, tigers and elephants? Children who lost their father, always
petted by me, how did they live in the great forest, without seeing their
parents?

"From childhood the Pandavas were woken, Keshava, by the sounds of conches and
drums, of tabors and flutes. They were always woken at home by the trumpeting of
elephants, the neighing of horses and the rumble of chariot wheels. On the tops
of mansions, lying on blankets and deerskins, they were woken by the sounds of
conches and kettledrums, with flutes and vinas, mixed with the chants of the
holy day, honoured by the twice-born. They honoured the brahmins with clothes,
jewels and ornaments, and were greeted with the blessings of great brahmins,
honoured by those who deserve honour, who praised them. Surely when they hear
the cries of wild beasts in the great forest, they cannot sleep, Janardana, for
they did not deserve this. How can they, who were woken by drums and tabors, by
conches and flutes, by the sweet songs of women, Madhusudana, and by bards and
heralds and sutas praising them, be woken in the great forest by the howls of
wild beasts?

"He is modest, steady in truth, self-controlled and compassionate to all beings.
He has mastered desire and hate, and follows the way of the good. He bears the
heavy yoke of the ancient royal seers, Ambarisha and Mandhatri, Yayati and
Nahusha, Bharata and Dilipa, and Shibi son of Ushinara. He is good in character
and conduct, knows dharma and keeps his promises. He is a king with every
virtue, fit to rule the three worlds, Ajatashatru, whose soul is dharma, bright
as pure gold. He is the best of all the Kurus in dharma, learning and conduct,
handsome and long-armed. How is Yudhishthira, Krishna?

"He has the strength of ten thousand elephants and the speed of the wind. He is
impatient, the Pandava, always dear to his brother and doing what pleases him.
He killed Kichaka and his kin, Madhusudana. That hero killed the Krodhavashas,
Hidimba and Baka. In courage he is like Shakra, in speed like the wind, in anger
like the great god. Bhima is the best of fighters. That scorcher of enemies has
put away his anger, his strength and his impatience, and, master of himself,
though impatient, he obeys his brother. He is a heap of energy, a great one, a
flood of strength, of boundless might. Bhimasena is terrible even to look at,
Janardana. Tell me, Varshneya, how is Vrikodara now?

"The middle Pandava, with arms like iron bars, Achyuta, the Arjuna who always
rivals with his two arms the thousand-armed Arjuna of old, Keshava, shoots five
hundred arrows at one stroke. In archery the Pandava is equal to King
Kartavirya. In energy he is like the sun, in self-control like a great seer, in
patience like the earth, in courage like great Indra. By his courage the Kurus
won great, blazing, famous lordship over all the kings, Madhusudana. The
Kauravas rely on the terrible strength of his arms. He is the best of all
chariot fighters, the Pandava of true courage. He is the refuge of the Pandavas,
as Vasava is of the gods. He is your brother and your friend. How is Dhananjaya
now?

"He is compassionate to all beings, held back by shame, a great master of
weapons, gentle and delicate and dharmic, and dear to me: Sahadeva, the great
archer, the hero, the ornament of the assembly. He serves his brothers, Krishna,
a young man skilled in dharma and purpose. His brothers always honour the
conduct of Sahadeva, Madhusudana, that great man of good conduct. Tell me,
Varshneya, about Sahadeva, the son of Madri, the hero, the lord of fighters, who
honours his elders and serves me.

"He is delicate, young, brave and handsome, the Pandava dear to all his
brothers, Krishna, their life walking outside them. Nakula, the wonderful
fighter, the great archer, the mighty one: is he well, Krishna, my child,
brought up in comfort? Strong-armed one, will I ever see Nakula again, the great
chariot fighter, used to comfort, not deserving sorrow, so delicate? In the time
it takes for an eyelid to fall I could find no happiness without Nakula, hero.
And now I am alive. Look at me.

"Dearer to me than all my sons is Draupadi, Janardana. She is of good family and
good character, rich in every virtue. She speaks the truth. She chose the world
of her husbands over the world of her sons, gave up her dear sons and followed
the Pandavas. She is of great birth, honoured with everything she could wish
for, a queen, wholly good. How is Draupadi, Achyuta? She has five brave
husbands, like fire, hard-hitting, great archers, and still Draupadi has her
share of sorrow. This is the fourteenth year, tamer of enemies, that I have not
seen truthful Draupadi, worn with grief for her sons. Surely a man does not win
happiness by good deeds, if Draupadi, who behaves as she does, does not win
lasting happiness.

"Not Bibhatsu, not Yudhishthira, not Bhimasena nor the twins are as dear to me
as Krishna, since I saw her in the hall. Nothing more painful than this ever
happened to me before, that Draupadi, who had always lived sheltered, was
brought before her fathers-in-law by that ignoble man, the follower of anger and
greed. All the Kurus saw her in the hall in a single garment. Dhritarashtra was
there, and great King Bahlika, Kripa and Somadatta, and the Kurus were wretched.
Of that whole assembly I honour the steward. A man is noble by his conduct, not
by wealth or learning. The character of the steward, Krishna, of great
understanding, deep and great-souled, is an ornament that stands holding up the
worlds."

---

Grieving and glad at once to see Govinda come, she told over all her many
sorrows.

"Dicing and killing deer, which bad kings practised of old, tamer of enemies,
did they bring these men happiness? It burns me that Krishna was abused in the
hall by the sons of Dhritarashtra before the Kurus, as she was. The banishment
from the city and the exile, scorcher of enemies, I am the home of every kind of
sorrow, Janardana, and the hidden life of my children, and their shutting away,
Keshava. It would not have been the worst trouble for me, scorcher of enemies,
to be with my sons. Today it is the fourteenth year since Duryodhana cheated us.

"If sorrow does not end in happiness, the fruit of merit is wasted. I never made
any difference between the sons of Dhritarashtra and the Pandavas. By that
truth, Krishna, may I see you with the enemy killed, crowned with fortune, freed
from this war with the Pandavas. They cannot be beaten, for their nature is
such.

"I blame my father, not myself and not Suyodhana. He handed me over to
Kuntibhoja like wealth handed over by cheats. When I was a child, playing with a
ball in my hand, your grandfather gave me to Kuntibhoja, a friend to his great
friend. Cheated by my father and by my in-laws, scorcher of enemies, I live in
terrible sorrow, Krishna. What is the fruit of my life?

"On the night the Left-handed Archer was born, a voice said to me, 'Your son
will conquer the earth, and his fame will touch heaven. When he has killed the
Kurus in a great battle and won the kingdom, Dhananjaya, son of Kunti, will
perform three sacrifices with his brothers.' I do not doubt that voice. I bow to
Dharma, the Ordainer, to the great Krishna for ever. Dharma upholds creatures.
If there is dharma, Varshneya, it will come true. And you too, Krishna, will
bring it all about.

"Widowhood does not grieve me so much, Madhava, nor the loss of wealth, nor
enmity, as being without my sons. What peace can my heart have when I do not see
Dhananjaya, the Gandiva bowman, the best of all who bear weapons? This is the
fourteenth year that I have not seen Yudhishthira and Dhananjaya, Govinda, or
the twins, or Vrikodara. Men perform the rites for the dead for those who are
lost when they are gone from sight. In effect they are dead to me, Janardana,
and I to them.

"Tell King Yudhishthira, whose soul is dharma, Madhava: 'Your dharma is wasting
away fast. Do not make it vain, my son.' Shame on me, Vasudeva, who live
depending on others. Better to die than to live on a living begged for. Then
tell Dhananjaya and Vrikodara, always ready for action: 'The time has come for
which a kshatriya woman bears a son. If you let the time pass when it has come,
you, who are honoured by the world, will do a very cruel thing. If you take to
cruelty, I will give you up for ever. When the time comes, even life must be
given up.' Tell the sons of Madri, always devoted to the dharma of a kshatriya:
'Choose the pleasures won by courage even over life. For wealth won by courage,
best of men, always pleases the heart of a man who lives by the dharma of a
kshatriya.'

"Go, strong-armed one, and tell Arjuna the Pandava, the hero, the best of all
who bear weapons: 'Follow the path of Draupadi.' You know well that Bhima and
Arjuna, when they are angry, are like two Destroyers. They could send even the
gods to their last end. It was an insult to those two that Krishna was brought
into the hall, and that Duhshasana and Karna spoke harsh words to her.
Duryodhana went against proud Bhimasena while the chief Kurus looked on. He will
see the fruit of it. Once Vrikodara takes up a feud, he does not let it rest.
Even after a long time Bhima's enmity does not rest, until the slayer of enemies
has made an end of his foes.

"The loss of the kingdom is not my sorrow, nor the defeat at dice. The exile of
my sons is not the cause of my sorrow. That the great, dark woman came into the
hall in one garment and heard harsh words, what could be more painful than that?
That lovely woman, always devoted to the dharma of a kshatriya, in her season,
Krishna, found no protector, though she had protectors. I have you as my
protector, Madhusudana, with my sons, and Rama, the best of the strong, and the
great chariot fighter Pradyumna. And I bear such sorrow today, best of men,
while unbeatable Bhima lives, and Vijaya, who never runs."

---

Then Shauri, the Partha's friend, comforted Pritha, his father's sister, who was
grieving and drowned in sorrow for her sons.

"What woman in the worlds is like you, aunt? You are the daughter of King Shura,
and you came into the family of Ajamidha. You are of great family, come like a
stream from one lake into another. You are a queen, wholly good, greatly
honoured by your husband. You are the mother of heroes and the wife of a hero,
rich in every virtue. A woman like you, so wise, can bear happiness and sorrow.

"Sleep and sloth, anger and delight, hunger and thirst, cold and heat: the
Parthas have mastered these, and those heroes always delight in happiness. The
Parthas have given up the pleasures of the village. They always love the
happiness of heroes. They would not be content with little, those strong ones of
great energy. The steady seek the extremes. Those who love the pleasures of the
village seek the middle. The highest hardships and the pleasures beyond human:
the steady delight in the extremes, not in the middle. They say reaching the end
is happiness, and the space between the ends is sorrow.

"The Pandavas and Krishna greet you. They send word that they are well and ask
after your health. Soon you will see the Pandavas well, with every aim achieved,
lords of the whole world, their enemies killed, crowned with fortune."

Comforted in this way, Kunti, overwhelmed with grief for her sons, held back the
darkness born of folly and answered Janardana. "Whatever is good for them,
strong-armed one, Madhusudana, do it in whatever way you think best, Krishna,
without breaking dharma, without trickery, scorcher of enemies. I know your
power, Krishna, your truth and your family, your firmness with friends, your
wisdom and your courage. You are the dharma in our family. You are truth. You
are great austerity. You are the protector, you are the great Brahman,
everything rests on you. As you have said, so it will be. In you it will be
true."

Govinda took his leave of her, walked round her keeping her on his right, and
the strong-armed one set out for Duryodhana's house.
