# Bhima Speaks for Peace

"I have heard Sanjaya's words, and I have heard yours," said the Lord. "I know
all they intend, and all you intend. Your mind rests on dharma, and theirs on
enmity. What could be won without war you would value highly. But that is not
the settled way of a kshatriya, lord of the people. All those in the stages of
life say a kshatriya should not live by begging. Victory or death in battle is
the eternal rule laid down by the Ordainer. That is the kshatriya's own dharma.
Weakness is not praised. No living can be had by holding to weakness,
Yudhishthira. Show your courage, strong-armed one. Kill your enemies, tamer of
enemies.

"The sons of Dhritarashtra are very greedy, scorcher of enemies. They have lived
together a long time and made friends and allies, and gathered strength. There
is no way they will treat you as an equal, lord of the people. They think they
are strong, with Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the rest. As long as you deal gently
with them, king, they will take your kingdom. Neither pity, nor weakness, nor
the claims of dharma and purpose will make the sons of Dhritarashtra do what you
want, tamer of enemies.

"This is the proof for you. The Pandavas felt no remorse at your loincloth, even
after doing that hard thing. You are generous, gentle and self-controlled. You
love dharma and keep your vows. And while the grandfather, Drona and wise Vidura
looked on, while all the chief Kurus looked on, he cheated you, king, by a trick
at dice. And the wicked, cruel man is not ashamed of that deed. Do not show
kindness, king, to a man of such character and conduct. Such men deserve death
from the whole world, Bharata, let alone from you.

"With unworthy words he wounded you and your younger brothers. He boasted, and
spoke gleefully with his brothers. 'Now the Pandavas have nothing of their own
here. Not even their name and their family are left to them. In a long time they
will be defeated. Their people, their nature lost, will take on another nature.'
These and other harsh words he spoke, and boasted among his kin when you had
gone to the forest. Those who were brought there saw you without fault, and they
sat in the hall with tears in their throats, weeping. The kings and the brahmins
did not welcome him. All who sat in the hall blamed Duryodhana.

"For a man of good family, blame is death, tamer of enemies. Death is a great
good, king, but blame is a miserable life. He was killed then, king, as soon as
he became shameless, and was blamed by all the kings on earth, great king. A man
whose conduct is like that is easily killed, like a tree propped on a post with
its roots cut. The ignoble, evil-minded man deserves death from the whole world,
like a snake. Kill him, tamer of enemies. Do not hesitate, king.

"All the same, I like it, faultless one, and it is fitting for you, that you
should bow down to your father and to Bhishma. I will go and clear up the doubt
of all the world, king, of all who are of two minds about Duryodhana. There, in
the middle of the kings, I will tell your virtues as a man, and his offences.
All the kings of the many countries will hear my words, full of dharma and
purpose and good. They will come round to you, as a man whose soul is dharma and
who speaks the truth. And they will see how he acted out of greed. I will blame
him among the people of the city and the country, with the old and the young, in
the gathering of the four classes. If you ask for peace and do not get your
dharma there, the kings will blame the Kurus and Dhritarashtra. When the world
has abandoned him, what is left to do? When Duryodhana is killed, king, let the
rest be done.

"I will go to all the Kurus and try to make peace without giving up your cause.
I will see what they are doing. When I have seen the Kauravas' plans for war, I
will come back, Bharata, for your victory. I expect nothing but war with the
enemy. All the omens appear to me so. Beasts and birds cry terribly, among the
best of the elephants and horses at nightfall. Fire too takes on terrible forms
and many terrible colours. A terrible destroyer of the world of men, Death, has
come here. Otherwise it would not be so. Get ready the weapons, the arrows, the
armour and the chariots, the elephants and the banners. Let all your fighters be
trained and ready on elephants, horses and chariots. Gather together everything
you need for the war, king of men. For Duryodhana, while he lives, will never
give back to you what was yours before, the rich kingdom taken from you at the
dice, chief of the Pandavas."

---

"Speak in whatever way will make peace with the Kurus, Madhusudana," said
Bhimasena. "Do not frighten them with war. Duryodhana is impatient, always
angry, hostile to his own good and proud. He should not be spoken to harshly.
Deal with him by conciliation. He is evil by nature, and his mind is like a
robber's. He is drunk with the pride of power, and he has made enemies of the
Pandavas. He does not look far ahead. He is harsh, he scorns others, he is cruel
in his courage. His anger lasts long, he cannot be led, he is wicked and loves
deceit. He would die before he would bend. He would never give up his own
opinion. I think peace with such a man is very hard, Krishna. He turns his back
even on his friends. He has given up dharma and he loves falsehood. He strikes
down the words and the hearts of his friends. In the grip of his anger, keeping
to his wicked nature, he follows evil by his nature, like a snake goaded with
grass.

"You know all about Duryodhana, what army he has, what character and nature,
what strength and what courage. Before, the Kurus and their sons were at peace,
and so were we. We were glad with our kin, like the gods with Indra as their
eldest. Now, through Duryodhana's anger, the Bharatas will burn, Madhusudana,
like forests burnt by fires at the end of the cold season.

"There are eighteen famous kings, Madhusudana, who cut down their kin, their
friends and their families. When the asuras were prospering and blazing with
energy, and the turn of dharma's time came, Bali was born. There were Udavarta
of the Haihayas, Janamejaya of the Nipas, Bahula of the Talajanghas and proud
Vasu of the Krimis. There were Ajabindu of the Suviras, Kusharddhika of the
Surashtras, Arkaja of the Balihas and Dhautamulaka of the Chinas. There were
Hayagriva of the Videhas, Varapra of the Mahaujases, Bahu of the Sundaravegas
and Pururavas of the Diptakshas. There were Sahaja of the Chedis and Matsyas,
Brihadbala of the Prachetas, Dharana of the Indravatsas, Vigahana of the
Mukutas, and Shama of the Nandivegas. These disgraces to their families, the
lowest of men, were born in their families at the end of an age, Krishna. And
this Duryodhana may be the one born to us Kurus at the end of an age, brought by
time, a firebrand of the family, the lowest of evil men.

"So speak to him slowly and gently, in words full of dharma and purpose, full of
what he desires, not harshly, for he is fierce in his courage. We will all go
below Duryodhana, Krishna. We will humble ourselves and follow him. Let the
Bharatas not perish. Vasudeva, see that we can at least stand aside from the
Kurus, so that bad policy does not touch them. Speak to the old grandfather,
Krishna, and those who sit in the hall. Let there be brotherhood among the
brothers. Let Dhritarashtra's son be calmed. I say this, and the king praises
it. Arjuna does not want war. There is great compassion in Arjuna."

---

When he heard this, strong-armed Keshava seemed to laugh. Bhima had never spoken
so gently before. It seemed to Shauri, Rama's younger brother, the archer with
the Sharnga bow, like lightness in a mountain or coolness in fire. Like the wind
kindling a fire, he set out to kindle Vrikodara with words, as he sat there
overcome with pity.

"At other times, Bhimasena, you praise only war. You want to crush the cruel
sons of Dhritarashtra, who delight in killing. You do not sleep. You lie awake,
lying face down, scorcher of enemies. You always speak terrible, restless,
furious words. You sigh, burning with your own anger the colour of fire, Bhima,
with your mind unquiet, like a fire with smoke. You lie alone groaning, like a
weak man under a burden. People who do not know think you are mad. You run about
groaning, Bhima, striking the earth with your feet, like an elephant tearing up
trees by the roots and trampling them.

"You take no pleasure in these people, Pandava. You stay alone in private. You
never welcome anyone, night or day. You sit alone smiling for no reason, or as
if weeping. You sit for a long time with your head on your knees and your eyes
closed. You are often seen frowning and licking your lips. All that is the work
of anger.

"'As the sun is seen rising bright in the east, and as the rayed one surely goes
round to the west, free, so I tell you this truly, and there will be no breaking
it. I will go after impatient Duryodhana and kill him with my mace.' That is
what you swore, touching your mace, among your brothers. And now your mind turns
to peace, scorcher of enemies.

"Oh, now that the time of war has come, do you see things against war as if they
were not against it? What fear has found you, Bhima? Oh, Partha, do you see bad
omens, at the end of sleep or of waking, that you want peace? Oh, you hope for
no manhood in yourself, like a eunuch. You are gripped by faint-heartedness, and
your mind is changed by it. Your heart trembles. Your mind sinks. You are
gripped by stiffness of the thighs, and so you want peace.

"A mortal's mind is fickle, they say, Partha, always moving, like a silk-cotton
seed blown by the force of the wind. This changed mind of yours is like human
speech from cows. It sinks the hearts of the sons of Pandu like men without a
raft. This is a great wonder to me, like a mountain moving, that you should say
such words, unlike Bhimasena. Look at your own deeds and your birth in your
family, Bharata. Get up. Do not give way to despair, hero. Be firm. This
weakness is not worthy of you, tamer of enemies. What a kshatriya does not win
by his strength, he does not enjoy."

---

Spoken to like this by Vasudeva, the ever-angry, impatient Bhima ran like a good
horse under the whip, and answered him at once.

"You think I want to do one thing, Achyuta, when I mean another. I am utterly
set on war, and truly brave. You know my nature, Dasharha, for you have lived
with me a long time. Or else you do not know me, like a man floating on a lake
who does not know its depth. That is why you attack me with unworthy words. How
could anyone who knows me, Bhimasena, say such unworthy things to me, Madhava,
as you have? So I will tell you this, joy of the Vrishnis, about my manhood and
my strength, which are not equal to others'.

"To praise oneself is never noble. But wounded by your insults, I will speak of
my strength. Look at these two, heaven and earth, Krishna, in whom all these
creatures live, unmoving, endless, the resting place, the mothers of all. If
they came together suddenly in anger, like two rocks, I would hold them apart
with my arms, with all that moves and does not move. Look at the space between
my arms, like great iron bars. I see no man who could get into it and escape.
The Himalaya, the ocean and the wielder of the thunderbolt, the slayer of Bala
himself, those three together with all their strength could not save one I have
seized.

"I would fight all the kshatriyas who attack the Pandavas, and trample them on
the ground under the soles of my feet. You do know my courage, Achyuta, how I
beat the kings and brought them under control. And if you do not know me, as the
light of the rising sun is not known, you will know me, Janardana, in the thick
of battle. Why do you lance me with harsh words, faultless one, as a wound is
lanced with a needle? I speak as I think. Know that I am more than this. On the
day the slaughter begins, in the thick of battle, you will see me drive back
elephants, chariot fighters and horsemen. You and the world will see me in my
fury killing men, bulls among kshatriyas, dragging out the best of them one by
one. My marrow does not sink. My mind does not tremble. Even if the whole world
were angry with me, I would have no fear. But it is only out of friendship,
Madhusudana, out of pity, that I bear all these troubles. Let the Bharatas not
perish."

---

"I said this out of love," said the Lord, "wanting to know your mind. Not to
mock you, not to show my learning, not out of anger, not for the sake of
talking. I know your greatness, and I know your strength. I know your deeds. I
do not look down on you. Whatever good you think of yourself, Pandava, I think a
thousand times more of you. You are as great, Bhima, as the family you were born
into, honoured by all the kings, with your kin and your friends.

"People who look into the doubtful matters of dharma, Vrikodara, cannot decide
the turn of things between the divine and the human. The very thing that is the
cause of a man's success is also the cause of his ruin. Human effort is
uncertain. Things seen one way by poets who see faults turn out another way,
like the gusts of the wind. Human work well planned, well guided and done
rightly can be blocked by fate. And what fate has not done can be undone by
human effort: cold and heat, rain, hunger and thirst, Bharata. Whatever else a
man does himself, against what is fated, that it is not blocked is its mark. The
world has no other way to live, Pandava, but by deeds. A man should act with
this understanding. The fruit comes from both together. The man who sets his
mind like this and keeps to his work is not troubled when he fails, and does not
rejoice too much when he succeeds.

"This is all I meant to say, Bhimasena. Victory over the Kurus in battle should
not be thought certain. A man should not let the reins go slack, or he may come
to despair or weariness when things turn. That is why I tell you this.

"Tomorrow, Pandava, when I reach Dhritarashtra, I will try to make peace without
giving up your cause. If they make peace, then my fame will be endless, your
wish will be done, and theirs will be the highest good. If they hold to their
course and do not accept my words, the Kurus, then war will come, a terrible
deed. In this war the burden rests on you, Bhimasena. The yoke must be borne by
Arjuna, and the others must be carried along. I shall be Bibhatsu's charioteer
when the battle comes. That is Dhananjaya's wish. I do not want there to be no
war. So, fearing for your resolve, Vrikodara, I pricked you with manly words and
kindled your fire."
