# Vidura's Night Counsel

Then wise King Dhritarashtra said to the doorkeeper, "I want to see Vidura.
Bring him here at once."

Sent by Dhritarashtra, the messenger said to the Steward, "Our lord, the great
king, wants to see you, wise one."

When he was told this, Vidura came to the king's palace and said, "Doorkeeper,
tell Dhritarashtra I am here."

"Vidura has come at your command, king of kings," said the doorkeeper. "He wants
to see your feet. What shall he do? Command me."

"Let wise, far-seeing Vidura come in," said Dhritarashtra. "It is never the
wrong time for me to see Vidura."

"Go into the inner palace of the wise great king, Steward," said the doorkeeper.
"The king tells me it is never the wrong time for him to see you."

So Vidura went into Dhritarashtra's house. With joined palms he spoke to the
king, who sat brooding. "Wise one, I am Vidura. I have come at your command. If
there is anything to be done, here I am. Command me."

"Sanjaya has come, Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "and he has gone, after blaming
me. Tomorrow he will speak Ajatashatru's words in the midst of the hall. Today I
have not found out what the Kuru hero said. It burns my limbs, and it has kept
me awake. Tell me what you see is best for one who lies awake and burns, my son,
for you are skilled in dharma and profit. Since Sanjaya came back from the
Pandavas my mind has had no proper peace. All my senses are disordered. What
will he say? That is all I can think of today."

"Sleeplessness comes to these," said Vidura: "to the weak man attacked by the
strong, the man without means, the man whose goods are stolen, the lustful man,
and the thief. I hope none of these great evils has touched you, king. I hope
you are not burning with greed for other men's wealth."

"I want to hear from you words of dharma, of the highest good," said
Dhritarashtra. "In this line of royal seers you alone are counted wise."

---

"He who does what is praised," said Vidura, "and does not do what is blamed, who
is no unbeliever and has faith: these are the marks of the wise man.

"The man whom anger and joy, pride and shame, stubbornness and self-regard do
not drag away from his purpose is called wise.

"The man whose plans and counsels others do not know, and who is known only by
what he has done, is called wise.

"The man whose work is not hindered by cold or heat, by fear or love, by wealth
or want, is called wise.

"The man whose understanding follows dharma and profit through the world, who
chooses profit over pleasure, is called wise.

"The wise, bull of the Bharatas, wish to do as much as they can, and do as much
as they can, and despise nothing.

"He learns quickly, and listens long. When he understands a thing he acts on it
with judgement, not from desire. He does not busy himself in others' affairs
unasked. That is the first sign of the wise.

"Men of wise understanding do not long for what cannot be had. They do not wish
to grieve for what is lost. In misfortune they are not bewildered.

"The man who first decides and then begins, who does not stop in the middle of
his work, who does not waste his time and is master of himself, is called wise.

"The wise, bull of the Bharatas, delight in noble deeds, do what brings
prosperity, and do not scorn what is good for them.

"The man who is not elated when he is honoured, nor pained when he is slighted,
and who is as calm as a deep pool of the Ganga, is called wise.

"The man who knows the truth of all beings, knows how all acts are joined, and
knows the means that men use, is called wise.

"The man who speaks freely, tells good stories, reasons well, has ready wit, and
can quickly explain a book, is called wise.

"The man whose learning follows his wisdom, and whose wisdom follows his
learning, who never breaks the bounds of the noble, earns the name of wise.

---

"The man who is proud without learning, grand in mind while poor, and who wants
wealth without work, is called a fool by the wise.

"The man who gives up his own affairs and follows another's, and acts falsely
for his friend's sake, is called a fool.

"The man who wants what should not be wanted, hates those who want him, and
hates the strong, is called a fool.

"The man who makes an enemy his friend, hates and harms his friend, and begins
wicked work, is called a fool.

"The man who lets his work drag on, doubts everything, and is slow when speed is
needed, is a fool, bull of the Bharatas.

"He comes in without being called, talks a great deal without being asked, and
trusts those who are not to be trusted: he is a fool and the lowest of men.

"He blames another for a fault he commits himself, and grows angry when he has
no power: he is the greatest fool of all.

"The man who, without knowing his own strength, and cut off from dharma and
profit, wants what cannot be had without effort, is called foolish here.

"The man who instructs one who is no pupil, king, who waits on emptiness, and
who serves a miser, is called a fool.

"The man who gains great wealth, learning or power, and goes about without
pride, is called wise.

---

"Who is crueller than the man who eats rich food alone, and wears fine clothes
alone, without sharing with those who serve him?

"One man does wrong, and many enjoy the fruit. Those who enjoy it go free, and
the doer is stained with the fault.

"An arrow shot by an archer may kill one man, or may not. But a plan let loose
by a clever man can kill a kingdom and its king.

"Settle the two by the one. Bring the three under your control by the four.
Conquer the five, know the six, give up the seven, and be happy.

"Poison kills one man, and a weapon kills one. A leaked counsel kills the king,
with his kingdom and his people.

"Do not eat something tasty alone. Do not think out your affairs alone. Do not
travel alone. Do not stay awake alone among sleepers.

"There is one thing, without a second, which you do not understand, king: truth
is the stairway to heaven, like a boat across the sea.

"The forgiving have one fault, and no second: people think a forgiving man is
weak.

"Dharma alone is the highest good. Forgiveness alone is the best peace.
Knowledge alone is the highest sight. Harmlessness alone brings happiness.

---

"The earth swallows these two, as a snake swallows creatures that live in holes:
a king who does not fight, and a brahmin who does not travel.

"A man shines in this world by doing two things: saying nothing harsh, and not
courting the wicked.

"These two follow others, tiger among men: women who want those whom others
want, and people who honour those whom others honour.

"These two are sharp thorns that dry up the body: the desire of the poor man,
and the anger of the powerless.

"These two, king, stand above heaven: the strong man who forgives, and the poor
man who gives.

"Two misuses of wealth rightly gained should be known: giving it to the
unworthy, and not giving it to the worthy.

---

"Those who know the Veda say that men have three ways of acting, the lowest, the
middle and the best. There are three kinds of men, king, the best, the worst and
the middling. Set each to the work that fits his kind.

"These three have no wealth of their own, king: the wife, the slave and the son.
Whatever they earn belongs to him to whom they belong.

---

"Wise men say that a mighty king should keep away from four. Let the wise man
know them: he should not take counsel with men of little wisdom, with the slow,
with the idle, or with flatterers.

"When you are blessed with fortune and live as a householder, my friend, let
these four live in your house. They are an old kinsman, a man of good family
fallen low, a poor friend, and a sister without children.

"Brihaspati, when the lord of the thirty gods asked him, named four things that
come about at once, great king. Hear them from me: the will of the gods, the
insight of the wise, the modesty of the learned, and the ruin of the wicked.

---

"A man should carefully tend five fires, bull of the Bharatas: his father, his
mother, the fire itself, his own self, and his teacher.

"By honouring five a man wins lasting fame in the world: the gods, the
Ancestors, men, beggars, and guests as the fifth.

"Five will follow you wherever you go: friends, enemies, those who stand
between, those you live by, and those who live by you.

"A mortal has five senses. If one of them has a hole, his wisdom runs out
through it, as water runs out of the foot of a water-skin.

---

"A man who wants prosperity must give up six faults: sleep, sloth, fear, anger,
idleness and procrastination.

"A man should leave these six, like a leaking boat at sea. They are a teacher
who does not teach, a priest who has not studied, a king who does not protect, a
wife who speaks unkindly, a cowherd who wants to live in the village, and a
barber who wants to live in the forest.

"A man must never give up these six virtues: truth, generosity, energy, freedom
from envy, forgiveness and firmness.

"The man who masters the six that always live within him, and conquers his
senses, is not touched by sins, still less by misfortunes.

"These six live on six, and no seventh is found. Thieves live on the careless,
doctors on the sick, women on the lustful, priests on those who sacrifice, kings
on those who quarrel, and the learned always on fools.

---

"A king must always give up seven faults, from which misfortunes rise, and by
which kings, even well rooted, mostly perish. They are women, dice, hunting and
drink, harshness of speech as the fifth, great harshness in punishment, and the
misuse of wealth.

"There are eight early signs of a man who is going to perish. First he hates
brahmins, and quarrels with brahmins. He takes brahmins' property, and wants to
kill brahmins. He delights in abusing them, and takes no pleasure in their
praise. He does not remember them when there is work to be done, and he grudges
them when they ask. A wise man should know these faults with his understanding,
and avoid them.

"These eight, Bharata, are the fresh butter of joy, and they are seen here, and
they are the sweetest. They are meeting with friends, a great gain of wealth, a
son's embrace, union in love, kind talk at the right time, a humble place among
your own people, getting what you wanted, and honour in the assembly.

"This house has nine doors, three pillars and five witnesses, and the knower of
the field dwells in it. The learned man who knows it is the highest of seers.

"Ten do not know dharma, Dhritarashtra. Hear them: the drunk, the careless, the
mad, the tired, the angry, the hungry, the hasty, the frightened, the greedy and
the lustful. These are the ten. So a wise man should not attach himself to such
states.

---

"On this they tell an old story, which the lord of the asuras sang for his son's
sake, and Sudhanvan too.

"The king who gives up desire and anger, who places his wealth with the worthy,
who knows the differences between things, who is learned and quick to act. The
whole world takes him as its measure.

"He knows how to win men's trust. He punishes those whose faults are proven. He
knows the right measure, and when to forgive. Fortune in full attends such a
man.

"He despises no one, however weak. He deals with his enemy with care and with
thought. He does not choose to fight the strong, and he shows his courage at the
right time. That man is steady.

"When misfortune comes he is never shaken. He seeks to act, and is never
careless. He bears sorrow when its time comes, and masters himself. He carries
the load, and his rivals are beaten.

"He does not leave home for no purpose. He makes no pact with the wicked, does
not touch another man's wife, and has no part in deceit, theft, slander or
drink. He is always happy.

"He does not start after wealth in a passion. When asked, he says only what is
true. He does not quarrel over a trifle. He is not angry when he is not
honoured, and he is not deluded.

"He does not envy, and he is kind. He does not, being weak, stand surety for
others. He does not say too much, and he bears a dispute. Such a man is praised
everywhere.

"He never dresses proudly. He does not boast to others of his manliness. In his
passion he says nothing bitter. People always hold him dear.

"He does not stir up a feud that has died down. He does not climb up in pride,
nor sink down. He does not grow angry and say, "I am in trouble." They call him
the first in noble character.

"He is not overjoyed at his own happiness, nor glad at another's sorrow. When he
has given, he does not regret it afterwards. A good man of noble character does
not boast.

"He who knows high and low, and seeks to learn the customs of countries, the
agreements of men and the duties of each birth. He is always welcome wherever he
goes, and rules over great numbers of people.

"The wise man who gives up deceit and delusion, envy and wicked deeds, what
kings hate, slander, feuds with many, and argument with the drunk, the mad and
the wicked, is the first among men.

"The man who does these daily works, self-control, purity, worship of the gods,
the rites of good fortune, atonement, and the many ways of the world, is raised
up by the gods.

"He marries with his equals, not with those beneath him. He makes friends, does
business and talks with his equals. He puts those who excel in virtue first. The
policies of that wise man are well laid.

"He eats little, and shares with those who depend on him. He sleeps little,
after working much. When he is asked, he gives even to his enemies. Misfortunes
leave the man who is master of himself.

"When others do not know anything of what he means to do, or what he has begun,
when his counsel is kept and well carried out, not the least of his affairs goes
wrong.

"The man who is set on bringing peace to all beings, who is true, gentle,
generous and pure in mind, is known among his kin like a great jewel, flawless
and clear.

"The man who feels deep shame before himself becomes the teacher of all the
world. Boundless in his power, gracious and collected, he shines by his own
light, like the sun.

---

"The five sons of Pandu, the king who was burned by a curse, were born in the
forest, five sons each like Indra. You raised them yourself when they were boys,
and taught them. They follow your commands, son of Ambika. Give them their
rightful kingdom, my father, and be happy, rejoicing with your sons. Then, lord
of men, neither gods nor men will have anything to question in you."

**Divergences.** Vidura's counsel is one of the parts of the epic that grew most
in the manuscripts. Many well-known sayings that Ganguli gives here are rejected
lines, and are not narrated:

* "Forgiveness is the virtue of the weak and the ornament of the strong", and "fire falling where there is no grass goes out by itself" (\*175).
* The two who pierce the orb of the sun, the yogi and the warrior killed facing the enemy (\*178); the two who do not shine, the idle householder and the busy beggar (\*179); the two who should be drowned with stones round their necks (\*180).
* The three gates of hell, desire, anger and greed; the three never to be abandoned; setting an enemy free (\*181).
* The four acts that bring fear when done wrongly (\*182).
* The six that are lost by neglect, the six ungrateful, the six joys and the six always unhappy (\*183, \*184).
* The long opening in which Vidura contrasts Yudhishthira's virtues with Dhritarashtra's trust in Duryodhana, Shakuni, Karna and Duhshasana (\*168, \*169).
* Other rejected lines (\*166, \*167, \*170–\*174, \*176, \*177, \*185) add sayings or repeat them.

Ganguli also gives a list of "eight qualities that glorify a man" (wisdom, high
birth and so on) before the house of nine doors. In the CE that list comes later,
at 5.35.45, and is told there.

**Choices.**

* The sayings are kept in the CE's order. "Is called wise" and "is called a fool" repeat because the text repeats them.
* 5.33.38 *yaś ca śūnyam upāsate*: "who waits on emptiness", that is, serves one who can give nothing.
* 5.33.43: the numbered riddle is kept as the text gives it. The traditional reading is: by the one (understanding) settle the two (what to do and what not to do); bring the three (friend, enemy and neutral) under control by the four (conciliation, gifts, division and force); conquer the five (senses); know the six (measures of policy); give up the seven (vices, listed at 5.33.74).
* 5.33.46: "There is one thing, without a second, which you do not understand": truth.
* 5.33.49 *aviroddhāram … apravāsinam*: "a king who does not fight, and a brahmin who does not travel", as the text says.
* 5.33.57: the three who have no wealth of their own are kept as the text gives them.
* 5.33.70 *ṣaṇṇām ātmani nityānām*: "the six that always live within him", which the text does not list. They are usually taken as the six inner enemies (desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride, envy).
* 5.33.78 *harṣasya navanītāni*: "the fresh butter of joy".
* 5.33.81: the body is "a house with nine doors, three pillars and five witnesses", in which "the knower of the field" (*kṣetrajña*) dwells. The novel keeps the image and does not expand it.
* 5.33.84 *putrārtham asurendreṇa gītaṃ caiva sudhanvanā*: the old story "sung by the lord of the asuras for his son's sake, and by Sudhanvan" is Prahlada's judgement between his son Virochana and Sudhanvan (2.61.59–79), which Vidura tells at 5.35. The verses that follow (5.33.85–102) are read, with Ganguli, as Vidura's own counsel, not as the song itself.
* 5.33.103 *śāpadagdhasya*: Pandu, "burned by a curse" (1.109).
