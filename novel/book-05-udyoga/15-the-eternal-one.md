# The Eternal One

"A twice-born man who studies the Rig, the Yajur and the Sama Veda," said
Dhritarashtra, "and does evil, is he stained by the evil or not?"

"Neither the Samans nor the Riks nor the Yajus save him from the evil of his
deeds, wise one," said Sanatsujata. "I do not tell you a lie. The Vedas do not
save a deceiver who lives by deceit from his sin. They leave him at his end, as
fledged birds leave the nest."

"If the Vedas cannot save one who knows them, wise one," said Dhritarashtra,
"why then this ancient chatter of the brahmins?"

"Austerity is practised in this world," said Sanatsujata, "and its fruit is seen
in another. These worlds belong to brahmins who are restrained in austerity that
has prospered."

"How does austerity, however rich, become simply prospered?" said Dhritarashtra.
"Tell us, Sanatsujata, so that we may know."

"There are twelve faults, anger first among them," said Sanatsujata, "and six
cruel ways, king. And there are twelve virtues, dharma first among them, spread
out in the teaching and known to the twice-born. Anger, desire, greed, delusion,
craving, pitilessness, envy, pride, grief, longing, jealousy and disgust: these
are the faults of men, and a man should always avoid these twelve. Each of these
waits on men, lord of kings, looking for an opening, as a hunter waits on deer.

"The boaster, the greedy man, the proud man, the man who nurses his anger, the
fickle man and the man who protects no one: these six, when they come to power,
practise evil dharma. They do not cease even in great trouble. The man whose
mind is set on pleasure, the man who prospers by hating, the man who regrets his
gifts, the miser, the weak man, the man who praises his own band, and the man
who hates his wife: these are another seven of cruel ways.

"Dharma, truth, self-control, austerity, freedom from jealousy, shame, patience,
freedom from envy, sacrifice, giving, steadiness and learning: these are the
twelve great vows of a brahmin. The man who does not stray from these twelve
could rule this whole earth. Know that a man who stands out by three of them, or
two, or even one, has nothing of his own.

"Deathlessness is placed in self-control, renunciation and care. The wise
brahmins say that truth is the head of these.

"Self-control is free of eighteen faults. They are these: going against what is
done and not done, falsehood, envy, desire and wealth, longing, anger, grief,
thirst, greed, slander, jealousy, craving, remorse, lust, forgetfulness, too
much talk, and thinking much of oneself. The good call the man who is free of
these faults self-controlled.

"Renunciation is of six kinds, and good. The first is not to rejoice when the
pleasant comes. The second is never to feel pain when the unpleasant comes. The
third is to give to a worthy man who asks, beloved wives and sons and anything
else, whatever word is asked, and even to say what should not be said. The
fourth is the man who becomes rich by giving things up, and does not use them as
he pleases. The fifth is the man who does not fall short in his work for lack of
them, but is like a pupil in his thinking. The sixth is the man who has every
virtue and wealth too.

"Care has eight faults, and a man should avoid them. When he is free of the five
senses and the mind, Bharata, and of the past and the future, he is happy.

"Austerity that is free of these faults and has these virtues is the one that
prospers and is simply prospered. That is what you ask me, king of kings. What
more do you want to hear?"

"People are mostly said to have the Vedas with the stories as the fifth," said
Dhritarashtra. "Others have four Vedas, others three, others two, others one,
and others none. Which of them should I know as a brahmin?"

"Because the one Veda was not known, there came to be many Vedas," said
Sanatsujata. "But of the one truth, king of kings, only now and then does
someone stand in the truth. So people make much of their learning without
honouring the Veda. Giving, study and sacrifice come from greed. The resolve of
those who fall from truth comes to nothing. Then sacrifice spreads out from the
holding of truth alone. One man's comes by the mind, another's by speech, and
another's by deeds. The man whose resolves come true is master of his resolves.

"He should keep the vow of consecration without show. This name comes from the
root of being, and truth itself is the highest for the good. Knowledge is
direct. Austerity grows from what is hidden. Know a brahmin who reads a great
deal as a great reader. So do not think a man is a brahmin just because he
talks, kshatriya. Know the one who does not stray from truth to be a brahmin.

"Atharvan sang those that are called the metres long ago, kshatriya, in the
creation of the seers. Those who learn them are knowers of the metres, but they
do not know what is to be known by the Veda. No one knows the Vedas, king, or
else someone understands the Vedas. The one who knows the Vedas does not know
what is to be known. The one who stands in truth knows what is to be known.

"I recognise as a brahmin the wise teacher who, with his own doubts cut, clears
away all doubts. A man should seek him to the east, and not to the south. Not to
the west, let alone across, and never in no direction at all. He should sit in
silence and not stir even in his mind. Then Brahman, lodged in his inner self,
turns towards him. A man becomes a muni by silence, not by living in the forest.
The man who knows the imperishable is called the best of munis. One who explains
all things is called a grammarian. The man who sees the worlds directly sees
everything. Standing in truth, a brahmin sees Brahman, kshatriya. I tell you
this, learned one, after the order of the Vedas."

---

"Sanatsujata," said Dhritarashtra, "you speak words of Brahman, of the highest
meaning, of every form. Speak to me, youth, of the highest teaching, which is
very hard to find among desires."

"This Brahman that you ask me about so eagerly," said Sanatsujata, "is not won
by one in haste. I will tell you the ancient knowledge of the unmanifest, won by
understanding and by the celibate life."

"You speak of the eternal knowledge of the unmanifest," said Dhritarashtra, "won
by the celibate life. It cannot be begun, and it lives here in its time, noble
one. How would a man win the state of a brahmin, and deathlessness?"

"Those in this world who conquer their desires," said Sanatsujata, "and bear
patiently the state of Brahman, draw the self out of the body, standing in their
true being, as a reed is drawn from munja grass. Father and mother make this
body, Bharata. But the birth the teacher gives is true, and free of age and
death. Those who enter the teacher's womb here, become an embryo and live the
celibate life, become the makers of teaching here. When they leave the body,
they go to the highest union.

"The man who covers the ears with truth, doing what is true and giving
deathlessness, should be thought of as father and mother. Knowing what he has
done, a man should never betray him.

"The pupil should always honour his teacher. Pure and careful, he should want to
study. He should not be proud or hold anger. That is the first quarter of the
celibate life. He should do what pleases the teacher with his life and his
wealth, in deed, mind and word. That is called the second quarter. He should
behave to the teacher's wife as to the teacher, doing as he is told and doing
what pleases. That is called the third quarter. When he has done the teacher a
service, the wise pupil should not boast of it, or even think, 'I am doing
this,' or have it said. That is the fourth quarter of the celibate life.

"Whatever wealth comes to him while he lives like this, he should hand over to
the teacher. So he comes to the way of the good, which has many virtues. He
should behave the same way to the teacher's son. Living like this, he grows in
every way here. He wins many sons and a firm place. The directions and the
spaces between rain on him, and people come to live with him in the celibate
life.

"By this celibate life the gods won their godhood, and the fortunate wise seers
the world of Brahma. By this the gandharvas and apsarases won their beauty. By
this celibate life the sun rises for the day. The wise man who would feed and
tear his whole body, king, burning it with austerity, passes beyond childhood by
this, and holds back death at the time of the end. Men win worlds that have an
end, kshatriya, by deeds that are made. But the man who knows reaches Brahman,
which is everything, by this. There is no other road to go."

"It shines as if white, as if red," said Dhritarashtra, "or black, or dark as
lampblack, or tawny. The learned brahmin sees it here. What form has that
deathless, imperishable place?"

"It does not shine as if white or red," said Sanatsujata, "or black, or the
colour of iron, or the colour of the sun. It does not stand on the earth or in
the sky. The ocean does not hold it as water. It is not found in the stars. It
does not rest in the lightning. Its form is not seen in the clouds, or in the
wind, or in the gods. It is not seen in the moon or in the sun. It is not seen
in the Riks, or the Yajus, or the Atharvans, or the spotless Samans. In the
Rathantara and the Brihat, king, and in the Great Vow, it would surely not be
seen.

"It cannot be crossed, and it lies beyond darkness. Even Death goes into it at
the time of destruction. It is smaller than the edge of a razor, and greater
than the mountains. It is the resting place. It is the deathless. It is the
worlds. It is Brahman. It is glory. Beings were born from it, and they go back
into it at the dissolution. The poets say it is free of sickness, the great
glory raised up, and that speech is only its changes. This whole world rests on
it. Those who know it become deathless.

---

"That bright one, the great light, the great glory shining, is the one the gods
worship, from whom the sun shines. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. From the
bright one Brahman arises. Brahman grows by the bright one. That bright one
among the lights, unheated, heats the heater. The yogis see him, the eternal
Lord.

"Waters, and from waters in the middle of the flood, two gods rest in the sky.
He, wearing those that go together and those that go apart, upholds both earth
and heaven. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. The bright one upholds both the
gods, the earth and heaven, the directions and the world. From him the
directions and the rivers flow. From him the great oceans were made. The yogis
see him, the eternal Lord.

"Horses draw him, standing on the wheel of the chariot of the fixed one, whose
work does not perish, him with the banner, the heavenly, ageless one in the sky.
The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. His form does not stand in likeness. No one
sees him with the eye. Those who know him by understanding, by mind and by
heart, become deathless. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"The river of twelve floods, guarded by the gods, the terrible one: those who
taste its honey go about it. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. The bee
gathers honey and drinks it for half a month. The Lord made it an offering in
all beings. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. Wingless ones fly to the fig
tree with golden leaves. There they become birds and fly off in every direction.
The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"From the full they draw out the full. From the full they made the full. They
take the full from the full, and the full alone remains. The yogis see him, the
eternal Lord. From him the wind came, and in him it always moves. From him came
fire and soma, and in him breath is spread. Know that everything comes from him.
We cannot name each thing. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"The breath swallows the downward breath. The moon swallows the breath. The sun
swallows the moon, and the highest swallows the sun. The yogis see him, the
eternal Lord. The swan, rising from the water, does not lift one foot. If he
always kept it as his priest, there would be neither death nor deathlessness.
The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. So that god, the great self, the Person,
swallows the fire. The self of the man who knows that Person does not come to
harm here. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"If a man spread a thousand thousand wings and flew, even if he were as swift as
thought, he would come to the middle only in the middle. The yogis see him, the
eternal Lord. His form does not stand in sight. Those whose being is purified
see him. The kindly wise man may see him with the mind. Those who take refuge in
him become deathless. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"Mortals hide, as snakes hide in holes, by their own teaching and their own
ways. Deluded people are confused among them, as men confuse a road and come to
danger. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. He is always honoured by the good.
For him there is no death. Where then is deathlessness? Truth and falsehood are
bound together with truth. The source of being and of not being is one and the
same. The yogis see him, the eternal Lord.

"This is not the same in men, by the good or by the bad. Know this to be the
same as deathlessness. Disciplined in this way, a man should seek that honey.
The yogis see him, the eternal Lord. Harsh words do not burn his heart, nor what
he has not studied, nor the fire offering he has not made. The mind should take
on the lightness of Brahman. The steady win a name for his wisdom. The yogis see
him, the eternal Lord.

"The man who sees the self in all beings like this, joined here and there, what
should he grieve for after that? As a great well is to a land flooded with water
on every side, so are all the Vedas to a brahmin who knows. The Person, the size
of a thumb, the great self, is not seen, though he is set in the heart. Unborn,
moving, never weary day and night, the poet who knows him sits at peace.

"I am your mother and your father. I am also your son. I am the self of all,
what is and what is not. I am the old grandfather, Bharata, the father and the
son. You live in my self. You are not mine, and I am not yours. The self is my
place, and the self is my birth. I am spoken of in the Veda, set firm in what
does not age. Smaller than the smallest, kindly, I am awake in all beings. They
know the father of all beings is placed in the lotus."

**Choices.**

* This is the *Sanatsujāta* section, one of the epic's teaching texts. It is kept in full and in order. Many verses are riddling and Vedic in style, and their sense is disputed; the prose stays as close to the words as plain English allows, and adds no commentary to the story.
* 5.43.10–11: the text announces "six" cruel ways and then lists six, and "another seven". Both lists are kept as given.
* 5.43.15–17: the eighteen faults of self-control are listed as the text gives them; the count follows the text.
* 5.43.18–20: the six kinds of renunciation are hard to separate in the verses. The division follows the plain sense.
* 5.43.21: the "eight faults" of carelessness are not listed in the CE verse.
* 5.43.33: the directions in which to seek the teacher are the text's.
* 5.44.4 *muñjād iṣīkām iva*: "as a reed is drawn from munja grass", a Vedic image.
* 5.44.21: the Rathantara and the Brihat are chants of the Sama Veda; the Great Vow is a rite.
* 5.45: the refrain *yoginas taṃ prapaśyanti bhagavantaṃ sanātanam* is kept in every verse where it stands.
* 5.45.10: "From the full they draw out the full" echoes an Upanishadic verse; it is rendered literally.
* 5.45.14: the swan that does not lift one foot from the water is the text's own image.
* 5.45.25–28: the speaker is Sanatsujata, speaking as the self.
