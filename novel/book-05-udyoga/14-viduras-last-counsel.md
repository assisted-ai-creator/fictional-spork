# Vidura's Last Counsel

"When an old man comes near," said Vidura, "a young man's life-breaths rise up
and try to leave him. By rising to meet him and greeting him, he gets them back.

"A steady man first gives a good guest a seat, brings water and washes his feet,
asks after his comfort and tells him of his own. Then, after looking to it, he
gives him food. The noble say that a man lives in vain if a brahmin who knows
the sacred verses takes water, honey-milk and a cow in his house and he gives
them out of greed, fear or meanness.

"Some guests do not deserve the water, even if they are very dear: a doctor, a
maker of arrows, one who has broken his vow of chastity, a thief, a cruel man, a
drinker, a killer of a foetus, a soldier for hire, and one who sells the Veda.

"Salt, cooked food, curds, milk, honey, oil and ghee, sesame, meat, roots and
fruits and greens, red cloth, every kind of scent, and molasses should not be
sold.

"The man who is free of anger, to whom a clod of earth and gold are the same, is
a mendicant. He has left sorrow behind and is done with alliance and war. He has
turned away from blame and praise, and goes about as if unconcerned with what is
pleasant and unpleasant. The man who lives on wild rice, roots, ingudi nuts and
greens, is well controlled, keeps the fire rites without being told, lives in
the forest and is attentive to guests, is the ascetic who carries the load and
does holy works.

"Having wronged a wise man, a man should not feel safe because he is far away.
The wise man's arms are long, and with them, when he is hurt, he hurts back.

"Do not trust the untrustworthy, and do not trust the trustworthy too much. Fear
born of trust cuts even the roots.

"A man should be free of jealousy and guard his wife. He should share, and speak
kindly. He should be gentle and sweet-spoken with women, but not fall under
their power. Women are to be honoured, fortunate, pure, the lights of the house.
They are called the fortune of the house, and so they must be guarded above all.

"A man should give the women's rooms to his father's care, and the kitchen to
his mother's. He should give the cattle to one like himself, and go to the
fields himself. He should carry on trade through his servants, and serve the
brahmins through his sons.

"Fire rose from water, the kshatriya from the brahmin, iron from stone. Their
power reaches everywhere, but it is quenched in the place it came from. The
good, born in good families, with power like fire, always lie forgiving and
without sign, like fire in wood.

"The king whose counsel is not known by those outside or those within, and who
has eyes everywhere, enjoys his power long. He should not speak of what he means
to do, but show what he has done. Then his counsel on dharma, pleasure and
profit is not broken. Counsel should be held on a mountain top, or on a palace
roof, alone, or in a forest free of reeds. One who is not a friend, Bharata,
should not know the highest counsel, nor a friend who is not wise, nor a wise
one who is not master of himself. In a minister there should be both the wish to
gain wealth and the power to keep counsel. When his councillors know only what
has been done, the king who keeps his counsel secret surely succeeds.

"The man who in his folly does deeds that are not praised falls with their
failure, even from life. The doing of praised deeds brings happiness. Not doing
them brings great regret afterwards.

"The earth is in the power of a king who knows standing still, growth and
decline, who knows the six measures of policy, and whose conduct is not scorned.
This earth rich in wealth belongs to the king whose anger and joy are not in
vain, who looks into his own affairs himself, and whose treasury is known to
himself. A king should be content with the name and the parasol. He should share
wealth with his servants, and not take everything alone.

"A brahmin knows a brahmin. A husband knows his wife. A king knows his minister,
and a king knows a king.

"An enemy who has fallen into your lap, and deserves to die, should not be let
go. From an enemy not killed, fear soon arises.

"A man should always take care to hold back his anger towards the gods, towards
kings and brahmins, and towards the old, the young and the sick. A wise man
should avoid pointless quarrels, which fools love. He wins fame in the world and
is not joined to misfortune. People do not want a master whose favour bears no
fruit and whose anger has no point, as women do not want a eunuch for a husband.

"Understanding does not bring wealth, nor dullness poverty. The wise man knows
the turns of the world's affairs, and no one else does.

"The fool always scorns those who are older in learning, character, age and
understanding, and older in wealth and birth, Bharata. Misfortunes come quickly
to the man who behaves ignobly, who is unwise, envious and lawless, foul-mouthed
and quick to anger.

"Keeping one's word, giving, not breaking an agreement, and speech well directed
win creatures over. The man who keeps his word, who is skilful, grateful,
thoughtful and honest, finds followers even when his treasury is empty.

"Firmness, calm, self-control, purity, compassion, speech that is not harsh, and
no betrayal of friends: these seven are the kindling of fortune. The man who
does not share, whose heart is wicked, who is ungrateful and shameless, is the
lowest of men, king, and should be avoided in the world.

"A guilty man who angers the innocent people of his household does not sleep
happily at night, any more than in a house with a snake in it. Those whose anger
would harm your welfare, Bharata, should always be kept happy, like gods.

"Wealth that is entrusted to women, to the careless and to the base is all in
doubt. Countries where a woman, a gambler or a child gives the orders sink
helplessly, like stone rafts in a river. I think those are wise who hold to what
is needed and not to fine distinctions, for fine distinctions lead to quarrels.
The man whom gamblers praise, whom bards praise, whom loose women praise, does
not live.

"You have passed over the Pandavas, great archers of boundless power, and put
the great lordship of the Bharatas in Duryodhana's hands. So you will soon see
him fall from it, dazed with the pride of power, as Bali fell from the three
worlds."

---

"Man is not master of his own rise and fall," said Dhritarashtra. "He is like a
wooden doll on a string. The Creator has put him in the power of fate. So speak,
and I will listen."

"Even Brihaspati, if he spoke out of season," said Vidura, "would have his
wisdom scorned and be dishonoured, Bharata. One man is loved for his gifts,
another for his kind words, another for his counsel and the strength of his
roots. But one who is loved is simply loved. The man who is hated is not thought
good, nor wise, nor learned. In the one we love we see good deeds, and in the
one we hate, bad deeds, Bharata. That is no loss, great king, which brings
growth. That should be counted a loss which, when gained, destroys much. Some
grow rich in virtues and others in wealth. Avoid those rich in wealth and poor
in virtue, Dhritarashtra."

"All you say looks to the future," said Dhritarashtra, "and the wise approve it.
But I cannot give up my son. Where there is dharma, there is victory."

"A man who has good qualities by nature, and is well trained," said Vidura,
"will never cause even the smallest harm to creatures. But there are men who
love to slander others, who always strive to cause others pain and to set them
against each other. To see them is to see evil, and to live with them is great
danger. It is a great wrong to take from them, and great danger to give to them.
A man should avoid those who are known as wicked, blamed by all who live with
them, and tainted with other great faults. When friendship with a low man ends,
his affection is lost, and so are the fruit of it and the happiness of
friendship. He works to slander you and strives to destroy you. Even for a small
wrong, in his folly, he does not come to peace. When a wise man has looked
closely, with his understanding, at such low, cruel, undisciplined men, he
should avoid them from afar.

"The man who helps a kinsman who is poor, wretched or sick grows in sons and
cattle, and wins fame that does not fade. Those who want their own good should
help their kin to grow, king of kings, and the growth of their family. So act
well. You will be joined to good fortune, king, if you honour your kin. Even kin
without virtues should be protected, bull of the Bharatas, let alone those with
virtues, who look to your favour. Be gracious to the wretched Pandavas, lord of
the people. Let them be given some villages to live on, lord. So you will win
fame in the world, lord of men.

"You are old, my father, and you must protect your sons. I too must speak for
your good. Know that I wish you well. A man who wants to prosper should not
quarrel with his kin, my father. Pleasures should be enjoyed with kin, bull of
the Bharatas. Eating together, talking together and loving each other are things
to be done with kin, never quarrelling. Kin carry us across here, and kin drown
us. The good carry us across, and the bad drown us. Be good to the Pandavas,
king of kings, giver of honour. Surrounded by them, you will be beyond the reach
of your enemies. When a kinsman comes to a prosperous kinsman and sinks, the
prosperous man takes on his sin, like a deer that comes near a hunter with a
poisoned arrow.

"Afterwards, best of men, you will grieve, when you hear that they, or your
sons, are dead. Think of that now. Life is uncertain. A man should not do at
first a deed that will make him writhe on his bed. No man but Bhargava, Shukra,
never errs. But what to do next is in the hands of the wise. If Duryodhana has
done this wrong to them before, you, the elder of the family, should set it
right, lord of men. Put them back in their place, and you will be free of blame
in the world, best of men, and honoured by the wise.

"The man who thinks over the good sayings of steady men for their fruit, and
acts on them in his affairs, stays long in fame. Discipline kills poverty.
Courage kills misfortune. Forgiveness always kills anger, and good conduct kills
ill luck. A family should be tested, king, by its belongings, its fields, its
house, its service, its food and its clothes.

"When heart meets heart, secret meets secret, and wisdom meets wisdom between
two men, their friendship does not wear out. A wise man should avoid the fool of
unformed wisdom, like a well hidden by grass, for friendship with him perishes.
The wise man should not make friends with the proud, with fools, with the fierce
and reckless, or with those who have fallen from dharma. A grateful, righteous,
truthful, generous, loyal man who has mastered his senses, who keeps to his
place and does not abandon his friend, is wanted as a friend.

"Not letting the senses go is no better than death. But letting them go too far
would destroy even the gods. The wise say that gentleness to all creatures,
freedom from envy, forgiveness, firmness and not slighting one's friends
lengthen life. The man who, with firm resolve, seeks to win back by good policy
what was lost by bad policy keeps the vow of a true man. The man who knows how
to meet the future, who is firm in the present, and who knows what is left to do
from the past, does not lose his wealth.

"What a man does again and again, in deed, mind and speech, that carries him
away. So he should do good. Touching what is auspicious, discipline, learning,
effort, honesty, and seeing the good often bring prosperity. Not losing heart is
the root of fortune, and of happiness when sorrow ends. The man who does not
lose heart becomes great and enjoys lasting happiness. Nothing is more
fortunate, nothing more wholesome, my father, than forgiveness in a man of
power, everywhere and always. The weak man should forgive everyone. The strong
man should forgive for the sake of dharma. For the man to whom gain and loss are
equal, forgiveness is always good.

"A man may freely enjoy the happiness that does not make him lose dharma and
profit. He should not keep a fool's vow. Fortune does not live with those who
are overcome by sorrow, the careless, unbelievers, the idle, the undisciplined,
and those without energy. Fools think an honest man, who is modest because he is
honest, is weak, and they attack him. Fortune does not come near, out of fear,
to the man who is too noble, too generous, too brave, too strict in his vows, or
too proud of his wisdom.

"The fruit of the Vedas is the fire offering. The fruit of learning is character
and conduct. The fruit of a wife is love and sons. The fruit of wealth is giving
and enjoying. A man who performs the rites for the dead with wealth gained
against dharma does not enjoy their fruit after death, because the wealth was
badly got.

"In wild forests and strongholds, in hard misfortunes and panic, and when
weapons are raised, those who have life left in them have no fear. Effort,
restraint, skill, care, firmness, memory, and beginning after due thought: know
that these are the root of prosperity. Austerity is the strength of ascetics.
Brahman is the strength of those who know Brahman. Violence is the strength of
the wicked. Forgiveness is the strength of the virtuous.

"These eight do not break a vow: water, roots, fruit, milk, ghee, what a brahmin
asks for, a teacher's word, and medicine.

"A man should not do to another what he would not like done to himself. That, in
short, is dharma. Anything else comes from desire.

"Conquer anger with calm. Conquer the bad with good. Conquer the miser with
giving. Conquer the liar with truth.

"Do not trust a woman-cheat, an idler, a coward, a fierce man, one who boasts of
his manhood, a thief, an ungrateful man, or an unbeliever. For the man who
greets others respectfully and always serves the old, four things grow: fame,
life, glory and strength.

"Do not set your mind on wealth that comes with too much hardship, or by
breaking dharma, or by bowing to an enemy. A man without learning is to be
pitied. A couple without children is to be pitied. A people without food is to
be pitied, and a kingdom without a king.

"The road is old age to embodied creatures. Water is old age to mountains. Going
without love is old age to women. The dart of words is old age to the mind. Not
reciting is the stain of the Vedas, and breaking vows is the stain of a brahmin.
Curiosity is the stain of a good woman, and living away from home is the stain
of women. Silver is the stain of gold, tin the stain of silver, lead the stain
of tin, and dirt the stain of lead.

"Do not try to conquer sleep with sleep, a woman with desire, fire with fuel, or
drink with drinking. The man whose friend is won by gifts, whose enemies are
beaten in war, and whose wife is won by food and drink, has a fruitful life.

"Those with a thousand live, and those with a hundred live. Dhritarashtra, let
go of your desire. There is no one who cannot live somehow. All the rice and
barley on earth, the gold, the cattle and the women, are not enough for one man.
The man who sees that is not deluded. King, I tell you again: treat your sons
and the sons of Pandu alike, if you have equal feeling for your own sons and the
sons of Pandu."

---

"The man who, when the good ask him, does what they want without attachment and
without sparing his strength," said Vidura, "soon wins fame, for the good, when
pleased, bring happiness. The man who gives up great wealth joined to what is
against dharma, before anyone can cry out against him, lays down his sorrows and
sleeps happily, like a snake that has shed its old skin.

"Lying to rise high, carrying tales to the king, and pressing a false charge
against a teacher are equal to killing a brahmin. Envy is one step from death.
Too much talk kills fortune. Not wanting to listen, haste and boasting are the
three enemies of learning. Where is learning for the one who wants comfort?
There is no comfort for the one who wants learning. The one who wants comfort
should give up learning, or the one who wants learning should give up comfort.

"Fire is never satisfied with wood, nor the great ocean with rivers, nor Death
with all beings, nor a woman with beautiful eyes with men.

"Hope kills firmness. Death kills prosperity. Anger kills fortune. Meanness
kills fame. Neglect kills cattle, king. One angry brahmin kills a kingdom.

"Goats, bronze, a chariot, honey, an antidote, a bird, a learned brahmin, an old
kinsman and a friend who has fallen low: let these always be in your house.
Goats, bulls, sandal, the vina, a mirror, honey and ghee, poison, copper, a
conch, gold, a navel-shell and yellow pigment: Manu said these auspicious things
should be kept in the house, Bharata, to worship gods and brahmins, and for
guests.

"And I tell you this, my father, above everything, a holy word of the highest
worth: never give up dharma out of desire, or fear, or greed, not even to save
your life. Dharma is eternal. Pleasure and pain are not. The soul is eternal,
but its cause is not. Give up what does not last and stand firm in what lasts.
Be content, for contentment is the highest gain.

"Look at the great kings, mighty and glorious, who ruled the earth full of
wealth and grain, who left their kingdoms and their great pleasures and have
gone into the power of Death. When a son dies, whom they reared through sorrow,
people lift him up, king, and carry him out of the house. With hair unbound,
weeping piteously, they throw him on the pyre like a log. Another man enjoys the
dead man's wealth. The birds and the fire enjoy the elements of his body. He
goes to the other world with two companions, his good and his evil, wrapped in
them. His kin, his friends and his sons leave him and turn back. His own deeds
follow the man thrown into the fire.

"Above this world and below the other there is great darkness, a blinding dark.
Know that it is the great delusion of the senses, king, and do not let it take
hold of you. If you can hear all these words and act on them, you will win the
highest fame in the world of the living, and you will have no fear, here or
hereafter.

"The self is a river, Bharata, with holy fords. Truth is its water, firmness its
banks, self-control its waves. The man who does holy deeds is purified by
bathing in it, for the self is always holy, and water is only water. Make
firmness your boat and cross the river whose water is the five senses and whose
crocodiles are desire and anger, and cross the hard places of birth.

"The man who honours and wins over a kinsman who is old in wisdom, old in
dharma, old in learning and old in years, and asks him what should and should
not be done, is never bewildered. By firmness guard the loins and the belly. By
the eyes guard the hands and the feet. By the mind guard the eyes and ears. By
deeds guard the mind and speech.

"The brahmin who always keeps his water rites, always wears the sacred thread,
always recites, avoids the food of the fallen, speaks the truth and serves his
teacher, does not fall from the world of Brahma. The kshatriya who studies the
Vedas, tends the sacred fires, offers sacrifices and protects his people, and is
killed in battle for the sake of cows and brahmins, his inner self purified by
weapons, goes to heaven. The vaishya who studies, and shares his wealth in time
with brahmins, kshatriyas and those who depend on him, smells the holy smoke of
the three fires and enjoys the pleasures of the gods in heaven after death. The
shudra who honours the brahmin, the kshatriya and the vaishya in turn, as is
right, and pleases them, is free of pain, his sins burned away, and when he
leaves his body he enjoys the pleasures of heaven.

"I have told you the dharma of the four classes. Hear why I speak of it. The son
of Pandu is falling away from the kshatriya's dharma. Set him to the dharma of
kings, king."

"It is as you always teach me," said Dhritarashtra. "My own mind, gentle one, is
as you say. But though I make up my mind like this towards the Pandavas, when I
meet Duryodhana it turns again. No mortal can pass beyond what is fated. I think
fate alone acts, and man's effort is useless."

---

"If there is anything you have not yet said in words, Vidura," said
Dhritarashtra, "tell me, for I want to hear it. What you say is wonderful."

"Dhritarashtra," said Vidura, "the ancient, eternal youth Sanatsujata has said
that there is no death, Bharata. He, the best of all the wise, will tell you all
the secret and open things that live in your heart, great king."

"Do you not know what that eternal one would tell me?" said Dhritarashtra. "Tell
me yourself, Vidura, if any wisdom is left in you."

"I was born of a shudra woman," said Vidura, "so I dare not say more than this.
But I know the youth's understanding, which is eternal. One who is born of a
brahmin womb may speak even what is most secret, and the gods do not blame him.
That is why I tell you this."

"Tell me, Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "how can I meet that ancient, eternal one
here, in this body?"

Then Vidura thought of the seer, firm in his vows. The seer knew he was being
thought of, and showed himself, Bharata. Vidura received him with the rites the
scriptures lay down. When he was seated at ease and rested, Vidura said to him,
"Lord, there is a doubt in Dhritarashtra's mind that I cannot speak to. Speak to
him of it, so that when he has heard it this lord of men may pass beyond
pleasure and pain. Then gain and loss, the dear and the hateful, old age and
death, fear and anger, hunger and thirst, pride and prosperity, discontent and
sloth, desire and anger, decay and growth, will not overcome him."

**Divergences.**

* **"Give up this one son."** A rejected passage here (\*231) has Vidura remind the king that he told him at Duryodhana's birth to give up that one son for the sake of the hundred. The advice itself is CE in Book 1, spoken by Vidura and the brahmins at the birth (1.107.29–32). It is not repeated here.
* **An appendix passage** after 5.39.31 (App. I No. 1) adds sayings on the six doors through which counsel leaks. Not narrated.
* **Fortune, blind and mad.** A rejected line (\*237) says Fortune wanders like a mad, blind cow. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*226–\*230, \*232–\*236, \*238–\*242) add sayings, including the seven faults of students (\*240). Not narrated.
