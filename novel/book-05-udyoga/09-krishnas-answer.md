summary: Krishna tells Sanjaya he wants both the Pandavas and Dhritarashtra's sons to prosper, but that action, not idleness, is the law of the world and of the gods; each class has its own duty, a king must punish the one who seizes what is not his, and a thief is a thief whether seen or unseen; he recalls how Draupadi was dragged into the hall and insulted by Karna, Duhshasana and Shakuni while the Kurus looked on; he will go to Hastinapura himself to try for peace; Duryodhana is a tree of anger, Yudhishthira a tree of dharma, and the two sides are like the forest and the tiger; Sanjaya takes his leave, and Yudhishthira sends greetings to every one in Hastinapura, from Bhishma to the servants and the blind, and ends: give me Indraprastha, or fight.
---

# Krishna's Answer

"Sanjaya," said Vasudeva, "I want the Pandavas to come to no harm. I want their
prosperity and what is dear to them. And I always wish growth, suta, to King
Dhritarashtra, who has many sons. It is always my wish, Sanjaya, and I would say
nothing else to them than 'Make peace.' I hear that this is what the king wants,
and I think it is fitting for the Pandavas.

"Peace here is very hard to make, Sanjaya, and the Pandava has shown it. When
Dhritarashtra and his sons are so greedy, how can a quarrel not grow between
them? You know from me and from Yudhishthira, Sanjaya, that I do not stray from
dharma. Why then do you speak of the ruin of the Pandava's good name, when he is
eager and doing his own work, living with his family as the ancient rule says?

"While this rule stands, the brahmins hold views high and low. Some say success
in the next world comes by action. Some say it comes by knowledge, giving up
action. But a man who does not eat does not feel full, however much he knows
about food. The knowledge that gets work done bears fruit, and no other. The
fruit of action is seen here. A man suffering from thirst drinks water and is at
peace. This rule is laid down by action, Sanjaya, and action is in it. Anyone
who thinks something other than action is better talks in vain, and he is weak.

"By action the gods shine in the other world. By action the wind blows here. By
action, never tiring, the sun rises every day and makes the day and night. Never
tiring, the moon goes through the months and half-months and the conjunctions of
the stars. Never tiring, the fire burns when it is kindled, doing its work for
creatures. Never tiring, the goddess Earth bears this great burden by her
strength. Never tiring, the rivers carry their waters swiftly and refresh all
beings. Never tiring, the mighty one rains, making sky and heaven roar. Never
tiring, the slayer of Bala lived the life of chastity, wanting to be the best of
the gods. He gave up comfort and what was dear to his mind, and by that action
Shakra won the highest place. Guarding truth and dharma with care, and
self-control, patience, fairness and kindness, practising them all, Maghavan won
the first place, the kingdom of the gods. Brihaspati lived the life of chastity,
as he should, with his mind collected and his self disciplined. He gave up
comfort and held back his senses, and by that he won honour among the gods. The
stars shine in the other world by action, and the Rudras, the Adityas, the Vasus
and the All-gods, King Yama, Kubera the son of Vishravas, and the bright
gandharvas, yakshas and apsarases. The sages shine in the other world by
practising chastity, the knowledge of the Veda, and the rites.

"You know this dharma of all the world, of brahmins, kshatriyas and vaishyas.
Why then do you, who know, and who are wise among the wise, strive for the
Kauravas, Sanjaya? Know that he is always joined to the sacred texts, to the
horse sacrifice and to the Rajasuya. And he is joined also to the bow and the
armour, to the hand-guards, the chariots and the weapons. If the Parthas could
find a way with the Kauravas without killing, they would have done a holy thing
to save dharma, by holding back Bhimasena in noble conduct. If, doing the work
of their fathers, they met death by the power of fate, doing their own work as
far as they could, even that death would be praised.

"Or do you think that the whole law of dharma, for kings, lies in fighting? Or
in not fighting? I will hear what you say. First look at the division of the
four classes, Sanjaya, and at your own work. Then hear the work of the Pandavas,
and praise or blame them, as you think.

"A brahmin should study and sacrifice, give gifts and go to the chief places of
pilgrimage. He should teach, and sacrifice for those who are fit, and accept
gifts that are known. In the same way a kshatriya, after protecting his people
by dharma, carefully, and giving gifts, offering sacrifices and studying all the
Vedas, should marry and live in his house, doing holy works. A vaishya should
study, and gather wealth by farming, herding and trade, and keep it carefully.
Doing what pleases the brahmins and kshatriyas, a man of dharma, doing holy
works, he should live in his house. Serving the brahmins and bowing to them: the
shudra should not study, and sacrifice is forbidden him. He should always be up
and working for prosperity, never tiring. This is the ancient dharma of the
shudra.

"A king guards all these with care, and sets all the classes to their own
duties. He is free of desire and fair to all his people, and he does not follow
desires that are against dharma. If there is someone better than he, known and
full of every virtue, let him, who knows, correct the wicked one. If he does not
want to, that is not good.

"When a cruel man covets another's land, and takes power by overturning the
rule, then the kings go to war. That is why armour, weapon and bow came to be.
Indra made them, armour, weapon and bow, for this work, to kill the robbers.

"A thief who steals wealth unseen, and one who seizes it by force in the open,
are both to be blamed, Sanjaya. How is Dhritarashtra's son any different? Out of
greed he thinks this is dharma, what he wants, following his anger. But the
Pandavas' share is settled. Why should others take it from us for no reason? If
we die fighting on this ground, even death is to be praised, for a father's
kingdom is better than another's. Tell the Kauravas these ancient laws, Sanjaya,
in the midst of the kingdom, and tell those fools whom Dhritarashtra's son has
gathered, who have fallen into the power of death.

---

"And look at the deed of the Kurus in the hall, which was worse still. The
Kurus, with Bhishma at their head, looked on at Draupadi, the Pandavas' dear
wife, famous, good in character and conduct, weeping, held by a man who followed
his desire. If the Kurus gathered there, young and old, had stopped him then,
Dhritarashtra would have done what I want, and done right by his sons.

"Duhshasana dragged Krishnaa into the hall, among her fathers-in-law, against
all that is proper. Brought there, she spoke pitifully, and saw no protector but
the Steward. Out of weakness, the kings gathered there could not answer in the
hall. Only the Steward spoke of what was right. He knew dharma, and he answered
that man of small mind. You did not speak of dharma like this in the hall, and
now you want to teach the Pandava. But Krishnaa did a pure deed there, and a
very hard one, when she came into the hall. By it she lifted the Pandavas out of
their trouble, and herself too, like a boat out of the ocean's flood.

"There, in the hall, where Krishnaa stood near her fathers-in-law, the suta's
son said to her, 'You have nowhere to go, Yajnaseni. Go now into the house of
the son of Dhritarashtra. Your husbands are beaten. They are nothing. Choose
another husband, lovely one.' That arrow of words from Karna, sharp and very
terrible, that cuts to the bone and strikes the vital parts, went deep into
Bibhatsu's heart, and it is lodged in Phalguna's heart still. When they were
putting on the black antelope skins, Duhshasana said bitter things to them:
'These are all lost, like barren sesame seeds. They have gone to ruin, to hell,
for a long time.' And at the time of the dice, the king of Gandhara, Shakuni,
said to the Parthas with his trickery, 'Nakula is lost. What do you have left?
Play with Krishnaa Yajnaseni.'

"You know all this, Sanjaya, the words that should never have been spoken at the
dice, as they were spoken. Now I myself want to go there, to set right this
matter that has gone wrong. If I can bring about peace for the Kurus without
losing the Pandavas' purpose, I will have done a holy deed of great fruit, and
the Kurus will be freed from the noose of death. Perhaps, when I speak words of
wisdom, full of dharma and profit, and free of harm, the sons of Dhritarashtra
will listen in my presence, and the Kurus will honour me when I come. If not,
know that the sons of Dhritarashtra, those fools, will be overthrown by Phalguna
in his chariot and by Bhima armed for battle, burned by their own deeds.

"The son of Dhritarashtra spoke cruel words to the defeated Pandavas. Bhimasena,
mace in hand, watchful, will surely remind Duryodhana of them when the time
comes.

"Suyodhana is a great tree made of anger. Karna is its trunk, Shakuni its
branches. Duhshasana is its rich flowers and fruit. Its root is foolish King
Dhritarashtra. Yudhishthira is a great tree made of dharma. Arjuna is its trunk,
Bhimasena its branches. The sons of Madri are its rich flowers and fruit. Its
root is I, and Brahman, and the brahmins.

"King Dhritarashtra and his sons are a forest, Sanjaya, and the sons of Pandu
are the tigers in it. Do not cut down the forest with its tigers. Do not drive
the tigers out of the forest. A tiger without a forest is killed. A forest
without a tiger is cut down. So the tiger should guard the forest, and the
forest should protect the tiger. The sons of Dhritarashtra are creepers,
Sanjaya, and the Pandavas are shala trees. A creeper never grows without leaning
on a great tree.

"The Parthas, tamers of enemies, stand ready to serve, and they stand ready to
fight. Let the lord of men, Dhritarashtra, do what he must do. The great
Pandavas, who walk in dharma, stand firm for peace, and they are mighty
fighters. Tell him this truly, learned one."

---

"I take my leave of you, god among kings," said Sanjaya. "I am going, Pandava.
May all be well with you. I hope I have not let any wrong word slip from my
tongue through the heat of my mind. I take my leave of Janardana, of Bhimasena
and Arjuna, of the sons of Madri, of Satyaki and Chekitana, and I go. May you be
well and happy. Look on me with kind eyes, kings."

"Go, Sanjaya, with our leave, and be well," said Yudhishthira. "You have never
done us any wrong. We know you, and so do they, all of us, as pure of heart, one
who stands in the middle, in the hall. You are a trusted envoy, Sanjaya, and
very dear to us. Your words are kind, your character good, and you see clearly.
You are never confused in your mind, Sanjaya, and you are never angry, even when
you are told the truth. You never speak harsh words that cut to the quick, nor
bitter flattery, nor sour words. I know your speech, suta, full of dharma and
profit, and free of harm. You are the envoy dearest to us here. Vidura might
come as a second. We used to see you often before. You are a friend as dear to
us as Dhananjaya's own self.

"Go quickly from here, Sanjaya, and wait on the brahmins who deserve it, those
of pure power, of good conduct, well born and full of every virtue. Greet from
me the brahmins who study the Veda, the mendicants, the ascetics who live always
in the forests, and the old men, and ask the others how they are. Meet King
Dhritarashtra's priest, and his teachers, and his officiating priests, as is
fitting, my son, and ask how they are, suta.

"Drona is to be greeted as he deserves, the teacher we love, who never turned
from us and guided us well, who lived as a celibate to learn the Veda, who made
the science of weapons four-footed again, and who is kind to us. Ask after the
health of Ashvatthama, who has finished his learning and is of good conduct, who
also made the science of weapons four-footed again, fierce, like the son of a
gandharva. Go to the house of Sharadvat's son, the great chariot fighter, best
of those who know weapons, and, naming me again and again, touch Kripa's feet
with your hand, Sanjaya. Take the feet of Bhishma, best of the Kurus, in whom
there are courage and kindness, austerity, wisdom and character, learning,
strength and firmness, and tell him of me.

"Greet the old king, the wise leader of the Kurus, whose eyes are his
understanding, who is learned and serves his elders, and tell him, Sanjaya, that
I am well. Ask after the health of Suyodhana, my friend, the eldest son of
Dhritarashtra, foolish, stupid, deceitful, wicked, who rules the whole earth.
Ask after the health of Duhshasana, my friend, his younger brother, just as
foolish and always of the same character, a great archer, bravest of the Kurus.

"Greet, as before, the wise bull of the Bahlikas, whose only wish is always
peace among the Bharatas. I think Somadatta is to be honoured, who has many fine
virtues, who is wise and never cruel, and who, out of affection, always bears
with anger. Somadatta's son is the most worthy of the Kurus. He is our brother,
Sanjaya, and my friend, a great archer, the best of chariot fighters. Ask after
his health, and his ministers'. And the other young leaders of the Kurus, our
sons, grandsons and brothers: whichever of them you meet, give each my greeting,
as fits him.

"The kings whom the son of Dhritarashtra has brought together to fight the
Pandavas, the Vasatis, the Shalvakas, the Kekayas, the Ambashthas and the chief
Trigartas, the heroes of the east, the north, the south and the west, and all
the mountain men who are not cruel and are of good character and conduct: ask
after the health of them all. The elephant riders, the chariot fighters, the
horsemen, and the great hosts of noble foot soldiers: tell them I am well, and
ask after the health of all of them. And the king's ministers who look after his
wealth, his doorkeepers, those who lead his army, those who reckon his income
and spending, and those who think about his great affairs.

"Ask after the health of the vaishya woman's son, a leader of the young, a poet,
never confused in affairs, very wise and full of every virtue, who never likes
war. Ask after Chitrasena, who is without equal at cheating and dice, whose
tricks are hidden, a good player who knows the dice, and who cannot be beaten in
a contest at dice. Ask after Shakuni, king of Gandhara, the mountain man, who
has no equal at cheating at dice, who feeds the pride of Dhritarashtra's son and
whose mind is false. Ask after Vaikartana, the hero who dares to beat the
unassailable Pandavas with a single chariot, who has no equal at deluding the
deluded. Ask after Vidura, our counsellor, whose wisdom has no bottom and who
sees far, who is devoted to us, our teacher, our servant, our father and mother
and friend.

"Meet the old women, Sanjaya, who are known for their virtues and are mothers to
us. Meet them all together and greet them, and say, 'Are your sons behaving well
to you, and kindly, mothers of living sons?' Then tell them, Sanjaya, that
Ajatashatru is well, with his sons. Ask after the health of all the women you
know there who are our wives, Sanjaya: 'Are you well guarded, fragrant,
blameless? Do you live carefully in your houses? Do you behave well and kindly
to your fathers-in-law, good women? Make your conduct such that your husbands
are pleased with you.' Meet our daughters-in-law, whom you know there, who have
come from good families and are full of virtues and have children, and tell
them, 'Yudhishthira greets you with love.' Embrace the girls in their houses,
Sanjaya, and ask how they are in my name: 'May your husbands be good and kind to
you, and may you be kind to your husbands.'

"Ask how the courtesans are, my friend, adorned, well dressed and fragrant,
pleasant, happy and prosperous, light in their looks and light in their words.
Tell the slave women's sons and the slaves of the Kurus, and the many hunchbacks
and lame who depend on them, that I am well, and ask last how they are. Is the
old way of living still kept? Does the son of Dhritarashtra give them their
comforts? Does Dhritarashtra, out of kindness, keep those who have lost limbs,
the poor and the dwarfs? And the blind, all of them, and the old, and the many
who live by their hands there: tell them I am well, and ask last how they are.
Say to them: 'Do not be afraid of sorrow, or of a wretched life. Surely some sin
was done in other lives. When I have held back my enemies and helped my friends,
I will keep you with food and clothes.'

"I have made gifts to the brahmins, and they are still to come, but alas, they
are not being kept. I see men fit for them there too. Tell the king that this is
what I want. And those who are without protectors, who are always weak and
struggle on their own, the foolish, the poor: ask after their health in my name,
my friend, in every way. And the others who have come from many quarters to take
refuge with the sons of Dhritarashtra, suta's son: when you see them, and all
who deserve it, ask how they are, and that they are unharmed. Ask all those who
have come and are coming, the envoys of the king who have come from every
quarter, how they are, suta, and then tell them I am well.

"There are no other fighters on earth like those whom the son of Dhritarashtra
has won. But dharma is eternal, and dharma is my great strength for destroying
my enemies. And tell Suyodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, this too, Sanjaya:
'The desire that torments the heart in your body, to rule the Kurus without a
rival, has no reason in it. We will not be the kind of men who do what you like.
Give me back Shakra's city, or fight, hero, first of the Bharatas.'"

**Divergences.**

* **"Govinda!"** In the dice scene of Book 2, Draupadi's prayer to Krishna is a rejected passage, and the CE names no cause for the endless garments (ledger L-19). Here Krishna recalls that in the hall she "saw no protector but the Steward" (5.29.33), and that her own deed rescued the Pandavas (5.29.35). Later in this Book, in the CE text, he says it is a debt that has grown and does not leave his heart, "that Krishnaa cried 'Govinda!' to me when I was far away" (5.58.21). The novel tells each as the text gives it and does not join them.
* **The kshatriya's duty.** A rejected passage (\*139) sets out the kshatriya's duties as a separate verse. The CE's 5.29.22 covers the king.
* **Rejected lines in Krishna's speech** (\*138, \*140–\*149) add, among other things, that the Kurus have ignored the ancient dharma of kings and a further form of the forest and tiger image. Not narrated.
* **Rejected greetings.** Rejected lines in Yudhishthira's message (\*150–\*159) add greetings to aged men of no learning, to traders and farmers, to Gandhari with Kunti, to Bhurishravas, Shalya, Shala and others, and a greeting to the "sons of Bhimasena". Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.29.4 *purākalpāt*: "as the ancient rule says".
* 5.29.16 *asya*: "he", the kshatriya, joined to sacred texts and sacrifices, and to bow and armour.
* 5.29.17 *ārye vṛtte bhīmasenaṃ nigṛhya*: "by holding back Bhimasena in noble conduct".
* 5.29.26: "If there is someone better than he … let him correct the wicked one" follows the CE's compressed verse.
* 5.29.27: the text says Indra made armour, weapon and bow "to kill the robbers".
* 5.29.33 *nānyaṃ kṣattur nātham adṛṣṭa kaṃ cit*: "saw no protector but the Steward", Vidura.
* 5.29.35 *naur iva sāgaraughāt*: "like a boat out of the ocean's flood", the text's image for Draupadi's rescue of the Pandavas (2.63–64).
* 5.29.38 *ṣaṇḍhatilāḥ*: "barren sesame seeds", Duhshasana's taunt (2.68.8, 13–14).
* 5.29.45–49: the two trees, the forest and the tiger, the creeper and the shala tree are kept whole.
* 5.30.10–11 *yo 'straṃ catuṣpāt punar eva cakre*: Drona and Ashvatthama each "made the science of weapons four-footed again", that is, complete in all its parts.
* 5.30.15–16: Yudhishthira's frank words about Duryodhana and Duhshasana are part of his greeting. He calls each *tāta*, "my friend".
* 5.30.18: *citrasenaḥ* is a son of Dhritarashtra, praised here as a dice player. *durjayo devitavyena saṃkhye*: "cannot be beaten in a contest at dice".
* 5.30.19: "as before" renders *purā yathā*, of Bahlika greeting him.
* 5.30.29 *sa eva bhaktaḥ sa guruḥ sa bhṛtyaḥ*: Vidura is "our teacher, our servant, our father and mother and friend".
* 5.30.36 *veśastriyaḥ*: "courtesans", greeted as the text greets them.
* 5.30.41: a compressed verse about his gifts to brahmins. The novel follows the plainest reading.
* 5.30.47 *śakrapuram*: "Shakra's city", Indraprastha.
