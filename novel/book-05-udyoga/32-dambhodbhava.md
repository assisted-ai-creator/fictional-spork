# Dambhodbhava

When great Keshava had spoken, all who sat in the hall were stunned, and their
hair stood on end. "What man would dare to answer that?" all the kings thought
in their minds. While all the kings sat silent, Jamadagnya spoke in the assembly
of the Kurus.

"Hear this one true parable, king, without doubting it. When you have heard it,
take what is good, if you think it right.

"Long ago there was a king named Dambhodbhava, lord of the whole earth. He
enjoyed the whole earth, so we have heard. Every day at dawn the mighty great
chariot fighter rose and sat asking brahmins and kshatriyas, 'Is there anyone,
shudra, vaishya, kshatriya or brahmin, who bears arms and is better than me or
my equal in battle?' Saying this, the king went about this earth, drunk with
great pride, thinking of no one else.

"Learned brahmins, not mean, fearless on every side, forbade the king again and
again as he boasted. Though he was forbidden often, he kept asking the
twice-born. He was proud and drunk with fortune. Then the brahmins, ascetics,
great-souled, keeping the vows of the Veda, angry and blazing, said to the
boasting king, 'There are two lions among men whose friendship spans many
births. You will never be equal to them, king.'

"When they said this, the king asked those twice-born again, 'Where are those
two heroes? Where were they born? What do they do? Who are they?'

"'Nara and Narayana, two ascetics, so we have heard,' said the brahmins. 'They
have come to the world of men. Fight them, king. We hear that those two great
ones, Nara and Narayana, are practising terrible austerity, beyond describing,
on Gandhamadana.'

"The king, unable to bear it, yoked a great army of six arms and went to where
those two unbeaten ones were. He went to the rough and terrible mountain
Gandhamadana and, searching, came upon those two unbeaten ascetics. He saw the
two best of men, wasted by hunger and thirst, their veins standing out, worn
thin by cold, wind and sun. He went up to them, clasped their feet and asked
after their health. They honoured him with roots and fruit, a seat and water,
and asked the king, 'What is to be done?'

"'I have conquered the earth with my arms,' said Dambhodbhava, 'and killed all
my enemies. Wanting a fight with you, I have come to this mountain. Give me that
as your hospitality. I have long wanted it.'

"'This hermitage is free of anger and greed, best of kings,' said Nara and
Narayana. 'There is no fighting in this hermitage. How could there be weapons,
or crookedness? Look for a fight somewhere else. There are many kshatriyas on
the earth.'

"Even when they said this, he spoke again. Though they bore with him again and
again and tried to soothe him, Bharata, Dambhodbhava, wanting to fight, kept
challenging the two ascetics. Then Nara took a handful of reeds, Kaurava, and
said, 'Come and fight, kshatriya, since you want a fight. Take up all your
weapons and yoke your army. I will cure you of your taste for war from now on.'

"'If you think this weapon fit for us, ascetic,' said Dambhodbhava, 'I will
fight you even with that. I have come wanting a fight.'

"When he had said this, Dambhodbhava, with his soldiers, wanting to kill the
ascetic, covered him on every side with a rain of arrows. The sage made nothing
of the terrible arrows that cut into enemy bodies, and drove them off with his
reeds. Then the unbeaten one let loose on him the terrible weapon of the reeds,
which cannot be countered, and it was like a wonder. The sage, who never missed
his mark, by magic filled their eyes, ears and noses with reeds. When the king
saw the sky white with reeds, he fell at his feet and said, 'Let me be safe.'

"Nara, the refuge of those who seek refuge, said to him, king, 'Honour brahmins
and keep to dharma. Never do this again. Never, filled with pride, insult
anyone, lesser or greater. That is the best thing for you, king. With your
wisdom trained, free of greed and of self-love, master of yourself,
self-controlled, patient, gentle and kind, protect your people, king. You have
our leave. Go in peace, and never act like this again. Ask the brahmins after
their health often, from us.'

"Then the king bowed at the feet of the two great ones and went back to his
city, and he practised dharma greatly. Great too was that deed which Nara did
long ago. And Narayana is still better, with very many virtues.

"So, king, while the best weapon has not yet been set to the Gandiva, the best
of bows, give up your pride and go to Dhananjaya. The Kakudika, the Shuka, the
Naka, the Akshisamtarjana, the Samtana, the Nartana, the terrible Ghora and the
Asyamodaka as the eighth: all men struck by these go to their death. They wander
about mad, senseless and out of their minds. Men sleep and leap about and vomit.
They pass water without stopping, and weep and laugh. The Partha's virtues are
beyond counting, and Janardana is greater still. You yourself know Dhananjaya,
son of Kunti, better than anyone.

"Know, great king, that those two heroes, the bulls among men, Nara and
Narayana, are Arjuna and Keshava. If you know this to be so, and do not doubt
me, take a noble mind and make peace with the Pandavas, Bharata. Or if you think
it best that there should be no split among you, make peace, best of the
Bharatas, and do not set your mind on war. Your family is greatly honoured on
earth, best of the Kurus. Let it stay so, bless you. Think of your own good."
