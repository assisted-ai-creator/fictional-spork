# Madhavi

"'The two twice-born ones,'" Narada went on, as Vaishampayana told it, "'the
brahmin and the bird, alighted on the peak of Rishabha. There they saw the
brahmin woman Shandili, rich in austerity. Suparna greeted her and Galava
honoured her. She welcomed them, and they sat down on a mat of grass. She
quickly cooked food for them, blessed with offerings and prayers. When they had
eaten, they were satisfied, and they lay down on the ground and slept, heavy
with the food.

"'After a short while Suparna woke, wanting to go on, and the bird saw that his
body had lost its feathers. The bird had become like a lump of flesh, with only
a face and feet. When Galava saw him like that, he was dismayed and asked him,
"What is this that has come to you from coming here? How long shall we have to
stay here now? What evil thought against dharma did you have in your mind? For
this can be no small wrong of yours."

"'"I did have a thought, twice-born one," Suparna told the brahmin. "I thought
of carrying this perfected woman from here to where Prajapati is, where the
great god Mahadeva is, and eternal Vishnu, where Dharma and the sacrifice are. I
thought she should live there. Now I bow down and beg the blessed woman, wanting
to please her. I had that thought in my mind, it seems, in my sorrow. Out of my
great respect for you, I did something here that you did not want. Whether it
was well done or ill done, in your greatness you should forgive it."

"'She was pleased, and she said to the king of birds and the bull among
brahmins, "Do not be afraid. You are Suparna. Suparna, put aside your alarm. You
have blamed me, child, and I do not forgive blame. The wrongdoer who blames me
will fall from the worlds. I am blameless and free of every bad mark, and I won
this highest perfection by keeping to right conduct. By right conduct a person
wins dharma. By right conduct a person wins wealth. By right conduct a person
gains fortune, and right conduct kills bad luck. So go from here as you please,
long-lived lord of birds. And you must never blame women, even when they deserve
it. You will be as you were before, full of strength and courage." Then his
wings became stronger than ever.

"'With Shandili's leave he went back the way he had come. But he found no horses
of that kind. Then Vishvamitra saw Galava on the road, and the best of speakers
said to him, in front of Vainateya, "Brahmin, the wealth you yourself promised
me: it is time to pay it, or whatever you think. I shall wait this long, and
longer. Take note of the way you will manage it, brahmin."

"'Then Suparna said to Galava, who was downcast and deeply grieved, "Now I have
seen for myself what Vishvamitra said. So come, best of the twice-born, and let
us take counsel, Galava. You cannot rest until you have given your teacher the
whole of what you owe."

"'Then Suparna, the best of birds, said to the despondent Galava, "It is made in
the earth by fire and increased by the wind. Because all of it is golden, it is
called gold. It holds and it supports, and so it is called wealth. So wealth
stays in the three worlds for ever. Always, under the two Proshthapadas, with
Shukra as lord of wealth, Shukra takes from men the wealth they have gathered.
It is guarded by Ajaikapad and Ahirbudhnya and by the giver of wealth. So what
is not to be had cannot be had, bull among brahmins. Without wealth you cannot
get the horses. So beg the wealth here from some king born in a line of royal
seers, who can meet our need without oppressing his citizens.

"'"There is a king, a friend of mine, born in the line of Soma. Let us go to
him, for he has riches on earth. He is the royal seer named Yayati, son of
Nahusha, whose courage is true. He will give, if I speak and you ask him
yourself. His riches were once very great, like those of the lord of wealth. But
the wise king has spent his wealth in giving."

"'Talking in this way, and thinking what would be best, the two of them came to
King Yayati at Pratishthana. He received them with honour, with the guest water
and fine food, and when he asked why they had come, the son of Vinata said, "Son
of Nahusha, this is my friend Galava, a treasure of austerity. He was
Vishvamitra's pupil for tens of thousands of years, king. When Vishvamitra gave
him leave, this brahmin wanted to do him a service, and said to the blessed one,
'What fee shall I give you as my teacher?' When he asked again and again,
Vishvamitra grew somewhat angry. He knew how little Galava had, and he told him,
'Give it. Give me eight hundred horses, white and of pure breed, bright as the
moon, each with one black ear. Let this be given as your teacher's fee, Galava,
if you think it right.' So said Vishvamitra, rich in austerity, in anger.

"'"This bull among brahmins, burning with great grief and unable to pay it, has
come to you for refuge. Tiger among men, when he has taken alms from you and his
care is gone, he will pay his teacher and practise great austerity. He will give
you a share of his austerity. With his own austerity he will fill you further,
though you are full of a royal seer's austerity. As many hairs as there are on a
horse, lord of men, so many worlds do those who give horses win, lord of the
earth. He is a worthy one to receive, and you are a worthy one to give. Let it
be like milk poured into a conch."

"'When Suparna had said this, true and excellent words, the king thought it over
carefully, and decided again and again. Yayati, lord of Vatsa and Kashi, the
performer of thousands of sacrifices, the generous giver and lord of gifts, said
this. He saw before him his dear friend Tarkshya, and Galava, bull among
brahmins, and the sign of austerity, and the alms that had been so praised. He
saw that they had passed over other kings born of the line of the sun and come
to him, having thought it over.

"'"Today my birth has borne fruit," he said. "Today my family has been saved.
Today you have saved this land of mine, blameless Tarkshya. But I must tell you,
my friend, that I am not as rich as you knew me before. My wealth has run out,
friend. And I cannot let your coming be in vain, bird, nor can I bear to
disappoint this brahmin seer's hope. So I shall give what will do the work. For
a man who has come in hope and goes back with his hope killed burns up the
family. Nothing is called more wicked than killing hope in the world, son of
Vinata, or than saying, 'There is none.' A man of standing whose hope is killed
and whose need is not met is himself killed, and he kills the sons and grandsons
of the man who did nothing for those who asked.

"'"So take my daughter, Galava. She will found four lines. She is like a
daughter of the gods, and she grows in every dharma. Gods, men and asuras always
long for the girl for her beauty. Kings will surely pay her bride-price, even
with a kingdom, let alone eight hundred black-eared horses. Take my daughter
Madhavi, my lord. Let me have grandsons by her. That is the boon I ask."

"'Galava took the girl, and the bird with him. "We shall see you again," they
said, and he set out with the girl. "Here is a way to the horses," said the
egg-born one. He took leave of Galava and went home.

"'When the king of the birds had gone, Galava went with the girl, thinking which
of the kings could pay the bride-price. In his mind he went to the Ikshvaku
Haryashva, the best of kings, at Ayodhya. He was very brave, with an army of
four divisions, with treasure, grain and troops. His citizens loved him and he
loved the brahmins. He wanted offspring, and he was calm and practising the
highest austerity. Galava the brahmin went to Haryashva and said, "King of
kings, this girl of mine will make a family grow by her children. Take her as
your wife for a bride-price, Haryashva. I shall tell you the price. Hear it and
decide."

"'King Haryashva thought it over for a long time. He sighed long and hot, the
best of kings, for he wanted offspring. "She is high in the six places that
should be high," he said, "fine in the seven that should be fine, deep in the
three that should be deep, and red in the five. Many gods and asuras would look
at her, and many gandharvas. She has many good marks and could bear many
children. She could bear a son who would rule the world. Tell me the price, best
of brahmins, bearing in mind what I have."

"'"Give me eight hundred horses," said Galava, "white as the moon, each with one
black ear, of good country breed and fine build. Then this lovely long-eyed girl
will be the mother of your sons, as the fire sticks are the source of fires."

"'When King Haryashva heard this, he was overcome with desire. The royal seer
said sadly to Galava, best of seers, "I have two hundred horses at hand of the
kind you want. Hundreds of my other horses graze about, but they are not what
you are after. So let me father only one child on her, Galava. Grant me this
wish."

"'When she heard this, the girl said to Galava, "A certain speaker of Brahman
gave me a boon. At the end of each birth I shall be a virgin again. So give me
to the king and take the fine horses. From four kings you will get the full
eight hundred, and I shall have four sons. Let my bride-price be gathered for
your teacher's sake, best of the twice-born. This is what I think, or do as you
think best, brahmin."

"'When the girl said this, the sage Galava said to King Haryashva, "Take this
girl, best of men, Haryashva. For a quarter of the price, father one son."

"'He took the girl and welcomed Galava. In due time and place he got the son he
wanted. He was named Vasumanas, and he was richer than the Vasus, like a Vasu
himself, a king who gave wealth.

"'In time wise Galava came back to Haryashva, whose heart was glad, and said to
him, "King, your son is born, like the young sun. It is time, best of men, for
me to go to another king for alms." Haryashva kept his word and kept to his
manhood. Since horses like these were hard to find, he gave Madhavi back. And
Madhavi left the king's shining fortune once more, became a virgin again at
will, and followed Galava. "Let the horses stay with you for now," said the
brahmin, and he set out with the girl for Divodasa, lord of the people.

"'"There is a very brave king, the lord of the Kashis, called Divodasa, the son
of Bhimasena, a ruler of men," said Galava. "Let us go to him, good lady. Come
slowly and do not grieve. He is a righteous and self-controlled king, and a
truthful one."

"'The sage went to him and was honoured as was right. Galava urged the king to
have a child.

"'"I have heard of this before," said Divodasa. "Why tell it at length, brahmin?
When I heard of it, I wanted it, best of the twice-born. And I value this
highly, that you passed over other kings and came to me. It will surely be. I
too have the same number of horses, Galava. I too shall father only one king on
her."

"'"So be it," said the best of the twice-born, and gave the girl to the king.
The king took her in the proper way. The royal seer delighted in her, as the sun
in Prabhavati, as fire in Svaha, as Vasava in Shachi, as the moon in Rohini, as
Yama in Dhumorna, as Varuna in Gauri, as the lord of wealth in Riddhi, as
Narayana in Lakshmi, as the ocean in Jahnavi, as Rudra in Rudrani, as the
Grandfather in Vedi. He delighted in her as Vasishtha's son in Adrishyanti, as
Vasishtha in Akshamala, as Chyavana in Sukanya, as Pulastya in Sandhya, as
Agastya in the princess of Vidarbha, as Satyavan in Savitri, as Bhrigu in
Puloma, as Kashyapa in Aditi, as Richika in Renuka, as Kaushika in Haimavati, as
Brihaspati in Tara, as Shukra in Shataparva, as the lord of the earth in the
earth, as Pururavas in Urvashi, as Richika in Satyavati, and as Manu in
Sarasvati. As King Divodasa delighted in her, Madhavi bore him one son,
Pratardana.

"'Then the blessed Galava came to Divodasa when the time came, and said, "Give
me back the girl. Let the horses stay with you while I go elsewhere for the
price, lord of the earth." Righteous King Divodasa, true to his word, gave the
girl back at the agreed time.

"'So famous Madhavi, true to her word, left her fortune once more, became a
virgin again, and followed the brahmin Galava. Galava, his mind on his own
business and thinking it over, went to Bhoja city to see King Ushinara. He went
to the king, whose courage was true, and said to him, "This girl will bear you
two sons, kings. Through her you will have your wish, here and hereafter, when
you have fathered two sons like the moon and the sun, king. But the price, you
who know all dharma, is four hundred horses, bright as the moon, each with one
black ear. This undertaking is for my teacher's sake. I have no use for horses.
If you can, great king, do it without a second thought. You have no children,
royal seer. Father two sons, king. Save your ancestors and yourself with the
boat of sons. A royal seer who enjoys the fruit of sons does not fall from
heaven. He does not go to the terrible hell where the childless go."

"'When King Ushinara heard these and many other words of Galava's, he answered
him. "I have heard what you say, Galava. But fate is strong, brahmin, and my
heart is inclined to it. I have two hundred horses of this kind, best of the
twice-born. Many thousands of others graze about. I too shall father only one
son on her, Galava. I shall take the road the others have taken, brahmin. I
would make it up to you in value, best of the twice-born. But my wealth is for
my townspeople and country folk. It is not for my own enjoyment. A king who
gives away others' wealth at his own pleasure wins neither dharma nor fame,
righteous one. So I shall accept her. Give her to me, the girl who is like a
child of the gods, so that I may have one son."

"'The best of the twice-born, Galava, honoured King Ushinara for saying this,
which was so good. When Ushinara had accepted her, Galava went to the forest.
The king delighted in her as a man of good deeds delights in fortune. He
delighted in her in mountain caves and at waterfalls, in gardens of every kind,
in woods and groves, in lovely mansions, on the roofs of palaces, in rooms with
windows like flying chariots, and in inner chambers. In time a son was born to
him, bright as the young sun. He was known by the name of Shibi, that best of
kings.

"'Galava, the brahmin, went to him and took the girl back. As he went, king, he
saw the son of Vinata.

"'The son of Vinata laughed and said to Galava, "It is good to see you here with
your purpose done, brahmin." When Galava heard the son of Vinata, he told him
that a quarter of the task was left.

"'Suparna, best of birds, said to Galava, "You need not try. This will not
succeed for you. Long ago at Kanyakubja Richika asked Gadhi for his daughter
Satyavati as his wife. Gadhi told him, 'My lord, give me a thousand horses,
bright as the moon, each with one black ear,' Galava. Richika said, 'So be it,'
and went to Varuna's home. At Ashvatirtha he got the horses and gave them to the
king. The king sacrificed with the Pundarika rite and gave them to the
twice-born. The kings bought two hundred each from them. The other four hundred,
best of the twice-born, were carried off by the Vitasta as they were being taken
across. So what cannot be had can never be had, Galava. Give this girl to
Vishvamitra in place of two hundred horses, righteous one, together with the six
hundred horses. Then your confusion will be gone, bull among brahmins, and your
task done."

"'Galava said, "So be it," and with Suparna he took the horses and the girl and
went to Vishvamitra. "Here are six hundred of the horses you wanted," said
Galava. "Take this girl in place of two hundred. Three righteous sons have been
born of her to royal seers. Father a fourth yourself, best of men. So let your
eight hundred horses be complete. Then I shall be free of my debt to you, and I
can practise austerity as I please."

"'Vishvamitra looked at Galava with the bird, and at the lovely girl, and said,
"Why was she not given to me in the first place, Galava? The four sons would
have been mine, to carry on my family. I accept your girl, for the fruit of one
son. Let the horses all go to my ashram and stay there."

"'The splendid Vishvamitra delighted in her, and fathered a son of his own,
Madhavi's son Ashtaka. As soon as the son was born, splendid Vishvamitra gave
him wealth and dharma and those horses. Then Ashtaka went to his city, which
shone like the city of Soma. Kaushika gave the girl back to his pupil and went
to the forest.

"'Galava, his teacher's fee paid with Suparna's help, said to the girl with a
glad heart, "You have borne one son who is a lord of giving, another who is a
hero, another devoted to truth and dharma, and another who sacrifices. So come,
lovely one. Your father has been saved by your sons, slender-waisted one, and so
have the four kings, and so have I."

"'Galava took leave of Suparna, the eater of snakes. He gave the girl back to
her father and went to the forest.'"

**Divergences.**

* **Garuda's thought.** A rejected passage (\*453) says Garuda, seeing Shandili's beauty, wanted to seize her. In the CE his thought was to carry her to the world of Prajapati, Shiva and Vishnu, so that she might live there (5.111.8–9). Not narrated.
* **Madhavi's boon.** It is Madhavi herself who tells Galava of her boon, from "a certain speaker of Brahman", that she will be a virgin again after each birth, and who proposes the plan of four kings (5.114.10–13). The rejected line \*455 adds that she spoke of every dharma. See ledger L-179.
* **The simile list.** Rejected lines (\*458–\*461) add many more couples to the list at 5.115.8–14, among them Himavat and Mena, Dushyanta and Shakuntala, Nala and Damayanti, Rama and Sita, and Krishna and Rukmini. Not narrated.
* **Haryashva's marks of beauty.** A rejected line (\*457) and an appendix passage (App. I No. 6) list which parts of the body are meant by the six high, seven fine, three deep and five red places. The CE does not list them (5.114.2), and nor does the novel.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*454 (Shandili's words to Garuda), \*456, \*462.

**Choices.**

* Narada's tale is told inside Vaishampayana's frame. Narada's speech is in single quotes, and Garuda's, Galava's and the kings' speech is in double quotes inside it. Speech quoted inside those, such as Vishvamitra's demand as Garuda tells it, is in single quotes again.
* 5.111.1 *dvijapakṣiṇau*: "the two twice-born ones, the brahmin and the bird". A bird is "twice-born", being hatched from an egg.
* 5.111.10 *manasā śocatā kila*: "I had that thought in my mind, it seems, in my sorrow."
* 5.111.14 *hīnayālakṣaṇaiḥ*: "free of every bad mark".
* 5.111.16: Shandili's words, "you must never blame women, even when they deserve it", are hers.
* 5.111.20–21: Vishvamitra's words are compressed in the text, and are given closely.
* 5.112.1–2: the plays on *hiraṇya*, "gold", and *dhana*, "wealth", from *dhā*, "to hold", are the text's.
* 5.112.3 is obscure. It is given as closely as it allows. Ganguli makes Agni the giver; the CE names Shukra.
* 5.112.4: Ajaikapad and Ahirbudhnya are deities of the two Proshthapada stars.
* 5.112.8 *evaṃ sa tu dhanaṃ vidvān dānenaiva vyaśodhayat*: "But the wise king has spent his wealth in giving", which is why Yayati says his wealth has run out (5.113.6). Ganguli reads it as advice to Galava.
* 5.112.20 *śaṅkhe kṣīram ivāsaktam*: "like milk poured into a conch", a perfect match.
* 5.113.2 *vatsakāśīśaḥ*: "lord of Vatsa and Kashi".
* 5.113.9 *dehi nāstīti vā vacaḥ*: "or than saying, 'There is none.'"
* 5.113.11 *caturṇāṃ vaṃśānāṃ sthāpayitrī*: "She will found four lines".
* 5.114.2: the six high, seven fine, three deep and five red places are left unexplained, as in the text.
* 5.114.6 *araṇīva hutāśānām*: "as the fire sticks are the source of fires".
* 5.114.17: the play on Vasumanas and *vasu*, "wealth", is the text's.
* 5.114.21 *kumārī kāmato bhūtvā*: "became a virgin again at will".
* 5.115.1 *bhaimaseniḥ*: "the son of Bhimasena", a king of Kashi, not the Pandava.
* 5.115.8–14: the list of divine and human couples is given in full, as in the CE. *vāsiṣṭhaḥ* is Vasishtha's son, Shakti; *vaidarbhī* is Lopamudra; *haimavatī* is Himavat's daughter.
* 5.116.2 *bhojanagaram*: "Bhoja city".
* 5.116.10 *vidhis tu balavān brahman pravaṇaṃ hi mano mama*: "But fate is strong, brahmin, and my heart is inclined to it."
* 5.116.12 *gataṃ mārgaṃ gamiṣyāmi parair aham*: "I shall take the road the others have taken."
* 5.117.4–8: Garuda's account of Richika's horses is his own.
