summary: Dhritarashtra asks the ancient youth Sanatsujata whether there is death; Sanatsujata answers that carelessness is death and carefulness immortality, that death is not a tiger but the delusion, anger and greed within a man, and that the man who kills his desires as they rise is death to them; he teaches that dharma drives out its opposite, that a brahmin should live unknown and unhonoured, that the Vedas cannot save a deceiver, and names the faults and virtues by which austerity becomes perfect; a brahmin is one who stands in truth, and a muni is made by silence, not by the forest; he tells of chastity under a teacher, whose birth is the true one, and of the imperishable that has no colour and no place; and he sings of the bright one whom the yogis see, the eternal lord, and speaks at the end as the self of all.

# Sanatsujata

Then wise King Dhritarashtra, honouring the words Vidura had spoken, questioned
the great Sanatsujata in private, wanting to reach the highest understanding.

"Sanatsujata," said Dhritarashtra, "I hear that you teach that there is no
death. And yet the gods and the asuras lived lives of chastity to escape death.
Which of these is true?"

"Some say freedom from death comes by action," said Sanatsujata, "and others
that there is no death. Listen to me, king, as I speak, and do not doubt it.
Both are true, kshatriya, and both have been held from the first. The poets
agree that delusion is death. I say that carelessness is death, and that
constant care is immortality. Through carelessness the asuras were overthrown.
Through care men become Brahman.

"Death does not eat creatures like a tiger. Its form cannot be seen. Some say
Yama is death, something other than this. But chastity that rests in the self is
the immortal. That god rules a kingdom in the world of the Ancestors, kind to
the kind and unkind to the unkind. From the mouths of men comes death, as anger,
carelessness and delusion. Deluded, living in its power, men die here and fall
there again. Then their senses follow them down, and so death gets the name of
dying. When their deeds ripen, clinging to the fruit of their deeds, they follow
it, and they do not cross over death.

"The man who, reflecting, strikes down the desires as they spring up, paying
them no heed and not waking to them, becomes death to them, and eats them as
death eats. The wise man who knows this strikes down his desires. The man who
follows his desires perishes with his desires. The man who throws off his
desires shakes off whatever dust is on him. For creatures this darkness without
light is seen as hell. They run as if seized, rushing headlong into a pit.

"Brooding desire kills a man first. Then desire and anger seize him. These bring
fools to death. The steady cross death by their steadiness. When a man thinks of
nothing else, kshatriya, death cannot take hold of him, any more than a tiger
made of straw. The inner self made of delusion, out of anger and greed: that is
the death in your body. Knowing that death is born like this, standing firm in
knowledge, a man does not fear death here. Death perishes when it comes into his
domain, as a mortal perishes when he comes into death's."

"Some here do not practise the dharmas," said Dhritarashtra, "and some do. Is
dharma struck down by evil, or does dharma strike down evil?"

"The fruit of both is tasted there," said Sanatsujata, "of dharma and of the
other. But the wise man drives out what is against dharma by dharma here. Know
that dharma is the stronger."

"Tell me of the eternal worlds they speak of," said Dhritarashtra, "the worlds
of their own dharma, for twice-born men who do good, and of the stages to them,
and of others beyond. I do not ask, learned one, about deeds."

"Those who do not compete in strength," said Sanatsujata, "as the strong compete
in strength, those brahmins shine in the world of heaven when they go from here.
Where a brahmin thinks food and drink are plentiful, like grass and reeds in the
rains, he should live there and not fret. Where evil and fear come to a man who
does not speak of himself, the one who acts as if he were nothing more than
others is the better man, not the other. His food is approved by the good who
does not fret over the one who keeps silent about himself, and who does not eat
what belongs to Brahman. As a dog eats its own vomit, always to its harm, so
they eat their vomit who live by showing off their own powers.

"A brahmin should think, 'My conduct must always be unknown.' Living among his
kin, he should let nothing be known. What brahmin would kill his inner self like
that? It is by this, kshatriya, that a man sees Brahman dwelling in him. He
should be unwearied, take no gifts, be respected, and cause no trouble. Learned,
he should not seem learned. Such a brahmin knows Brahman and is a seer.
Twice-born men who are poor in human wealth and rich in the Vedas cannot be
assailed or shaken. Know them to be the body of Brahman. The man who knows all
the gods who reward the sacrifice is still not equal to the brahmin, for he must
strive.

"The man who is honoured without striving is truly honoured. When he is honoured
he should not think of it, and when he is not he should not fret. When he is
honoured, he should think, 'The learned honour people here.' When he is not, he
should think, 'Fools who do not know dharma, clever in the ways of the world,
will not honour what should be honoured.' Honour and silence never go together.
This world belongs to honour, they know, and the other to silence.

"Fortune is where happiness lives here, but it also stands in the way. The
fortune of Brahman is very hard for a man without wisdom to win, kshatriya. The
good say its gates are many and hard to keep: truth, honesty, modesty,
self-control, purity and learning, six that hold back pride and delusion."

---

"If a twice-born man studies the Rig, the Yajus and the Sama," said
Dhritarashtra, "and does evil, is he stained by the evil or not?"

"Neither the Samans nor the Rig nor the Yajus save him from an evil deed, wise
one," said Sanatsujata. "I do not lie to you. The Vedas do not carry a deceiver
across from his sin, one who lives by deceit. At the end the Vedas leave him, as
fledged birds leave the nest."

"If the Vedas cannot save the man who knows the Veda, wise one," said
Dhritarashtra, "then why have the brahmins always talked so?"

"Austerity practised in this world bears fruit in another," said Sanatsujata.
"These worlds belong to brahmins who are disciplined in a rich austerity."

"How does austerity become perfect and complete, Sanatsujata?" said
Dhritarashtra. "Tell us, so that we may know."

"Anger and the rest are twelve faults," said Sanatsujata, "and there are six of
cruelty and the others, king. And dharma and the rest are twelve, spread out in
the scriptures, known as the virtues of the twice-born. Anger, desire, greed,
delusion, restless curiosity, pitilessness, envy, pride, grief, longing,
jealousy and disgust are the faults of men. A man must always avoid these
twelve. Each of them waits on men, king of kings, looking for an opening, as a
hunter waits on deer.

"The boaster, the covetous, the proud, the man who holds on to anger, the fickle
and the man who does not protect: when these six prosper, they practise evil
ways. The good do not, even in great hardship. The man who knows only pleasure,
the man who prospers by hatred, the man who regrets his gifts, the miser, the
weak, the man who praises his own kind, and the man who hates his women: these
are seven more of cruel ways.

"Dharma, truth, self-control and austerity, freedom from jealousy, modesty,
patience and freedom from envy, sacrifice, giving, firmness and learning: these
are the twelve great vows of a brahmin. The man who does not stray from these
twelve could rule this whole earth. The man who excels in three, or two, or even
one of them should be known as one who calls nothing his own.

"Self-control, renunciation and care: in these immortality is placed. The wise
brahmins say they have truth at their head. Self-control has eighteen faults:
going against what should and should not be done, falsehood, envy, desire and
wealth, longing, anger, grief, thirst, greed, slander, jealousy, restless
curiosity, remorse, pleasure, forgetfulness, excessive talk, and
self-importance. The man who is free of these faults the good call
self-controlled.

"The best renunciation is of six kinds. When he gets what is dear, he does not
rejoice. When what is unwelcome comes, he is never pained. Of his dear wife and
sons, his word is always, 'It must be given,' to the worthy man who asks, and no
other word. He speaks even what should not be spoken: that is known as the third
virtue. He lives with his possessions given up, and does not use them for
pleasure. And he does not fall short in his works because of it, like a man with
the mind of a pupil. Such a man, even if he is rich, has every virtue.

"Care has eight faults. A man should avoid them. Free from the five senses and
the mind, from the past and the future, he is happy. The austerity that is free
of these faults and joined to these virtues becomes perfect and complete. That
is what you asked me, king of kings. What more do you want to hear?"

---

"Most people are spoken of as knowing the Vedas with the stories as a fifth,"
said Dhritarashtra. "Others know four Vedas, others three, others two, others
one, and others none. Which of them should I know as a brahmin?"

"Because the one Veda was not known, king of kings, the Vedas became many," said
Sanatsujata. "Of the one truth, few stand in the truth. So, without neglecting
the Veda, they set their wisdom on what is great. Giving, study and sacrifice go
forward out of greed. For those who fall from truth, their resolve comes to
nothing. Then the sacrifice is spread out by holding to truth alone. For one it
is done by the mind, for another by speech, for another by deeds. The man whose
resolve succeeds stands above his resolves. Because he is not steady, he should
keep the vow of consecration. The name comes from the root of the word. Truth is
the highest thing of the good. Knowledge is seen before the eyes. Austerity
bears its fruit unseen.

"Know that a brahmin who reads a great deal is a great reader. So, kshatriya, do
not think a man a brahmin by his talk alone. Know that the brahmin is the one
who never leaves the truth.

"The metres, kshatriya, were sung by Atharvan in the beginning, in the creation
of the seers. Those who know the metres, who have studied them, do not know what
is to be known through the Veda, or what is to be known at all. No one is the
knower of the Vedas, or else only someone, king, understands them. The man who
knows the Vedas does not know what is to be known. But the man who stands in
truth knows what is to be known.

"I know him as a brahmin who is discerning and can explain, who has cut away his
own doubts and explains all doubts. One should not look for Him in the east, nor
in the south, nor in the west, nor across, nor in any quarter at all. One should
sit in silence and worship, and not stir even in the mind. Then Brahman, resting
in his inner self, turns towards him. A man becomes a muni by silence, not by
living in the forest. The man who knows the imperishable is called the best of
munis. From expounding all things a man is called a grammarian. The man who sees
the worlds before his eyes becomes all-seeing. Standing in truth, kshatriya, a
brahmin sees Brahman. I tell you this, learned one, following the Vedas in their
order."

---

"Sanatsujata," said Dhritarashtra, "this supreme speech of Brahman that you
speak, of every form, is the rarest of all things to be wished for. Tell me
this, youth."

"This Brahman you ask me about, and rejoice at so much," said Sanatsujata, "is
not won by one in haste. I will tell you the ancient knowledge of the
unmanifest, which is perfected by understanding and by chastity."

"You speak of the eternal knowledge of the unmanifest," said Dhritarashtra,
"perfected by chastity. It cannot be won by works, and yet it dwells here in
time, noble one. How does a man win the state of Brahman, which is immortality?"

"Those in this world who conquer their desires," said Sanatsujata, "and bear the
state of Brahman, resting in goodness, draw the self out of their bodies, as a
reed is drawn from the munja grass.

"Father and mother make this body, Bharata. But the birth that the teacher gives
is true, and free of old age and death. Those who enter the teacher's womb here,
and become an embryo, and live in chastity, become makers of the teaching here,
and when they leave the body they go to the highest yoga.

"The one who covers the ears with truth, doing it well, and gives the immortal:
think of him as father and mother, and knowing what he has done, never betray
him.

"The pupil should always respect his teacher, and seek to study, pure and
careful. He should not be proud or hold on to anger. This is the first foot of
chastity. He should do what pleases the teacher, with his life and with his
wealth, in deed, in mind and in word. This is called the second foot. As he
behaves to the teacher, so he should behave to the teacher's wife, doing as he
is told and doing what pleases. This is called the third foot. When he has done
some service for the teacher, a wise man should not speak of it, or think, 'I
did this,' or make others say it. That is the fourth foot of chastity.

"Living like this, whatever wealth comes to him he should give to the teacher.
So he comes to the conduct of the good, rich in virtue, and the same conduct
holds towards the teacher's son. Living like this, he grows in every way here,
and has many sons and a firm place. The quarters and the regions rain their
gifts on him, and people live under the shelter of his chastity.

"By this chastity the gods won godhood, and the fortunate, wise seers the world
of Brahma. By this the gandharvas and apsarases won their beauty. By this
chastity the sun rises to make the day. The learned man who burns his whole body
with austerity, as if to consume it and split it, passes beyond childishness by
it, and holds death back at the time of the end. People win worlds that come to
an end, kshatriya, by deeds they have made. By this the learned man goes to
Brahman, which is everything. There is no other road to go."

"It shines like white," said Dhritarashtra, "or like red, or black, or dark as
collyrium, or tawny. What form has that immortal, imperishable state that the
learned brahmin sees here?"

"It does not shine like white or like red," said Sanatsujata, "nor black, nor
the colour of iron, nor the colour of the sun. It does not stand on the earth,
nor in the sky. The water of the ocean does not hold it. It does not rest in the
stars, nor in the lightning. Its form is not seen in the clouds, nor in the
wind, nor in the gods. It is not seen in the moon, nor in the sun. It is not
seen in the Rig, nor in the Yajus, nor in the Atharva, nor in the spotless
Samans, king, nor in the Rathantara or the Brihat chants, nor surely in the
Mahavrata.

"It cannot be crossed, beyond the darkness. Even Death goes into it at the time
of destruction. Its form is finer than a razor's edge, and greater than the
mountains. That is the foundation. That is the immortal, the worlds, Brahman,
glory. Creatures were born from it, and go back into it. It is free of sickness,
great, lifted high, glory itself. The poets say speech is a change of it. In it
this whole world rests. Those who know it become immortal."

---

"That bright, great light, blazing, great glory, which the gods worship, by
which the sun shines: the yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"From the bright one Brahman comes to be. Brahman grows by the bright one. That
bright one, in the midst of the lights, unheated, gives heat. The yogis see him,
the lord, the eternal.

"The waters, and from the waters, in the midst of the flood, the two gods rested
in the sky. He, clothed in what turns together and what turns apart, bears both
earth and heaven. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"The bright one bears both gods, earth and heaven, and the quarters and the
world. From him the quarters and the rivers flow. From him the great oceans were
made. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"He stands on the wheel of the chariot whose work does not decay. Horses draw
him, bannered, heavenly, unageing, in the sky. The yogis see him, the lord, the
eternal.

"His form stands in no likeness. No one sees him with the eye. Those who know
him so, with thought and mind and heart, become immortal. The yogis see him, the
lord, the eternal.

"There is a terrible river of twelve streams, guarded by the gods. Seeing its
honey, men move along it. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"The bee gathers that honey and drinks it for half a month. The Lord made it the
offering in all beings. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"The wingless came to the ashvattha tree with golden leaves. There they grew
wings and flew off in every direction. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"From the full they draw the full. From the full they made the full. They take
the full from the full, and the full alone remains. The yogis see him, the lord,
the eternal.

"From him the wind came, and in him it always moves. From him came fire and
Soma, and in him breath is spread out. Know that everything comes from him. We
cannot tell it. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"The breath swallows the downward breath. The moon swallows the breath. The sun
swallows the moon. The Supreme swallows the sun. The yogis see him, the lord,
the eternal.

"The swan, rising from the water, does not lift one foot. If he, the priest,
lifted it for ever, there would be neither death nor the immortal. The yogis see
him, the lord, the eternal.

"So that god, the great self, the person, swallows the fire. The man who knows
that person, his self is not harmed here. The yogis see him, the lord, the
eternal.

"If one swift as thought spread his wings and flew for a thousand thousand, he
would only come to the middle, in the middle. The yogis see him, the lord, the
eternal.

"His form does not stand in sight. Those whose being is pure see him. The kind,
wise man may see him with the mind. Those who take refuge in him become
immortal. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"Mortals hide themselves, by their own training and their own conduct, as snakes
hide in holes. The deluded are bewildered by them, as men are led astray on the
road, to their fear. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"He is ever made of being and not-being. There is no death for him, so how could
there be the immortal? Truth and untruth are bound to the same truth. The source
of being and of not-being is one. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"It is seen alike in men, with the good or with the bad. Know it as the same
thing as the immortal. Joined to it, a man should seek that honey. The yogis see
him, the lord, the eternal.

"Harsh words do not burn his heart, nor the thought, 'I have not studied, I have
not made the fire offering.' His mind takes on the lightness of Brahman. The
steady give it the name of wisdom. The yogis see him, the lord, the eternal.

"The man who sees his self like this in all beings, joined to one thing and
another, why should he grieve after that? As much use as there is in a well when
the water floods everywhere, so much use is there in all the Vedas for a brahmin
who knows.

"The person the size of a thumb, the great self, is not seen, though he dwells
in the heart. Unborn, he moves day and night, untiring. Knowing him, the seer
sits at peace.

"I am your mother and your father, and I am your son again. I am the self of
all, of what is not and of what is. I am the old grandfather, the father and the
son, Bharata. You all live in my self. You are not mine, and I am not yours. The
self is my place, and the self is my birth. I am the one the Veda speaks of,
standing in what does not age. Finer than the fine, glad in mind, I am awake in
all beings. They know him who is the father of all beings, placed in the lotus."

**Divergences.**

* **The Sanatsujatiya grew in the manuscripts.** Many passages that Ganguli translates here are rejected lines. Among them: Dhritarashtra's question on silence (*mauna*) and its answer (\*254), the long exchange on the unborn one and on action (\*246, \*247), the gradations of austerity (\*255–\*260), the list of eight virtues of care (\*266), the verses on the Veda and the knowable (\*270, \*271), and in the hymn the golden egg from which Brahma was born (\*294) and the thumb-sized person seen only by yogis (\*300, \*301). None is narrated.
* **Appendix I No. 2.** After 5.44.24 a long appendix passage repeats the twelve faults and the twelve virtues in another form and adds the six marks of friendship and the eighteen faults of pride. Ganguli's section 5.45 translates this passage. It is rejected, and not narrated.
* **The last line.** A rejected line (\*302) ends the teaching with an obscure riddle about poison and the black antelope skin. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* This is among the hardest passages of the epic. The novel renders the CE's words as plainly as it can and does not add the commentary that fills Ganguli's version (his parentheses on "Conditions", "accidents" and so on). Where a verse is obscure, it is kept close to the words and noted here.

* 5.42.9: the man who strikes down his desires "becomes death to them, and eats them as death eats".
* 5.42.13 *tārṇa ivāsya vyāghraḥ*: "a tiger made of straw".
* 5.42.17: Dhritarashtra's question is compressed. "I do not ask about deeds" follows Ganguli.

* 5.43.10–11: the text says "six" and then lists seven more, as it does.
