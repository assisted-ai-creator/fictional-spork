# Draupadi's Hair

"Yudhishthira has said all that needs to be said, Janardana," said Arjuna. "But
I have heard your words, scorcher of enemies. It seems to me you do not think
peace will be easy here, lord, because of Dhritarashtra's greed, or because of
the weakness that has come on us. You also think a man's courage is fruitless.
Yet no fruit comes from courage without action. What has been said is so, and
also not so. Nothing should be seen as impossible. Do you think our trouble is a
wrong done at the start, that they do deeds which bear no fruit? Work well done,
lord, can bear fruit. So act, Krishna, in such a way that there may be peace
with the others.

"You are the greatest friend of the Pandavas and the Kurus, hero, as Prajapati
is of the gods and the asuras. Bring health to the Kurus and the Pandavas. I do
not think it hard for you to do what is good for us. If that is to be done,
Janardana, you will do it just by going, no doubt of it. And if you mean to do
something else to that wicked man, hero, it will all happen as you mean it. Let
there be peace with them, or whatever you intend. Whatever you decide after
thinking it over, Krishna, will weigh with us.

"Does he not deserve death, that wicked man, with his sons and kin, who could
not bear to see that fortune in the son of Dharma? When he saw no means that
kept to dharma, Madhusudana, that cruel gambler took it by a cruel means. How
could a bowman born among kshatriyas turn back when he is challenged, even when
he faces death? When I saw us beaten by adharma and sent off to the forest,
Varshneya, I thought Suyodhana had already earned death. It is no wonder,
Krishna, that you want to help your friends. But how should it be done, gently
or otherwise? Or if you think their death is better at once, let it be done
quickly. Do not think about it any more. You know how that evil-minded man
tormented Draupadi in the middle of the hall, and how that was borne. I cannot
believe, Madhava, that he will behave well to the Pandavas, any more than seed
sown on salt ground will grow. So do quickly, Varshneya, what you think fitting
and good for the Pandavas, whatever we must do next."

"It is as you say, strong-armed Pandava," said the Lord. "But all this depends
on two kinds of action, Bibhatsu. A field may be rich and clean, prepared by the
farmer, but without rain, son of Kunti, it will never bear fruit. People might
call the watering that is done by effort the work of man. But there too a man
must see the drying up that fate brings about. The great ones of old settled it
with their understanding: the world runs by fate and human effort joined. I will
do all that human effort can do. But I can never do the work of fate.

"That evil-minded man has given up dharma and truth, and goes his way. He is not
troubled by deeds like these. His counsellors, Shakuni, the suta's son and his
brother Duhshasana, add to his wicked thinking. He will not come to peace by
giving up the kingdom, Partha. Suyodhana and those bound to him will not come to
peace except by death. And the Dharma King does not want to give it up by bowing
down. That evil-minded one will not give the kingdom when he is asked for it. I
do not think he should be given Yudhishthira's message. The Dharma King told the
purpose of it. That wicked Kaurava will not do any of it. And when he does not
do it, he will deserve death from the world.

"He deserves death from me too, Bharata, and from the world, for he wronged all
of you when you were boys. That cruel, wicked man robbed you of your kingdom,
and the evil one is not calmed when he sees the fortune of Yudhishthira. More
than once he tried to set me against you, Partha, but I did not take up his
wicked plan. You know his deepest mind too, strong-armed one, and that I want to
do what pleases the Dharma King. You know his self and my deepest mind, Arjuna,
and yet for no reason you doubt me, as if you did not know. And you have also
learned what is divine and highest, ordained by fate, Partha. How could there be
peace with the others? I will do whatever I can do by word and deed, Pandava.
But I do not hope for peace with the others.

"How could he have said, wanting peace, the things he said at the cattle raid,
though Bhishma begged him, when the year's road was over? They were defeated
then, when you set your mind to it. And Suyodhana was not content with a tiny
piece, or a moment. In every way I must carry out the Dharma King's command, and
show up the wicked deeds of that wicked man once more."

---

"The Dharma King, who knows dharma and is generous, has said many things,
Madhava," said Nakula, "full of dharma and true. Bhimasena too, knowing the
king's mind, has spoken of peace and of the strength of his arms, Madhava. You
have heard what Phalguna said too. And you have told your own mind many times,
hero. Set all this aside. When you have heard the other side's mind, best of
men, do what you think fit for the time. A man's mind changes with each cause,
Keshava. A man must do himself what the time requires, tamer of enemies. A thing
thought of one way turns out another. Men's minds in the world do not last, best
of men.

"We had one mind when we lived in the forest, another when we were hidden, and
another again, Krishna, now that we are seen. When we wandered in the forest,
Varshneya, our longing for the kingdom was not what it is now. Hearing that our
forest life was over, hero, these seven akshauhinis have gathered, Janardana, by
your grace. What man here would not tremble when he sees these tigers among men,
of unthinkable strength and courage, weapons in hand in battle?

"In the middle of the Kurus, speak to him gently first, then with fear in it, so
that foolish Suyodhana will not be troubled. Think of Yudhishthira, Bhimasena,
unbeaten Bibhatsu, Sahadeva and me, of you and Rama, Keshava, and Satyaki of
great courage. Think of Virata with his son, Drupada with his counsellors,
Dhrishtadyumna son of Prishata, the bold king of Kashi and Dhrishtaketu lord of
the Chedis. What mortal of flesh and blood would fight these in battle? Just by
going, strong-armed one, you will surely win what the Dharma King wants. Vidura,
Bhishma, Drona and Bahlika can understand what is best when you tell them,
faultless one. They will win over King Dhritarashtra, and Suyodhana of wicked
conduct with his counsellors. With Vidura to listen and you to speak, Janardana,
what purpose that had gone astray would they not set back on the road?"

---

"What the king has said is the eternal dharma," said Sahadeva. "But act in such
a way, tamer of enemies, that there is war. Even if the Kurus want peace with
the Pandavas, Dasharha, still bring about war with them. How can my anger at
Suyodhana be quieted without his death, after seeing Panchali so tormented in
the hall? If Bhima and Arjuna, Krishna, and the Dharma King, who keeps dharma,
give up dharma, I want to fight him in battle alone."

"The wise and strong-armed Sahadeva has spoken the truth," said Satyaki. "My
anger would be quieted by Duryodhana's death. You know how your own anger rose
when you saw the Pandavas in the forest wearing bark and deerskins, suffering.
So what the heroic son of Madri, the bull among men, has said is the mind of all
the fighters, best of men."

As wise Yuyudhana spoke these words, a terrible lion's roar went up from all the
fighters there. All the heroes everywhere honoured his words, eager for battle,
crying "Well said! Well said!" and cheering the grandson of Shini.

---

Krishna, the daughter of King Drupada, with her long, black hair, heard the
king's words, full of dharma and purpose and good. Wasted with grief, she spoke
to the Dasharha as he sat there. She honoured Sahadeva and the great chariot
fighter Satyaki. Then, seeing Bhimasena calm, the proud woman spoke in deep
distress, her eyes full of tears.

"You know, strong-armed Madhusudana, you who know dharma, how the son of
Dhritarashtra and his counsellors brought down the Pandavas from happiness by a
trick, Janardana. You know too, Dasharha, the counsel King Yudhishthira gave
Sanjaya in private. You heard all that Sanjaya was told: 'Give us five villages,
my son, Kushasthala, Vrikasthala, Asandi and Varanavata, and for the fifth any
one at all, strong-armed one.' That is what Duryodhana and his friends were to
be told, Keshava. And when he heard it, Krishna, Suyodhana did not do what
modest Yudhishthira asked, wanting peace, Dasharha.

"If Suyodhana wants peace without giving back the kingdom, Krishna, then you
must not go there to make it. The Pandavas with the Srinjayas can stand against
the terrible, angry army of Dhritarashtra's son, strong-armed one. Nothing can
be won from them by conciliation or by gifts. So do not show them pity,
Madhusudana. Enemies who are not quieted by conciliation or gifts, Krishna,
should have the rod let loose on them, by one who wants to protect his life. So
the great rod must be hurled on them quickly, Achyuta, by you, strong-armed one,
and by the Pandavas and the Srinjayas. That would be fitting for the Parthas,
and bring you glory. If it is done, Krishna, it will bring happiness to the
kshatriyas.

"A kshatriya who has taken to greed must be killed by a kshatriya, Dasharha, or
by one who is not a kshatriya, keeping to his own dharma. The only exception is
a brahmin, my son, whatever evil he has fallen into. For the brahmin is the
teacher of all the classes, and takes the first share of what is offered. Those
who know dharma know that as there is fault in killing one who should not be
killed, Janardana, so there is fault in not killing one who should be. Act,
Krishna, in such a way that this fault does not touch you, with the Pandavas,
Dasharha, and the Srinjayas and their armies.

"I will say it again, Janardana, trusting in you. What woman on earth is like
me, Keshava? I am the daughter of King Drupada, risen from the middle of the
altar, the sister of Dhrishtadyumna, your dear friend, Krishna. I came into the
family of Ajamidha, the daughter-in-law of great Pandu, the queen of the sons of
Pandu, who shine like five Indras. To me were born five sons by the five heroes,
great chariot fighters, Krishna, your kin by dharma just as Abhimanyu is. And I
was seized by the hair and dragged into the hall and abused, while the sons of
Pandu looked on, while you lived, Keshava. While the Kauravas lived, and the
Panchalas and the Vrishnis, I was made a slave of evil men, standing in the
middle of the hall. While the Pandavas watched, without anger, without moving, I
longed for you in my mind, Govinda: 'Save me!'

"There the blessed king, my father-in-law, said to me, 'Choose a boon, Panchali.
You deserve a boon, and I honour you.' I said, 'Let the Pandavas be free, with
their chariots and weapons.' And so they were let go to live in the forest,
Keshava. You know such sorrows, Janardana. Save me, lotus-eyed one, with my
husbands, my kin and my family. Am I not, Krishna, by dharma, the
daughter-in-law of both Bhishma and Dhritarashtra? And I was made a slave. Shame
on Bhimasena's strength, and shame on the Partha's archery, Krishna, if
Duryodhana lives even for a moment. If I deserve your kindness, if you feel pity
for me, Krishna, turn all your anger on the sons of Dhritarashtra."

When she had said this, the lovely woman with the dark-cornered eyes took her
hair in her left hand. It was soft to gather, curling at the ends, beautiful and
very dark, sweet with holy perfumes, with every lucky mark, gleaming like a
great snake. The lotus-eyed woman who walked like an elephant came up to the
lotus-eyed one. Krishna, her eyes full of tears, spoke to Krishna.

"This hair, lotus-eyed one, that Duhshasana's hand pulled, must be remembered at
all times by you, when you seek peace with the others. If Bhima and Arjuna are
so weak, Krishna, as to want peace, my old father will fight, with his sons, the
great chariot fighters. And my five sons, of great courage, Madhusudana, will
fight the Kurus with Abhimanyu at their head. If I do not see Duhshasana's dark
arm cut off and covered in dust, what peace will my heart have? Thirteen years
have passed for me while I waited, keeping my anger in my heart like a blazing
fire. My heart is breaking, pierced by Bhima's dart of words, now that the
strong-armed one sees only dharma."

When she had said this, the woman with the long eyes wept, her voice choked with
tears, sobbing and trembling. The woman with the full hips shed tears from her
eyes, hot as if melted, that rained on her breasts.

Strong-armed Keshava spoke to her and comforted her. "Soon you will see the
women of the Bharatas weeping, Krishna. Like you, timid one, they will weep,
their kin and family killed, their friends and armies killed, those you are
angry with, proud lady. I will do it, with Bhima, Arjuna and the twins, at
Yudhishthira's command, and by fate, as the Ordainer has made it. If the sons of
Dhritarashtra, ripe for time, will not listen to my words, they will lie dead on
the ground, food for dogs and jackals. The Himalaya mountain might move, the
earth might split into a hundred pieces, heaven with its stars might fall, but
my word will not be in vain. I promise you truly, Krishna. Hold back your tears.
Soon you will see your husbands with their enemies killed, crowned with
fortune."
