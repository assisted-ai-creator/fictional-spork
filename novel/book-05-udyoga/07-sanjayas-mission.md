summary: Dhritarashtra sends Sanjaya to the Pandavas at Upaplavya; he praises the Pandavas, admits he has never found a fault in them, and confesses his fear of Arjuna, Bhima, the twins, their allies, Krishna and above all Yudhishthira's anger; at Upaplavya Sanjaya greets Yudhishthira, who asks after every one of the Kurus, and whether they remember what Arjuna, Bhima and the twins have done; Sanjaya answers that the old king grieves, and then, before all the allies, gives Dhritarashtra's message, that the Pandavas, born to dharma, should not win a victory that would be the same as defeat, and he begs them with joined hands for peace.

# Sanjaya's Mission

"They say the sons of Pandu have come to Upaplavya, Sanjaya," said
Dhritarashtra. "Go and find out about them. Greet Ajatashatru, and say, 'How
lucky that you have come to a village, blameless one.' Say to them all, Sanjaya,
'Are you well?' They have lived through a hard stay they did not deserve. They
are good men, and they have done us good, though they were dealt with falsely.
They will soon make peace with us.

"I have never seen any false dealing in the Pandavas, Sanjaya, not ever. They
won all their fortune by their own courage, and they brought it all to me. I
have searched, and I cannot find any fault in them, anything at all for which I
could blame the Parthas. They always act for dharma and for profit. They love
comfort, but they do not give in to their desires. Heat and cold, hunger and
thirst, sleep and sloth, anger and joy and carelessness, the Parthas master them
all with firmness and wisdom, and strive for dharma and profit. They give their
wealth to their friends when the time comes. Their friendship does not wear out
from living together. The Parthas give honour and wealth as each deserves. There
is no one on the side of Ajamidha's son who hates them, except the wicked,
crooked fool Duryodhana, and Karna, who is meaner still. These two feed the fire
of those great men, who have gone without comfort and pleasure.

"Duryodhana, grown up in comfort, thinks it is a fine thing, and the boy thinks
he can take the Pandavas' share while they are alive. But when Arjuna and
Keshava, Vrikodara, Satyaki, the two sons of Madri and all the Srinjayas follow
Ajatashatru's path, it is better to give before there is war.

"The Left-handed Archer, with the Gandiva bow, standing in his chariot, could
sweep the earth alone. So could Keshava, Vishnu, whom no one can assail, the
great lord of the three worlds. What mortal could stand before him, the one who
is chosen and praised among all the gods, when he pours out his floods of arrows
with the roar of the rain cloud, swift as swarms of locusts? With the Gandiva
the Left-handed Archer, in a single chariot, conquered the northern quarter and
the Northern Kurus. He took their wealth and made them his followers and his
tributaries. At Khandava, with the Gandiva, the Left-handed Archer defeated the
gods and Indra, and gave the forest to the fire, and added to the fame and
honour of the Pandavas.

"There is no one today with a mace who is the equal of Bhima, no one on an
elephant who is his match. In a chariot, they say, he is no less than Arjuna,
and in the strength of his arms he has the power of ten thousand elephants. Well
trained, fierce, and with a feud to settle, if he is angry he will burn up the
sons of Dhritarashtra in a moment. He is always furious and strong. Not even
Vasava himself could beat him in battle. The two sons of Madri are clear-headed,
strong and quick of hand, well trained by Phalguna. Like two hawks falling on
flocks of birds, they would leave no Kurus alive.

"In their midst is fierce Dhrishtadyumna, one of the Pandavas' own, the chief of
the Somakas, with his ministers, ready to give his life for the Pandavas'
victory. Virata, lord of the Shalveyas, who lived with them and has served their
purpose, a man in his prime, with his sons, is always on the Pandavas' side and
devoted to Yudhishthira, so I hear. The five Kekaya brothers, mighty archers
driven out of the Kekaya country, want their kingdom back from the Kekayas, and
live with the Parthas, eager for war.

"All the heroes among the kings of the earth have been brought together and have
settled on the Pandavas' side. I hear they are brave, and loyal, and bound by
affection to the Dharma King. Warriors of good family from the mountains and
from strongholds, and mlecchas, foreigners with every kind of weapon, have
gathered and settled on the Pandavas' side. The Pandya king, like Indra, has
come for the Pandavas, a great man with many heroes around him, a hero of the
world, whose power no one can match. And I hear that Satyaki, who learned his
weapons from Drona, Arjuna, Vasudeva, Kripa and Bhishma, and who they say is the
only match for Krishna's son, has settled on the Pandavas' side.

"The Chedis and the Karushas have joined them too, with kings of every might.
Among them once shone the lord of the Chedis, blazing like the sun, full of
glory, who thought no one could stop him in battle, the best of all bowmen on
earth. Krishna crushed all the might of the kshatriyas and struck him down by
force. To add to the glory and honour of the Yadavas, he once cut down
Shishupala in battle, whose pride all the kings, with the king of the Karushas
at their head, had fed. When they saw that no one could stand against Keshava in
his chariot drawn by Sugriva and the other horses, the others left the lord of
the Chedis and ran, like small deer when they see a lion. And he who dared to
face Vasudeva in single combat, hoping to win, lay dead, killed by Krishna, like
a karnikara tree torn up by the wind.

"When I think of what they have told me of Keshava's courage on their behalf,
Sanjaya, when I remember the deeds of that Vishnu, I cannot find peace, son of
Gavalgana. No other enemy could ever stand against those whose leader is the
lion of the Vrishnis. My heart trembles with fear when I hear that the two
Krishnas are together in one chariot. If my foolish son, whose mind is perverse,
does not go to war with those two, then they will not burn up the Kurus,
Sanjaya, as Indra and Vishnu burned the army of the daityas. For I think
Dhananjaya is Shakra's equal, and the Vrishni hero is eternal Vishnu.

"The Pandava, Ajatashatru, the son of Kunti, delights in dharma, and shame holds
him back. He is fierce and proud, and he was cheated by Duryodhana. If he were
not what he is, his anger would burn up the sons of Dhritarashtra. I do not fear
Arjuna, or Vasudeva, or Bhima, or the twins, as much as I fear the anger of the
king when his wrath is kindled, suta. I am always more afraid of that. He is
great in austerity and chastity, and his mind's intent would come true. I know
his anger in battle, Sanjaya, and I know it is justified, and today I am very
much afraid.

"So go quickly in a chariot, as I send you. Go to the army of the Panchala king
and ask Ajatashatru how he is. Speak to him again and again with affection. And
when you meet Janardana, my son, the great-minded, the noblest of heroes, ask
after his health from me, and tell him that Dhritarashtra wants peace with the
Pandavas. The son of Kunti would not refuse any word of Vasudeva's, suta.
Krishna is dear to them, as dear as themselves, and he is wise and always
devoted to their work. Gather the Pandavas and the Srinjayas, Janardana,
Yuyudhana and Virata, and ask after their health from me, and all five sons of
Draupadi too. Whatever you think is timely for the others there, and good for
the Bharatas, say that among the kings, Sanjaya, so that it does not provoke
them and there is no war."

---

When Sanjaya heard King Dhritarashtra's words he went to Upaplavya to see the
Pandavas of boundless power. He came to King Yudhishthira, the soul of dharma,
and first bowed low before him. Then the suta's son spoke. Sanjaya, son of
Gavalgana, son of a suta, spoke to Ajatashatru with confidence.

"How lucky, king, that I see you well, with allies, like great Indra. The wise
old King Dhritarashtra, Ambika's son, asks after your health. Is Bhima well,
first of the Pandavas? Is Dhananjaya, and are Madri's two sons? Is Krishna
Draupadi well, the king's daughter, true to her vows, the wife of heroes, with
her sons, the proud lady, in whom you find all you desire, Bharata, and wish her
well?"

"Welcome, Sanjaya, son of Gavalgana," said Yudhishthira. "I am glad to greet
you, suta. I tell you that I am well, learned one, and my younger brothers too.
It is long since I have heard news of the Bharata, the old king of the Kurus,
suta. Seeing you, Sanjaya, I feel I see the lord of men himself, I am so glad.

"Is our grandfather well, my friend, Bhishma, the old Kaurava, proud and very
wise, and full of every dharma? Is his conduct still what it was? Is King
Dhritarashtra well, the great son of Vichitravirya, and his sons? Is the great
King Bahlika well, son of Pratipa, the learned? Is Somadatta well, my friend,
and Bhurishravas, true to his promises, and Shala? Are Drona and his son, and
the brahmin Kripa, the great archers, free of sickness? They are very wise, pure
in all the sciences, the first of the bowmen on earth. Are they honoured, my
friend? Are these bowmen free of sickness? All the young bowmen on earth envy
the Kurus, in whose kingdom the handsome, well-behaved great archer, Drona's
son, lives. Is Yuyutsu well, my friend, the very wise prince, son of the vaishya
woman? Is Karna, the minister, well, whom the fool Suyodhana obeys?

"Are the old women of the Bharatas well, the mothers, the cooks, the slave
women, the daughters-in-law, the sons, the sisters' sons, the sisters, the
daughters' sons? Are they free of sorrow?

"Does the king still give the brahmins their living as before, my friend? Does
the son of Dhritarashtra not take away my gifts to the twice-born, Sanjaya? Does
King Dhritarashtra, with his sons, overlook the brahmins' overbearing ways? Does
he not neglect them in their need, as if they were only a road to his own ends?
This is the highest, brightest light the Creator has set in the world of the
living for his creatures. If those fools do not restrain their greed, there will
be the utter ruin of the Kauravas.

"Does King Dhritarashtra, with his sons, look to the living of his ministers?
Are there no enemies in the shape of friends, who, with one of their own
friends, hope to live by setting them against each other? Do none of the Kurus
speak of any wrong done by the Pandavas, my friend? When they see bands of
robbers gathered together, do they remember the Partha, the leader in battle? Do
they remember the straight-flying arrows, loud as thunder, sent from the Gandiva
by the arm and the bowstring, when that bowman shakes his bow? I have seen no
one on earth, and heard of no one, equal to Arjuna or better, whose one approved
hand-throw is sixty-one sharp, keen-edged, well-feathered arrows.

"Do they remember Bhimasena, fierce, mace in hand, making the hosts of his
enemies tremble in the battle line, as he strides among them like an elephant in
rut through a bed of reeds? Do they remember Sahadeva, the son of Madri, who
beat the Kalingas gathered at Dantakura, shooting with his left hand and with
his right, the mighty one? Do they remember Nakula, the son of Madri, sent out
before your eyes, Sanjaya, son of Gavalgana, who brought the western quarter
under my power?

"Do they remember the defeat at Dvaitavana, when they came on the cattle
expedition with their wicked plan? There Bhimasena and Jaya freed the fools who
had fallen into the enemy's hands. I guarded Arjuna from behind, and Bhimasena
the sons of Madri. The bearer of the Gandiva scattered the enemy hosts and came
back safe. Do they remember that?

"Surely nothing can be done here by a single good deed, Sanjaya, if we cannot
win over the son of Dhritarashtra with all our hearts."

---

"It is right, Pandava, best of the Kurus," said Sanjaya, "that you ask after the
Kurus and their people. The great-minded best of the Kurus you ask about are
well, my son, Partha. Know this, Pandava: the son of Dhritarashtra has good old
men with him, and he has wicked men too. The son of Dhritarashtra would give
even to an enemy. How could he cut off the brahmins' gifts?

"The way he behaves to you is not right. To wrong those who do no wrong, as if
they had, is not good. Dhritarashtra and his sons would be traitors to their
friends if they hated you, who behave well, for they would be the ones behaving
badly. He does not approve it, Ajatashatru, and he suffers bitterly. The old man
grieves inside, for he hears from the brahmins when they meet that betraying a
friend is worse than the gravest sins.

"They remember you, lord of men, when the armies meet, and Jishnu, leader in
battle. When the noise of drums and conches rises, they remember Bhimasena, mace
in hand. They remember the two sons of Madri in the thick of battle, falling on
every quarter, raining showers of arrows on the army without pause, great
chariot fighters no one can shake in battle.

"But I do not think a man can know what is to come, king, what the future will
be, if you, Pandava, who have every virtue, have met with such hard trouble. You
yourself, Ajatashatru, can set all this right again with your wisdom. The sons
of Pandu, all like Indra, would not give up dharma for the sake of desire. You
yourself, Ajatashatru, can make peace by your wisdom, so that the sons of
Dhritarashtra, the Pandavas, the Srinjayas and all the other kings who have
gathered here may find their happiness. Hear what your father Dhritarashtra said
to me at night, Ajatashatru, king, when he met with his ministers and his sons.
Hear his words."

---

"The Pandavas and the Srinjayas are gathered here," said Yudhishthira, "and
Janardana, Yuyudhana and Virata. Tell us the message Dhritarashtra gave you, son
of Gavalgana, suta's son."

"I greet Ajatashatru and Vrikodara," said Sanjaya, "Dhananjaya and the two sons
of Madri, Vasudeva, Shauri, Yuyudhana, Chekitana and Virata, the old lord of the
Panchalas, and Dhrishtadyumna, son of Prishata, Yajnasena's son. Listen, all of
you, to what I have to say. I say it wishing the good of the Kurus.

"King Dhritarashtra welcomes peace, and he had my chariot yoked in haste. Let
the Pandavas accept this, for the sake of the king, with his brothers, his sons
and his people. Let there be peace.

"Parthas, you have every virtue, and firmness, gentleness and honesty. You were
born in a high family. You are kind and generous, shame holds you back, and you
know what must be done. A low act does not suit you. Your strength is such, and
you have a terrible army. Any wrong you did would stand out like a drop of
collyrium on a white cloth. Who that knows would ever do a thing in which the
utter ruin of all is seen, a thing that brings forth sin, that leads to hell,
that ends in nothing, where defeat and victory are the same?

"Blessed are they who have done their duty by their kin. Your sons, your friends
and your kin should give up a life that is condemned, so that the Kurus may
surely prosper. If you, Parthas, were to punish the Kurus, overcome all your
enemies and crush them, that life of yours would be the same as death. Life is
not good when you live by killing your kin.

"Who could conquer you, with Keshava, with Chekitana, guarded by the arms of
Prishata's son, with Satyaki, even if he had the gods and Indra for his allies?
And who, king, would dare to face the Kurus in battle, guarded by Drona and
Bhishma, by Ashvatthama, Shalya, Kripa and the rest, and by Radheya, with the
kings of the earth? Who could destroy the great army of King Dhritarashtra's son
without being destroyed himself? So I see no good at all in victory, or in
defeat.

"How could the Parthas, like low men of low family, do a deed that is empty of
dharma and profit? So I bow and plead with Vasudeva, and with the old lord of
the Panchalas. With joined palms I come to you for refuge. How can there be
well-being for the Kurus and the Srinjayas? Vasudeva or Dhananjaya would never
refuse any word of yours. Asked for their lives first, how could they refuse
anything else? I say this, learned one, to bring it about. This is the view of
the king, with Bhishma at his head, so that you may have the highest peace
here."

**Divergences.**

* **Dhritarashtra's lament.** A rejected line (\*125) has Dhritarashtra say at the start that his son Duryodhana has fallen into the power of death. Not narrated.
* **Kripa.** A rejected line (\*129) adds Kripa, "Sharadvat's son", to Yudhishthira's questions. The CE already names "the brahmin Kripa" (5.23.10).
* **The gifts of kingdoms.** A rejected passage (\*133) has Sanjaya promise wealth, jewels and kingdoms to the Pandavas if they obey the old king. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*126–\*128, \*130–\*132) add details. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.22.1 *grāmam upasthitas tvam*: "that you have come to a village", of Upaplavya.
* 5.22.14 *hastyāroho nāsti samaś ca tasya*: "no one on an elephant who is his match".
* 5.22.18 *śālveyānām adhipo vai virāṭaḥ*: Virata, "lord of the Shalveyas".
* 5.22.19: the five Kekaya brothers are exiles who want their kingdom back. Here they are on the Pandavas' side. In 5.19.25 five Kekaya brothers bring Duryodhana an akshauhini. The text has both.
* 5.22.23 *kārṣṇipratimam*: Satyaki is "the only match for Krishna's son".
* 5.22.25–28: the killing of Shishupala, as Dhritarashtra has heard it. *sugrīvayuktena rathena* is "his chariot drawn by Sugriva and the other horses". Sugriva is one of Krishna's horses.
* 5.22.30 *kṛṣṇāv ekarathe*: "the two Krishnas together in one chariot", Krishna and Arjuna.
* 5.22.36 *tāta*: Dhritarashtra calls Sanjaya "my son", an affectionate address, not a relationship.
* 5.23.16 *hetor iva vartmabhūta*: "as if they were only a road to his own ends".
* 5.23.21 *yasyaikaṣaṣṭir niśitās tīkṣṇadhārāḥ … hastavāpaḥ*: Arjuna's "one approved hand-throw is sixty-one sharp, keen-edged, well-feathered arrows".
* 5.23.23: Sahadeva beat the Kalingas at Dantakura. The novel keeps the text's attribution.
* 5.23.25 *jayaś ca*: "Jaya", a name of Arjuna. Jaya is also the first of the secret names given in 4.5.30. The next verse shows it is Arjuna who fought.
* 5.23.26: "I guarded Arjuna from behind, and Bhimasena the sons of Madri" follows Ganguli's reading of a compressed verse.
* 5.25.6 *añjanabinduvat*: "like a drop of collyrium on a white cloth", the text's image.
* 5.25.15: "Asked for their lives first, how could they refuse anything else?" renders *prāṇān ādau yācyamānaḥ kuto 'nyad*.
