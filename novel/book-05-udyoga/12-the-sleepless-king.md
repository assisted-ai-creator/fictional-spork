summary: Dhritarashtra asks Vidura for more; Vidura counsels him to weigh the consequences of every act, to gather wealth as a bee gathers honey, to conquer himself before others, to guard his tongue, and warns that the gods take away the understanding of those they mean to ruin, as they have taken his sons'; he tells how Virochana and Sudhanvan staked their lives on which of them was better, and how Prahlada judged truly against his own son; he sets out the wrongs that equal the killing of a brahmin, the eight qualities that make a man shine and the marks of a true assembly, and ends by asking how the king can hope for prosperity with the kingdom in the hands of Duryodhana, Shakuni, Duhshasana and Karna.

# The Sleepless King

"Tell me what you see must be done for one who lies awake and burns," said
Dhritarashtra, "for you are skilled in dharma and profit, my son, and pure.
Teach me properly, Vidura. Tell me, with your wisdom, all that Ajatashatru has
in mind. Tell me what you think is wholesome, you whose spirit does not fail,
and what will do the Kurus good. I suspect evil, and I see nothing but evil, and
I ask you with a troubled mind. Tell me all of it, wise one, just as it is:
everything Ajatashatru means."

"Good or evil," said Vidura, "hateful or pleasant, a man should say it unasked
to anyone whose defeat he does not want. So I will tell you, king, wishing the
Kurus well. Listen to my words, which are for your good and full of dharma.

"Do not set your mind on deeds that succeed by deceit, Bharata, and are done by
wrong means. And if a deed done by the right means, with care, does not succeed,
king, a wise man should not let his mind sink over it.

"Look at the consequences of acts that have consequences. Think them through
before you act, and do not rush. When he has looked at the consequences, at how
his acts will ripen, and at his own strength, a steady man either acts or does
not.

"A king who does not know the measure of his place, his growth and his decline,
of his treasury, his country and his army, does not keep his kingdom. But one
who looks to these measures, as they are set out, and is joined to the knowledge
of dharma and profit, wins the kingdom.

"A man should not behave badly because he has a kingdom. Bad conduct kills
fortune, as old age kills fine looks.

"A fish, greedy for what it sees, swallows the iron hook covered with the best
bait, and does not look at the consequence. A man who wants prosperity should
eat what can be eaten, what should be eaten, what can be digested once eaten,
and what does him good when it is digested.

"A man who picks unripe fruit from a tree gets no juice from it, and its seed is
destroyed. But a man who takes ripe fruit in its season gets juice from the
fruit, and fruit again from the seed.

"As the bee takes honey and spares the flowers, so a man should take wealth from
men without harming them. Pick flower by flower, like the garland-maker in his
garden, and do not cut the root, like the charcoal-burner.

"'What will happen to me if I do this? What will happen to me if I do not?' A
man should think his deeds through like that, and then act, or not act.

"Some things should never be begun, for they always turn out the same way, and
any effort put into them would be useless. A wise man quickly begins the things
that have small roots and great fruits, and does not hinder them.

"The man who looks at everything straight, as if drinking it in with his eyes,
is loved by his people even when he sits in silence. The man who pleases the
world in four ways, with his eye, his mind, his speech and his deeds, is the man
the world is pleased with in return. The man whom creatures fear, as deer fear
the hunter, loses the earth even when he has won it to the edge of the sea.

"A king who has won his fathers' and grandfathers' kingdom by his own power, and
then falls into wrong ways, throws it away, as the wind blows away a cloud. For
a king who practises dharma, as the good have practised it from the beginning,
the earth, full of wealth, grows and increases his prosperity. But for one who
gives up dharma and follows what is against it, the earth shrivels up, like a
hide laid on the fire.

"The same effort that is spent crushing another's kingdom should be spent
guarding your own. Win a kingdom by dharma and guard it by dharma. When a king
has won a fortune rooted in dharma, he does not lose it, and it does not leave
him.

"Take what is worth having from everything, even from a madman raving, or a
child crawling, as gold is taken from stones. A steady man gathers good sayings
and good deeds from the wise, here and there, as a gleaner gathers grain.

"Cows see by smell. Brahmins see by the Vedas. Kings see by their spies. Other
people see with their eyes.

"The cow that is hard to milk suffers more, king. The one that is easy to milk
is not troubled. What bends without being heated, they do not heat. Wood that
bends of itself, they do not bend. By this example a steady man should bow to
the stronger. The man who bows to the stronger bows to Indra.

"Cattle have the rain cloud for their protector. Kings have their friends. Women
have their husbands. Brahmins have the Vedas.

"Dharma is guarded by truth, learning by practice, beauty by washing, and family
by conduct. Grain is guarded by measure, horses by training, cows by being
looked at often, and women by plain clothes. I think family is no measure of a
man whose conduct is lost. Even among those born low, conduct is what counts.

"The man who envies others their wealth, looks, strength, family, happiness,
good fortune and honour has a sickness without end.

"Afraid of doing what should not be done, of leaving undone what should be done,
and of letting counsel out too early, a man should not drink what intoxicates.

"Pride of learning, pride of wealth, and the third, pride of birth: these are
the prides of the proud, and in the good they are the very things that tame.

"When the good ask the bad for something, the bad think themselves good, however
notorious they are. The good are the refuge of the self-possessed. The good are
the refuge of the good. The good are the refuge of the bad. But the bad are not
the refuge of the good.

"The man with good clothes wins the assembly. The man with cows wins his hopes.
The man with a carriage wins the road. The man with good character wins
everything. Character is the chief thing in a man. When it is lost, he has no
use for life, or wealth, or kin.

"The food of the rich is mostly meat, bull of the Bharatas. The food of the
middling is mostly milk and curds. The food of the poor is mostly salt. And yet
the poor always eat the better meal, for hunger gives food its taste, and hunger
is very hard for the rich to find. In the world the wealthy mostly have no power
to eat, king of kings, while the poor can digest even wood.

"The low fear having no living. The middling fear death. The best of men fear
dishonour most of all.

"Of all the intoxications, the pride of power is the worst, worse than drink and
the rest. A man drunk with power does not come to his senses until he has
fallen.

"This world is tormented by the senses, running unchecked among the things of
the senses, as the stars are tormented by the planets. The man who is beaten by
the five that were born with him, and wear him down, sees his troubles grow like
the moon in the bright half of the month.

"The man who has not conquered himself and wants to conquer his ministers, or
who has not conquered his ministers and wants to conquer his enemies, is
helpless and is lost. The man who first conquers himself, as if he were a
country, and then his ministers and his enemies, does not hope to conquer in
vain. Fortune waits long on a steady man whose senses are in his power, whose
ministers are won, who holds the rod over wrongdoers, and who acts after
testing.

"The body is a man's chariot, king. His self is the driver, and his senses are
the horses. Driven by those good horses, well broken, the wise and careful man
goes happily, like a steady charioteer. But unchecked they can kill him, as
unruly, untrained horses kill a bad driver on the road.

"The fool, carried off by his senses, sees gain in loss and loss in gain, and
thinks great sorrow is happiness. The man who gives up dharma and profit and
follows the power of his senses quickly loses fortune, life, wealth and wife.
The man who is lord of riches but not lord of his senses falls from his
lordship, because he is not lord of his senses.

"A man should seek himself through himself, with mind, understanding and senses
held in check. For the self alone is the friend of the self, and the self alone
is the enemy of the self.

"Desire and anger, king, like two big fish caught in a net of small mesh, tear
through a man's wisdom. The man who looks to dharma and profit and gathers what
he needs is well provided, and always prospers in happiness. The man who has not
conquered the five enemies within, that destroy the mind, and wants to conquer
other enemies, is overcome by his enemies. For we see wicked men destroyed by
their own deeds, and kings by the turmoil of their kingdoms, because they are
not lords of their senses.

"When the wicked are not cast off, the same punishment touches the innocent,
because they are mixed together. The wet burns with the dry when they are mixed.
So a man should make no pact with the wicked.

"The man who, out of folly, does not hold down the five enemies of his own that
rise up with their five purposes is swallowed by misfortune.

"Freedom from envy, honesty, purity, contentment, kind speech, self-control,
truth and calm are not found in the wicked. Knowledge of the self, calm,
patience, constancy in dharma, guarded speech and generosity are not found in
the low, Bharata.

"Fools hurt the wise with abuse and slander. The speaker takes on the sin, and
the one who forgives is freed from it. Violence is the strength of the wicked.
Punishment is the strength of kings. Service is the strength of women.
Forgiveness is the strength of the virtuous.

"Control of speech, lord of men, is thought the hardest thing of all. A man
cannot say much that is both meaningful and pleasing. Speech of many kinds, well
spoken, brings good. The same speech, badly spoken, king, leads to harm.

"A forest cut with an axe grows again, and a man pierced with arrows heals. But
a hideous wound made by an evil word never heals. Barbs, reeds and iron arrows
can be drawn out of the body. But the dart of a word cannot be drawn out, for it
lies in the heart. The arrows of speech fly out of the mouth, and the man they
strike grieves day and night. They find nothing but the tender places of
another. A wise man does not loose them at others.

"When the gods mean to bring a man to defeat, they first take away his
understanding, and he sees things upside down. When his understanding is muddied
and ruin is at hand, bad policy looks like good and will not leave his heart.
That is what has happened to your sons' understanding, Bharata, in their quarrel
with the Pandavas, and you do not see it.

"Yudhishthira, who has every sign of a king, who could be king of the three
worlds, is your pupil, Dhritarashtra. Let him rule. He stands far above all your
sons, favoured by fortune, full of power and wisdom, knowing the truth of dharma
and profit. Out of kindness and compassion, and out of respect for you, king of
kings, the best of the upholders of dharma bears many hardships."

---

"Tell me more, wise one," said Dhritarashtra, "words joined to dharma and
profit. I am never satisfied with hearing them. What you say is wonderful."

"Bathing at every holy ford," said Vidura, "and honesty towards all beings may
be equal, or honesty may be better. Be honest with your sons always, lord. You
will win the highest fame here, and after death you will reach heaven. As long
as a man's good name is sung in the worlds, tiger among men, so long is he
honoured in heaven.

"On this they tell an old story, the dispute between Virochana and Sudhanvan
over Keshini.

"'Are brahmins better, Virochana,' Keshini asked, 'or the sons of Diti? And why
should Sudhanvan not sit on the couch?'

"'We children of Prajapati are the best, Keshini, the best of all,' said
Virochana. 'These worlds are ours. Who are the gods? Who are the brahmins?'

"'Let us wait here in the hall of audience, Virochana,' said Keshini. 'Sudhanvan
will come in the morning. I would like to see the two of you together.'

"'I will do as you say, gentle one,' said Virochana. 'You will see Sudhanvan and
me together in the morning.'

"'I will touch your golden seat, son of Prahlada,' said Sudhanvan, 'but I will
not sit on it with you, as if we were one and the same.'

"'Let them bring you a plank, or a bundle of grass, or a mat,' said Virochana.
'You are not fit to share a seat with me, Sudhanvan.'

"'Your father himself would sit below me when I was seated,' said Sudhanvan.
'You are a child, brought up in comfort at home. You know nothing.'

"'All the gold, cattle and horses we asuras have,' said Virochana, 'let us stake
them, Sudhanvan, and ask those who know.'

"'Keep your gold and cattle and horses, Virochana,' said Sudhanvan. 'Let us
stake our lives, and ask those who know.'

"'Where shall we go, when we have staked our lives?' said Virochana. 'I will
never stand before the gods, or before men.'

"'We will go to your father, when we have staked our lives,' said Sudhanvan.
'Prahlada would not tell a lie, even for his son.'

"'These two are seen here,' said Prahlada, 'who never went about together
before, come by the same road, like two angry snakes. Why do you go about
together now, who never went together before? I ask you, Virochana, are you
friends with Sudhanvan?'

"'I am no friend of Sudhanvan's,' said Virochana. 'We have staked our lives.
Prahlada, I ask you: do not answer the question falsely.'

"'Let them bring water and honey-milk for Sudhanvan,' said Prahlada. 'You are to
be honoured, brahmin. A fat white cow has been made ready.'

"'The water and honey-milk were given me on the road,' said Sudhanvan.
'Prahlada, tell us the truth of the question we ask.'

"'Brahmin, when a son stands on one side,' said Prahlada, 'and you on the other,
and a man is the witness, how can such as I decide the question of the two who
dispute? And if a man gives no answer at all, true or false, I ask you this,
Sudhanvan: what does such a bad judge live through?'

"'The bad judge lives through the night,' said Sudhanvan, 'that a wife lives
through when her husband has taken another wife, that a man beaten at dice lives
through, and a man whose body aches under a heavy load. He lives through the
night of a man shut out of a city, hungry at the outer gate, who sees his
enemies grow in number. A man who lies about a beast kills five of his line. A
lie about a cow kills ten. A lie about a horse kills a hundred. A lie about a
man kills a thousand. He who lies about gold kills those born and those unborn.
A lie about land kills everything. Never tell a lie about land.'

"'Angiras is better than I am,' said Prahlada, 'and Sudhanvan is better than
you, Virochana. His mother is better than your mother. So he has beaten you.
Virochana, Sudhanvan is now the master of your life. Sudhanvan, I would like you
to give me Virochana back.'

"'Because you chose dharma,' said Sudhanvan, 'and did not lie out of love, I
give you back your son, Prahlada, whom it is so hard to give back. Here is your
son Virochana, Prahlada, given back by me. But let him wash my feet in front of
the girl.'

---

"So, king of kings," said Vidura, "you should not tell a lie for the sake of
land. Do not go to your ruin, with your sons and ministers, by following your
sons.

"The gods do not take a stick and guard a man, as a herdsman guards his cattle.
When they want to protect a man, they give him understanding. As far as a man
sets his mind on good, so far all his purposes succeed, no doubt of it.

"The Vedas do not carry across from his sin a cheat who lives by trickery. At
the end the Vedas leave him, as fledged birds leave the nest.

"They say a man should avoid these: drinking, quarrels, feuds with many, coming
between husband and wife, dividing kin, what the king hates, disputes between
man and woman, and a road that is corrupt.

"These seven should not be taken as witnesses: a palm-reader, a merchant who was
once a thief, a cheat with the dice, a doctor, an enemy, a friend and an actor.

"The fire offering made for pride, silence kept for pride, learning pursued for
pride, and sacrifice made for pride: these four things drive away fear, but done
wrongly they bring it.

"These are all equal to the killers of brahmins. There is the man who burns
houses, the poisoner, the son of an adulterous wife who eats his food, the
seller of soma, the maker of arrows and the informer. There is the man who
betrays his friend, the adulterer, the killer of a foetus, the man who defiles
his teacher's bed, and the twice-born who drinks. There is the man who is too
harsh, the crow-like man, the unbeliever and the scorner of the Veda. And there
is the man who takes the sacrificial ladle for hire, the outcast, the miser even
when he is rich, and the man who harms one who asked him for protection.

"Gold is known by the fire of burning grass. A gentle man is known by his
dealings, and a good man by his conduct. A hero is known in danger, a steady man
in hard times, and friends and enemies in great misfortune.

"Old age takes beauty, hope takes firmness, death takes life, envy takes the
practice of dharma, anger takes fortune, serving the base takes character,
desire takes shame, and pride takes everything.

"Fortune comes from good deeds, grows with boldness, takes root through skill,
and stands firm through self-control.

"Eight qualities make a man shine: wisdom, good family, self-control, learning,
courage, speaking little, giving as far as he can, and gratitude. My friend, one
quality takes hold of all these great qualities by force: when the king honours
a man, that one quality outshines all the rest.

"There are eight marks of the world of heaven in this world of men, king. The
good follow four of them, and the good practise four. Sacrifice, giving, study
and austerity: the good follow these four. Self-control, truth, honesty and
kindness: the good practise these four.

"It is no assembly where there are no elders. They are no elders who do not
speak dharma. It is not dharma where there is no truth. It is not truth that is
mixed with deceit.

"Truth, beauty, learning, knowledge, good family, character, strength, wealth,
courage and fine speech: these ten are the sources of connection between men.

"The man who does evil has an evil name and reaps nothing but evil fruit. The
man who does good has a good name and reaps nothing but good fruit. Evil done
again and again destroys wisdom. The man whose wisdom is destroyed always begins
what is evil. Good done again and again increases wisdom. The man whose wisdom
has grown always begins what is good. The envious man, who bites like a snake,
who is cruel and makes enemies, soon comes to great misery by doing evil. The
man free of envy, of formed wisdom, always doing fine deeds, comes to happiness
without hardship, and shines everywhere.

"The man who takes wisdom from the wise is wise. A wise man who has won dharma
and profit can prosper in happiness.

"By day a man should do what will let him live happily at night. In eight months
he should do what will let him live happily in the rains. In youth he should do
what will let him live happily when old. All his life he should do what will let
him live happily after death.

"They praise food that has been digested, a wife whose youth is past, a hero who
has come through the battle, and an ascetic who has reached the far shore.

"The hole that is stopped with wealth got against dharma stays open, and
something else breaks open beyond it.

"The teacher rules those who are master of themselves. The king rules the
wicked. And Yama, son of Vivasvat, rules those who sin in secret.

"The source of seers, of rivers, of great families, of women, and of wrongdoing
should not be sought.

"The kshatriya who delights in honouring the twice-born, who is generous and
honest with his kin, has his share of heaven, king, and rules the earth long.

"Three men gather the earth's golden flowers: the hero, the learned man, and the
man who knows how to serve.

"Of deeds, Bharata, those of the mind are the best, those of the arm are
middling, those of the legs are the lowest, and those of carrying burdens are
the lowest of all.

"With the lordship placed in Duryodhana, in Shakuni, in foolish Duhshasana and
in Karna, how can you hope for prosperity? The Pandavas have every virtue, bull
of the Bharatas, and they behave to you as to a father. Behave to them as to
sons."

**Divergences.**

* **"Those who say pleasant things are easy to find."** The well-known saying that men who speak pleasantly are easy to find, but a speaker and hearer of unpleasant good advice are rare, is a rejected line here (5.34.\*186). The CE has it later, at 5.37.14, and it is told there.
* **"The gods do not guard with a stick"** appears as a rejected line at 5.34.\*192 and in the CE text at 5.35.33. The novel tells it once, at 5.35.33.
* **Keshini's bridegroom choice.** A rejected passage (\*193) sets the story at Keshini's bridegroom choice. In the CE she simply asks Virochana her question (5.35.6).
* **The hamsa.** A long rejected passage (\*207, \*209) brings in a swan (hamsa) who questions Prahlada, and adds the division of sin among the chief, the doer and the silent members of an assembly. Not narrated. The same teaching on the silent assembly is also rejected at \*214.
* **"It is no assembly…"** This verse (5.35.49) is CE here, spoken by Vidura. The vulgate also puts it in Draupadi's mouth in the dice hall, where it is rejected (ledger L-118).
* Other rejected lines (\*187–\*191, \*194–\*206, \*208, \*210–\*213, \*215–\*218) add sayings or repeat them. Not narrated.
