# Yayati's Fall

"'Then the king,'" said Narada, "'wanted to hold a bridegroom choice for her
again. Puru and Yadu, her brothers, went to an ashram at the meeting of the
Ganga and the Yamuna. They put Madhavi, holding the garland, on a chariot, and
drove her round the ashram. Nagas, yakshas and men, birds and beasts and winged
creatures, and those who live in the mountains, trees and forests, were gathered
there. The place was crowded with the lords of many peoples and lands, and the
forest was ringed on every side with seers like Brahma himself.

"'As the suitors were pointed out to her, the lovely girl passed over them all
and chose the forest as her bridegroom. The girl got down from the chariot and
bowed to her kin. Then Yayati's daughter went into the holy forest and practised
austerity. With fasts of many kinds, with consecrations and vows, she made
herself light and lived like a deer. She grazed on the best of the young grass,
soft and green like shoots of beryl, bitter and sweet. She drank the best of
waters from holy streams, sweet, pure, cool and clear. In forests full of deer,
where lions had gone away and forest fires had passed by, in empty thickets, she
roamed with the deer, like a doe that lives in the forest, and practised great
dharma, bound by chastity.

"'Yayati for his part kept to the ways of the kings before him. He lived many
thousands of years, and then he met the law of time. Through the two best of
men, Puru and Yadu, two lines grew, and by them the son of Nahusha was
established in this world and in the next. King Yayati was glorified in heaven,
a king like a great seer, a lord who enjoyed the best fruits of heaven.

"'When a long time had passed, many thousands of years, full of good things, and
the royal seers and great seers sat in glory, Yayati despised all men, and the
gods and the hosts of seers. His understanding was clouded, and his mind was
filled with pride. Then the god Shakra, slayer of Bala, knew him for what he
was, and all the royal seers said, "Shame on him! Shame!" As they looked at the
son of Nahusha, they began to ask, "Who is this? Whose king is he? How did he
come to heaven? By what deed did he win perfection? Where did he gather his
austerity? How is he known in heaven, and who knows him?"

"'So the kings who lived in heaven asked each other about King Yayati when they
saw him. The keepers of the flying palaces, the guards of heaven's gate by the
hundred, and the keepers of the seats were asked, and they all said, "We do not
know." Their knowledge was covered over, and none of them knew the king. And in
a moment the king lost his power.

"'He was shaken from his place and fell from his seat. His mind trembled, and
the fire of grief overcame him. His garlands faded and his understanding was
lost. His crown and his armlets fell off. He reeled, all his limbs went slack,
and his ornaments and his clothes fell from him. He could not be seen, and he
saw them, and again and again he did not see them. Empty, with an empty mind, he
was about to fall to the earth. "What evil thought against dharma did I have in
my mind," the king wondered, "that I should be shaken from my place?"

"'The kings there, and the siddhas and the apsarases, saw Yayati fallen and with
nothing to hold on to. Then a man came, one who throws down those whose merit is
spent, and said to Yayati, at the command of the king of the gods, "You are
drunk with pride beyond measure. There is no one you do not despise. Your pride
has lost you heaven. You do not deserve it, prince, and no one knows you. Go.
Fall."

"'"Let me fall among the good," said the son of Nahusha three times. As he was
falling, the best of those who have a goal thought where he could go. Just then
the king saw four bulls among kings in the Naimisha forest, and he fell among
them. They were Pratardana, Vasumanas, Shibi son of Ushinara and Ashtaka, and
they were pleasing the lord of the gods with a Vajapeya sacrifice. Yayati
smelled the smoke of their sacrifice, which had risen to the gate of heaven, and
he fell towards the earth. The king held on to that river of smoke, which joined
earth and heaven, as if it were the Ganga flowing down. The king fell among the
four fine men at the end of their sacrifice, his kinsmen, who were like the
guardians of the world. The royal seer Yayati fell, his merit spent, among those
four great fires, lions among kings, like offerings in a fire.

"'All the kings said to him, as if he were the very image of fortune, "Who are
you? Whose kinsman are you, of what land or city? Are you a yaksha, or a god, or
a gandharva or a rakshasa? You do not look like a man. What is it you want?"

"'"I am Yayati, a royal seer," said Yayati. "My merit is spent, and I have
fallen from heaven. I thought, 'Let me fall among the good,' and so I have
fallen among you."

"'"May your wish come true, bull among men," said the kings. "Take the fruit of
all our sacrifices, and our dharma."

"'"I am not a brahmin, whose wealth is what he is given," said Yayati. "I am a
kshatriya. And my mind is not bent on destroying others' merit."

"'Just then the kings saw Madhavi, who had come along on her wanderings as a
deer. They greeted her and said, "Why have you come? What can we do at your
command? We are all yours to command, for we are your sons, you who are rich in
austerity."

"'When Madhavi heard what they said, she was filled with joy. She went to her
father Yayati and bowed to him. When she saw her sons bowing their heads, the
ascetic said, "These are your daughter's sons, king of kings. They are my sons,
and not strangers to you. They will save you. This was ordained long ago. I am
your daughter, king, Madhavi, who lives as a deer. I too have gathered dharma.
Take half of it. Because all men share in the fruit of their children, king,
they want daughter's sons, lord of the earth, as you did."

"'Then all the kings bowed their heads to their mother and did her honour. They
said to their mother's father, filling the earth with their voices, loud and
matchless and kind, "Let us save our mother's father, who has fallen from
heaven."

"'Then Galava came there from that place and said to the king, "Climb to heaven
with an eighth part of my austerity."

"'As soon as the good men had recognised him,' said Narada, 'Yayati, the bull
among men, was free of his torment and in his heavenly state again. He wore
heavenly garlands and clothes, was dressed in heavenly ornaments, and had a
heavenly fragrance, and his feet did not touch the earth.

"'Then Vasumanas, famous in the world as a lord of giving, spoke first, raising
his voice to the king. "Whatever I have won in the world without blame from any
class, that too I shall give you now. Take it for your own. The fruit of a man
who is generous, the fruit of a man who is patient, and the fruit I have won by
keeping the fires: take it for your own."

"'Then Pratardana, a bull among kshatriyas, said, "As I have always delighted in
dharma, and always been devoted to war, so take for your own the fame I have won
in the world from the kshatriya's dharma, and the fruit of the name of hero."

"'Wise Shibi, son of Ushinara, spoke in a sweet voice. "I have never told a lie,
to children or to women, in jest or in battle, in disaster, in danger or in
trouble. By that truth, go to heaven. I would give up my life, my kingdom, king,
my work and my pleasures, but never the truth. By that truth, go to heaven. As
dharma is pleased with me by truth, and fire by truth, and Shakra is pleased by
truth, by that truth go to heaven."

"'Then the royal seer Ashtaka, the son of Kaushika and Madhavi, who knew dharma,
spoke to the king who had performed hundreds of sacrifices. "I have performed
Pundarikas by the hundred, my lord, and Gosavas and Vajapeya sacrifices. Take
their fruit. I have no jewels, no wealth and no other goods that I have not used
for the sacrifices. By that truth, go to heaven."

"'As his daughter's sons spoke to the king, one after another, so the king left
the earth, step by step, and went to heaven. So all the kings together, by their
good deeds, quickly saved Yayati, who had fallen from heaven. Born in four royal
lines and making them grow, the daughter's sons raised their wise mother's
father to heaven by their own dharma, won by sacrifice and giving.

"'"We have the virtues of the king's dharma," the kings said, "and the virtues
of every dharma. We are your daughter's sons, king. Climb up to heaven, lord of
the earth."

"'Raised to heaven by those good kings, who gave generous fees, Yayati said
goodbye to his daughter's sons and took his place in heaven. A rain of
sweet-smelling flowers of every kind fell on him, and a holy wind with a holy
scent embraced him. He mounted to the unshaken place won for him by the fruit of
his daughter's sons, and grown by his own deeds he blazed with the highest
splendour. Hosts of gandharvas and apsarases sang and danced for him. He was
welcomed gladly in heaven to the sound of drums. Gods, royal seers and bards
praised him in many ways. He was honoured with the finest guest water and
greeted by the gods.

"'When he had won the fruit of heaven, and was at peace and calm in his mind,
the Grandfather spoke to him, as if to refresh him with his words. "You have
gathered dharma on all four feet by deeds that served the world. This world of
yours cannot be lost, and nor can your fame in heaven. And again today, royal
seer, by good deeds here. The minds of all who live in heaven were covered with
darkness, and so they did not know you. Because they did not know you, you were
thrown down. But out of love your daughter's sons have saved you, and you have
come here. You have reached the place won by your own deeds, unshaken, eternal
and holy, the highest, fixed and unchanging."

"'"Lord," said Yayati, "I have a doubt. Please cut through it. There is no one
else I can ask, Grandfather of the worlds. Over many thousands of years I grew
great fruit by protecting my people, and won it by floods of sacrifices and
gifts. How did it run out in so short a time, so that I was thrown down? You
know, lord, that the worlds I had won were eternal."

"'"Over many thousands of years you grew great fruit by protecting your people,"
said the Grandfather, "and won it by floods of sacrifices and gifts. It ran out
through this one fault, so that you were thrown down, king of kings. Through
your pride the dwellers in heaven cried shame on you. This world is not made
eternal by pride, royal seer, nor by strength, nor by cruelty, nor by cunning,
nor by tricks. You must not despise anyone, king, lower, higher or in between.
For there is no one anywhere so low as those burned up by pride. Those who tell
this story of the fall and the rising will surely come through even hard times."

"'This was the fault that Yayati fell into long ago through pride, lord of the
earth,' said Narada, 'and Galava through too much obstinacy. One should listen
to friends who want one's good and wish one well. One should not be obstinate,
for obstinacy leads to ruin. So you too, son of Gandhari, give up pride and
anger. Make peace with the Pandavas, hero. Give up your rage, king.

"'What a man gives, king, what he does, the austerity he practises and what he
offers in the fire, that is never lost, and it never grows less. No one else
enjoys it. The doer alone does. This great story, the best of all, is the view
of the learned, who have put away anger and passion. When a man has looked at it
well, the vision of the three aims of life, which runs in so many ways through
the world, will be his, and he will enjoy the earth.'"

**Divergences.**

* **Madhavi chooses the forest.** At her bridegroom choice Madhavi passes over all the suitors and *vanaṃ vṛtavatī varam*, "chose the forest as her bridegroom" (5.118.5), and lives in austerity like a deer. She does not marry Galava, and no one else is chosen. See ledger L-179.
* **The two tellings of Yayati's fall.** Book 1 tells another version (1.86–88), in which the four grandsons offer Yayati their worlds and he refuses them, and their mother is not named. Here, in Narada's story, Madhavi gives half her merit and Galava an eighth, and the kings' merit lifts him (5.119.24, 28; 5.120.15–17). Both are CE, and each is told as its speaker tells it.
* **Duryodhana's answer.** A rejected passage (\*470) adds that Duryodhana did not heed Narada. In the CE Narada's story ends with its lesson and the praise of the tale (5.121.18–22). Not narrated. Nor are \*469 and \*471.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*463–\*468, which repeat Yayati's rising, add to Pratardana's and Ashtaka's words, and add a line on friends.
