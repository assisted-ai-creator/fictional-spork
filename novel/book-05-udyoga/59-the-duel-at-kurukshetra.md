# The Duel at Kurukshetra

"'Smiling, as it were,'" said Bhishma, "'I said to him as he stood ready in the
battle, "I cannot fight you on the ground while I stand on a chariot. Mount a
chariot, hero, and put on armour, strong-armed Rama, if you want to fight me in
battle."

"'Then Rama said to me, smiling on the battlefield, "The earth is my chariot,
Bhishma, the Vedas are my horses, like fine horses. The wind is my charioteer,
and the mothers of the Veda are my armour. Well covered by them in battle, I
will fight, joy of the Kurus." Speaking like this, son of Gandhari, Rama, true
in courage, surrounded me on every side with a great mass of arrows.

"'Then I saw Jamadagni's son standing in a heavenly chariot, holding every
weapon, glorious, wonderful to see. It was made by the mind, holy, wide as a
city, yoked with heavenly horses, armoured and adorned with gold, and it had a
banner, strong-armed one, with the moon for its emblem. He held a bow, with
quivers bound on, and wore the lizard-skin guard on his fingers. Akritavrana,
his friend, who knew the Veda and was very dear to the Bhargava, drove the
chariot for him as he set out to fight. Calling me to battle again and again,
crying out, "Come on!", the Bhargava seemed to gladden my heart. I went alone to
meet Rama alone, mighty, unassailable like the rising sun, the destroyer of the
kshatriyas.

"'Then, when I was three bowshots away, I reined in my horses, got down, laid
aside my bow, and went on foot to the best of seers, Rama, to honour the best of
the twice-born. I greeted him by the rules and spoke these fine words. "I will
fight you in battle, Rama, who are my better and my superior, my teacher,
devoted to dharma. Wish me victory, lord."

"'"So it must be done, best of the Kurus, by one who wants to prosper," said
Rama. "This is the dharma, strong-armed one, of those who fight their betters. I
would have cursed you, lord of the people, if you had not come like this. Fight
carefully in battle, Kaurava, holding to your steadiness. But I do not wish you
victory, for I stand here to beat you. Go, fight by dharma. I am pleased with
your conduct."

"'Then I bowed to him, mounted my chariot quickly, and blew my conch, worked
with gold, again on the battlefield. Then the battle began between him and me,
Bharata, for very many days, king, each wanting to beat the other. In that
battle he struck me first with nine hundred and sixty heron-feathered arrows,
bright as fire. My four horses and my charioteer were stopped by them, lord of
the people, and I stood in the battle in my armour. Bowing to the gods and the
brahmins, Bharata, I said to him as he stood ready, smiling, as it were:

"'"I have honoured your place as teacher, though you have no bounds. But hear
further, brahmin, the means of gaining dharma. I do not strike at the Vedas that
are in your body, nor your great brahminhood, nor the very great austerity you
have practised. I strike at the dharma of the kshatriya that you have taken up,
Rama. For a brahmin becomes a kshatriya when he raises a weapon. See the power
of my bow, see the strength of my arms. I will cut your bow in two, hero, with
its arrow."

"'I let fly a sharp broad-headed arrow at him, bull of the Bharatas, and with it
the end of his bow was cut off and fell to the ground. I let fly nine hundred
heron-feathered arrows with bent joints at Jamadagni's son's chariot. Sticking
in his body, driven by the wind, those arrows moved, dripping blood, like
snakes. Wet with blood all over, his wounds streaming blood, Rama shone then,
king, like Meru giving out its ores, like an ashoka at the end of winter adorned
with red clusters. Here and there Rama looked like a kimshuka tree, king.

"'Then Rama took another bow in his anger and rained those very sharp,
gold-feathered arrows on me. Fierce, piercing my vitals in many places, with
great force, like snakes, fire and poison, they reached me and made me tremble.
Then I steadied myself again in the battle and in my anger covered Rama with
arrows by the hundred. Struck by those sharp arrows, bright as fire and sun,
like poisonous snakes, Rama became as if dazed. Then, filled with pity, I blamed
myself and said, "Shame! Shame on war and on the kshatriya class!", bull of the
Bharatas. Again and again I said, king, swept by a flood of grief, "Oh, I have
done evil by this kshatriya work, that my teacher, a twice-born man whose soul
is dharma, has been hurt by arrows like this." After that I struck no more at
Jamadagni's son, Bharata. Then, at the close of the day, the thousand-rayed sun,
having warmed the earth, set, and the battle stopped.

---

"'Then my skilled charioteer drew out the arrows from himself, from the horses
and from me, lord of the people. With the horses bathed, rolled, watered and
steady, the battle began again in the morning when the sun rose. When mighty
Rama saw me coming quickly, in armour, standing on my chariot, he made his
chariot ready in earnest. Seeing Rama coming, eager for battle, I quickly laid
down my fine bow and got down from my chariot. I greeted him in the same way,
Bharata, mounted my chariot, and stood fearless before Jamadagni's son, eager to
fight.

"'Then he covered me with a great rain of arrows, and I covered him with a rain
of arrows as he rained on me. Jamadagni's son in his fury again sent arrows at
me, king, like snakes with blazing mouths. I cut them down suddenly in the air
with sharp broad-headed arrows by the hundred and the thousand, king, again and
again. Then mighty Jamadagni's son launched heavenly weapons at me, and I warded
them off with weapons, strong-armed one, wanting to do the greater deed. Then a
great noise arose in the sky on every side. I used the wind weapon against
Jamadagni's son, and Rama struck it back with the weapon of the guhyakas,
Bharata. Then I charged the fire weapon with its mantra and used it, and the
lord Rama warded it off with Varuna's weapon. So I warded off Rama's heavenly
weapons, and splendid Rama, tamer of enemies, who knew the heavenly weapons,
warded off mine.

"'Then, king, the best of the twice-born, mighty Jamadagni's son, keeping me on
his left, pierced me in the chest in his fury. I sank down on my fine chariot,
best of the Bharatas. When I was overcome by faintness, struck by Rama's arrow,
my charioteer quickly drove me off the distance of a cow's call, best of the
Bharatas. When they saw me driven off, badly wounded and senseless, all Rama's
followers, led by Akritavrana, and the daughter of Kashi, shouted for joy,
Bharata.

"'When I came to myself, I knew it and said to my charioteer, "Drive to where
Rama is, charioteer. I am ready, and the pain has gone." Then the charioteer
drove me with those splendid horses, swift as the wind, that seemed to dance,
Kauravya. Reaching Rama, Kaurava, I covered him in my fury with a net of arrows,
wanting to beat him in his fury. As they came, Rama cut each of my
straight-flying arrows with three arrows of his own in the battle. All my
well-sharpened arrows were crushed, cut in two by Rama's arrows, by the hundred,
in the great battle.

"'Then I loosed another bright, shining arrow like Time at Rama, Jamadagni's
son, meaning to kill him. Struck hard by it, he fell under the arrow's power,
fainted at once and fell to the ground. When Rama was lying on the ground, the
whole world cried out in alarm, Bharata, as if the sun had fallen. Then all the
ascetics, deeply alarmed, ran to the son of Bhrigu, and the daughter of Kashi
too. They embraced him and slowly comforted him, Kauravya, with hands cool with
water and with blessings of victory. Then Rama, shaken, rose and said to me,
setting an arrow to his bow, "Stand, Bhishma. You are dead." It was loosed and
fell quickly on my left side in the great battle, and I was so shaken by it that
I swayed like a tree.

"'Then, king, he killed my horses with a swift weapon in the great battle and
confidently covered me with those feathered arrows. Then I too loosed a swift
weapon that could not be warded off in the battle, strong-armed one, and those
arrows, Rama's and mine, hung between us, quickly covering the sky on every
side. The sun gave no heat, covered by the net of arrows, and the wind in that
space howled as if blocked by clouds. Then from the shaking of the wind, the
rays of the sun, the heat and their own nature, fire was born. All those arrows,
set ablaze by the fire they had made themselves, fell to the ground as ashes,
king. Then Rama in his fury quickly rained on me arrows in hundreds of
thousands, millions, tens of millions, ten thousands, billions and tens of
billions, Kauravya. In the battle I cut them down too with arrows like poisonous
snakes and made them fall to the ground like serpents, king. So that battle went
on, best of the Bharatas. When the twilight had passed, my teacher withdrew.

---

"'When I met Rama again the next day, the battle was very terrible and
tumultuous, best of the Bharatas. The hero, who knew heavenly weapons, the lord
whose soul was dharma, used many heavenly weapons day after day. I destroyed
those weapons in the tumultuous battle with weapons that struck them back,
Bharata, giving up the life that is so hard to give up. When his weapons had
been destroyed in many ways by my weapons, the Bhargava of great energy grew
angry, ready to give up his life in the battle.

"'Then great Jamadagni's son, blocked by weapons, hurled a spear of terrible
form, like a blazing meteor sent by Time, its point aflame, filling the worlds
with splendour. With blazing arrows I cut it in three as it came, blazing like
the sun at the end of time, and brought it down to the ground. Then a holy,
fragrant wind blew. When it was cut, Rama, blazing with anger, hurled twelve
more terrible spears. Their form cannot be described, Bharata, for their
splendour and speed. I was shaken when I saw them, like great meteors of fire
from every quarter, of many forms, blazing with fierce splendour, like the
twelve suns at the destruction of the world. Then I spread a net of arrows,
looked, broke through with my net of arrows, king, and loosed twelve arrows in
the battle, and so I destroyed those terrible spears.

"'Then great Jamadagni's son threw other terrible spears with golden shafts,
bright, bound with golden bands, blazing like great meteors. I warded off those
fierce ones with my shield and brought them down with my sword in the battle,
lord of men. With heavenly arrows I rained on Jamadagni's son's heavenly horses
and his charioteer in the battle. When he saw the gold- bright spears cut down,
like snakes that have shed their skins, the great man who had crushed the lord
of the Haihayas, filled with anger, brought forth a heavenly weapon. Then lines
of blazing arrows fell, fierce as swarms of locusts, and covered my body, my
horses, my charioteer and my chariot thickly. My chariot was covered with arrows
on every side, king, and so were my horses and my charioteer. The yoke and the
pole too, and the axle, were cut by arrows and broken.

"'When that rain of arrows had passed, I rained a flood of arrows on my teacher
in return. Wounded by the arrows, that heap of Brahman poured a great deal of
blood from his body without stopping. As Rama was burned by the net of arrows,
so I was very deeply pierced. Then the battle stopped in the afternoon, as the
sun sought the mountain of setting.'"
