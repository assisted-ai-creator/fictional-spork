# The Pandavas' Counsel

When Sanjaya had gone back, Yudhishthira the Dharma King spoke to Krishna, the
Dasharha, the bull among all the Satvatas.

"This is the time for friends to show themselves, Janardana," he said. "I see no
one but you who can carry us through our troubles. It is because we rely on you,
Madhava, that we have been bold enough to ask the proud, deluded son of
Dhritarashtra and his ministers for our share. You protect the Vrishnis in all
their troubles, tamer of enemies. Protect the Pandavas in the same way. Save us
from a great danger."

"Here I am, strong-armed one," said the Lord. "Tell me what you want to say. I
will do all of it, whatever you tell me, Bharata."

"You have heard what Dhritarashtra and his son mean to do," said Yudhishthira.
"All that Sanjaya told me is Dhritarashtra's own view, Krishna. Sanjaya is his
other self, and he laid bare his mind. An envoy speaks as he has been told. If
he said anything else he would deserve death.

"Dhritarashtra is looking for peace with us without giving back the kingdom. He
is greedy and his mind is wicked, and he does not treat us as he treats his own.
We lived twelve years in the forest, and one year in disguise, because
Dhritarashtra ordered it. We thought he would hold to that agreement, my lord.
We did not break it, Krishna, and the brahmins know that we did not. The old
king does not see his own dharma. Or he sees it, and follows his foolish son's
orders because he is greedy for his son. He takes Suyodhana's side against us,
Janardana, and he is false to us. He is greedy, and he acts as if he were doing
himself good.

"What could be more painful than this, that I cannot provide for my mother or
for my friends? With the Kashis, the Chedis and Panchalas and the Matsyas, and
with you as my protector, slayer of Madhu, I asked for five villages. I asked
for Kushasthala, Vrikasthala, Asandi and Varanavata, and any other as the fifth,
Govinda. Let us be given five villages or towns, sir, where we can live
together, so that the Bharatas are not destroyed. But the wicked son of
Dhritarashtra will not agree even to that. He thinks it all belongs to him. What
could be more painful than that?

"When a man of good family, grown old, is greedy for what belongs to others, the
greed destroys his understanding. When his understanding is destroyed, it
destroys his shame. When shame is destroyed, it harms his dharma. When dharma is
destroyed, it destroys his fortune. When fortune is destroyed, it destroys the
man, for a man who has nothing is as good as dead. Kinsmen, friends and priests
turn away from a man who has nothing, as birds leave a tree that has no flowers
or fruit, my friend. And this too is a kind of death, friend, when kinsmen turn
away from me as if I were an outcast, as the breath leaves a dead body. Shambara
said there is no state worse than one where no meal can be seen, either today or
tomorrow.

"They say wealth is the highest dharma. Everything rests on wealth. The rich are
alive in this world, and men without wealth are dead. Those who rely on their
own strength and drag a man away from his wealth destroy his dharma, his wealth,
his pleasures and the man himself. Some people who come to this state choose
death. Some go off to a village, some to the forest, and some go away to die.
Some turn mad. Others fall into the power of their enemies. Some become slaves
to others for the sake of wealth.

"Misfortune is heavier for a man than death, for his fortune is the root of his
dharma and his pleasures. The death that dharma brings is the everlasting road
of the world. It surrounds all beings, and no one passes beyond it. A man who
has been poor from birth does not suffer so much, Krishna, as a man who has
grown up in comfort, won good fortune and lost it. When he falls into great
trouble through his own fault, he blames the gods, Indra and all, and never
himself. Not all the sciences together can ease his blame. He grows angry with
his servants and turns sour against his friends. Then anger overtakes him, and
he grows more and more confused. Once confusion has hold of him, he does cruel
things. Evil deeds bring the mixing of castes, and the mixing of castes leads to
hell. That is the end of those who do evil.

"If he does not wake up, Krishna, he goes to hell. What wakes him is wisdom
alone. The man with the eye of wisdom is not lost. When a man gains wisdom, he
looks to the sciences. When he keeps to the sciences, he keeps to dharma, and
shame is its finest part. The man with shame hates evil, and his fortune grows.
As long as he has fortune, he is a man. A man who keeps always to dharma, calm,
and always carrying his work through, does not set his mind on what is against
dharma, and does no evil. A man without shame, or a deluded man, is neither
woman nor man. He has no part in dharma. He is like a shudra. The man with shame
protects the gods, the ancestors and himself, and so he goes to the deathless
state. That is the goal of those who do good.

"You have seen all this with your own eyes in me, slayer of Madhu. You have seen
how I have been thrown out of my kingdom and live in these lodgings. We cannot
give up our fortune by any right means. If we are killed while we strive for it,
even that is good.

"In this, Madhava, our first plan is that we and they should share our fortune,
at peace and on equal terms. The last resort is terrible, full of cruel deeds
and killing. It is that we kill the Kauravas and take those kingdoms for
ourselves. Even enemies who are no kin to us and are base should not be killed,
Krishna. How much less men like these? Most of them are our kinsmen, our allies
and our elders. To kill them would be the worst of sins. What good can there be
in war?

"This kshatriya dharma is evil, and we are born kshatriyas. It is our own
dharma, or against dharma, but any other way of life would be despised. The
shudra serves, the vaishyas live by trade, and we live by killing. The brahmins
have chosen the begging bowl. Kshatriya kills kshatriya, fish lives on fish, and
dog kills dog, Dasharha. See how this dharma has come down to us.

"In war there is always strife, Krishna. Lives are lost in battle. Strength
serves only to carry out a plan, and victory and defeat come by chance. Beings
do not choose when they live or die. No one can gain happiness before its time,
best of the Yadus, nor sorrow either. One man kills many, and many kill one. The
coward kills the hero, and the man of no name kills the famous man. Both sides
are seen to win, and both to lose. And in running away there is loss and ruin.

"War is evil in every way. Who kills and is not struck down in turn? For the man
who is killed, Hrishikesha, victory and defeat are the same. I think defeat is
no better than death. And the man who wins, Krishna, surely suffers loss as
well. In the end others kill someone he loves. My friend, when he has lost his
strength and no longer sees his sons and brothers, he grows sick of life,
Krishna, on every side. It is the brave who are killed in war, the modest, the
noble and the kind. The lesser people escape.

"Even when a man has killed his enemies, Janardana, regret stays with him
always. An evil chain of consequences follows, and someone is left alive. The
one who is left gathers strength, and does not leave a single survivor on the
other side. He tries to wipe them all out, to bring the feud to an end. Victory
breeds hatred, and the loser lives in misery. The man at peace sleeps happily,
having given up victory and defeat alike. But the man in a feud always sleeps
badly, with an uneasy mind, as if there were a snake in the house.

"The man who wipes out everyone loses his good name. He wins lasting shame among
all beings. Feuds do not die down, even after a long time. There are always
those who will tell the story, and a man is born into the family. Hatred is not
calmed by hatred, Keshava. It grows even more, Krishna, like a fire fed with
butter. So there is no peace any other way. In the end there is always an
opening, and for those who look for an opening this fault never ends.

"The pride of a man is a strong sickness that torments the heart. Peace comes by
giving it up, or by turning the mind away from it. Or else, slayer of Madhu,
there might be rich fruit from destroying one's enemies root and all, but that
would be crueller still. The only peace apart from giving up is killing, with
all its danger, and the ruin of our enemies and of ourselves too. We do not want
to give it up, and we do not want the family destroyed. So the peace that comes
by bowing down is the best.

"When men have striven in every way and want no war, and conciliation is
rejected, war is certain. Then comes the time for courage. When conciliation is
rejected, something terrible begins. Wise men have seen it in dogs. There is the
wagging of the tail, the snarl, the answering bark, the circling, the baring of
teeth and the howl, and then the fight. Then the stronger one wins, Krishna, and
eats the meat. It is just the same among men. There is no difference at all. It
is always the way of the strong to scorn the weak and oppose them, for it is the
weak who bows down.

"A father, a king and an old man deserve honour in every way. So Dhritarashtra
deserves our honour and respect, Janardana. But Dhritarashtra's love for his son
is strong, Madhava. He is in his son's power, and he will reject our submission.
What do you think is right to do now, Krishna? How can we keep both our wealth
and our dharma, Madhava? Whom else could I ask in such a difficult matter,
slayer of Madhu, but you, best of men? Who else is there like you, Krishna, who
loves us and wants our good, who knows how all things turn out and knows all
certainty, and is our friend?"

When he had said this, Janardana answered the Dharma King. "I will go to the
Kuru court for the sake of both sides. If I can win peace there without giving
up anything of yours, I shall have done a great and holy deed, king, and it will
bear great fruit. I shall free the angry Kurus and Srinjayas from the noose of
death, the Pandavas and the sons of Dhritarashtra, and this whole earth."

"I do not think you should go to the Kurus, Krishna," said Yudhishthira.
"Suyodhana will not do what you say, however well you say it. The kshatriyas of
the earth are gathered there, and they follow Suyodhana's lead. I do not like
you going into the middle of them, Krishna. If harm came to you, Madhava,
nothing could please us, not wealth, not godhood, let alone happiness, not even
lordship over all the gods."

"I know the wickedness of Dhritarashtra's son, great king," said the Lord. "But
if we do not go, all the kings in the world will blame us. And all the kings of
the earth together could not stand before me when I am angry, any more than
other beasts can stand before a lion. If they were to do anything wrong to me, I
would burn up all the Kurus. That is my mind. My going there will never be
wasted, son of Pritha. We may gain what we want, or at least we shall be free of
blame."

"Whatever you think best, Krishna," said Yudhishthira. "Go safely to the
Kauravas. I shall see you back again, safe, and with your purpose done. Go to
the Kurus, Vishvaksena, my lord, and calm the Bharatas, so that we can all live
together with glad hearts and good minds. You are a brother and a friend to
Bibhatsu, and dear to me. We cannot doubt you, you are so close to us. Go
safely, for our good. You know us, you know the others, you know what we want,
and you know what should be said. Say to Suyodhana whatever is good for us,
Krishna. Whatever is in keeping with dharma and does us good, speak it, Keshava,
gentle words or otherwise."

"I have heard Sanjaya's words," said the Lord, "and I have heard yours. I know
all that is in their minds and in yours. Your mind rests on dharma. Theirs rests
on hatred. What can be had without war you would value highly. But that is not
the kshatriya's proper work, lord of the people. All who live by the orders of
life say that a kshatriya should not go begging. Victory or death in battle is
the eternal law the Creator laid down. That is the kshatriya's own dharma, and
weakness is not praised. A man cannot live by weakness, Yudhishthira. Show your
courage, strong-armed one. Kill your enemies, tamer of enemies.

"The sons of Dhritarashtra are very greedy, scorcher of enemies. They have lived
together for a long time and grown close. They have made friends, and they have
built up their strength. There is no way that they will treat you as an equal,
lord of the people. They think they are strong, with Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and
the rest. As long as you treat them gently, king, they will keep your kingdom
from you. Not out of pity, nor out of weakness, nor for the sake of dharma or
gain, will the sons of Dhritarashtra do what you want. Here is the proof of it,
son of Pandu. They put you in a loincloth, cruel as that was, and they did not
regret it.

"Bhishma, Drona, wise Vidura and all the leading Kurus watched, and saw what it
was, when he cheated you with a trick at the dice, king. You are generous,
gentle and self-controlled, you love dharma and you keep your vows. And the
wicked, cruel man is not ashamed of what he did. Do not show any kindness to a
man who behaves like that, king. People like that deserve death from anyone,
Bharata, let alone from you.

"He hurt you and your younger brothers with shameful words. He boasted, and he
was delighted, and he said to his brothers, 'The Pandavas have nothing of their
own left here. Even their name and their lineage will not remain. In a long
while their ruin will come. People will turn back to their natural state once
their masters are gone.' He said those harsh words and others like them, and he
boasted among his kinsmen when you had gone into the forest.

"The people who had been brought there saw that you were innocent. They sat in
the hall with tears in their throats, weeping. The kings and the brahmins did
not cheer him. All who sat in the hall blamed Duryodhana. For a man of good
family, blame and death stand side by side, tamer of enemies, and death is the
better. Death has great merit, king, but blame is a wretched way to live. He was
killed the moment he felt no shame, great king, when all the kings of the earth
blamed him. It is a small thing to kill a man who behaves like that. He is like
a tree with its roots cut, held up only by a prop. The evil-minded wretch
deserves death from everyone, like a snake. Kill him, killer of enemies. Do not
hesitate, king.

"All the same, blameless one, it suits you and pleases me that you should bow to
your father and to Bhishma. I shall go and clear up the doubts of all the world,
king, of all those who are still in two minds about Duryodhana. There, among the
kings, I shall tell your virtues, which no other man has, and his crimes. I
shall speak words that are good, and in keeping with dharma and gain. All the
kings who rule the many lands will hear them, and they will come over to you, as
a man of dharma and a speaker of truth. And they will see how greed has driven
him. I shall blame him before the townspeople and the country folk too, old and
young, when the four classes are gathered together. If you ask for peace, no one
will say you have done wrong. But the kings will blame the Kurus and
Dhritarashtra. When the world has abandoned him, what is left to do? Once
Duryodhana is dead, king, let what remains be done.

"I shall go to all the Kurus, and I shall try to make peace without giving up
anything of yours. I shall watch what they do. I shall learn what the Kauravas
are doing to get ready for war, and I shall come back, Bharata, for your
victory. In every way I expect war with the enemy, for all the omens that show
themselves to me point to it. Beasts and birds cry out terribly at nightfall,
and among the best of the elephants and the horses. The fire takes on terrible
shapes and many terrible colours. This dreadful thing, the ruin of the world of
men, could not be happening unless Death himself had come here.

"Make ready your weapons, your arrows, your armour and your chariots, your
elephants and your banners. Let all your warriors be trained and ready on their
elephants, horses and chariots. Whatever you need for war, lord of men, get it
all together. For Duryodhana will never, while he lives, be willing to give you
this kingdom, chief of the Pandavas. It was rich and yours before, and it was
taken at the dice."

**Divergences.**

* **Janamejaya's question.** Ganguli opens with Janamejaya asking what the Pandavas did when Sanjaya had gone. This is \*366, rejected. Not narrated.
* **The council before Krishna.** A rejected passage (\*367) has Yudhishthira gather Arjuna, Bhima, the twins, Virata, Drupada and the Kekaya and go to Krishna together, to ask him to go to the Kuru court so that they need not fight Bhishma and Drona. In the CE Yudhishthira simply speaks to Krishna. Not narrated, and nor are \*368–\*372.
* **The five villages.** Ganguli names Avishthala and Makandi; the CE has Kushasthala and Asandi (5.70.15). See ledger L-174.
* **Draupadi dragged by the hair.** In Krishna's speech the rejected line \*374 adds Duhshasana dragging Draupadi by the hair and calling her "cow", and the brothers held back by Yudhishthira. Ganguli has it. Not narrated. Also not narrated: \*373, which adds the brahmins, the king and the city to the onlookers at the dice.
