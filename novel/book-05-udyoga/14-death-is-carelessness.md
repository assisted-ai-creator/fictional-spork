# Death Is Carelessness

"When good men ask a man for something," said Vidura, "and he does it without
holding back, using all his strength, fame soon comes to that good man. When
good men are pleased, they bring happiness. The man who gives up a great gain
joined with adharma, before anyone cries out against it, sleeps happily, free of
sorrows, like a snake that has slipped its worn-out skin.

"Lying to get ahead, slandering to the king, and pressing a false charge on a
teacher are equal to killing a brahmin. Envy is the next step to death. Too much
talk kills fortune. Not listening, haste and boasting are the three enemies of
learning. Where is learning for the man who wants comfort? There is no comfort
for the man who wants learning. Let the one who wants comfort give up learning,
or the one who wants learning give up comfort.

"Fire is not filled by firewood, nor the ocean by rivers, nor Death by all
creatures, nor a beautiful woman by men. Hope kills steadiness. Death kills
plenty. Anger kills fortune. Meanness kills fame. Neglect kills cattle, king.
One angry brahmin kills a kingdom.

"Let these always be in your house: a goat, bronze, a chariot, honey, an
attracting charm, a bird, a brahmin learned in the Veda, an old kinsman and a
friend fallen on hard times. A goat, a bull, sandalwood, a vina, a mirror, honey
and ghee, poison, a vessel of udumbara wood, a conch, gold, musk and yellow
pigment: Manu said these lucky things should be kept in the house, Bharata, for
the worship of the gods and brahmins, and for guests.

"And this, my son, I tell you as the highest of all, a holy and very great
truth. Never give up dharma, for desire, for fear, for greed, or even for the
sake of life. Dharma lasts for ever. Happiness and sorrow do not. The living
self lasts for ever. Its cause does not. Give up what does not last, and stand
on what lasts. Be content. Contentment is the greatest gain.

"Look at the kings of great strength and great glory who ruled the earth full of
wealth and grain. They left their kingdoms and their great pleasures and went
into the power of Death. People carry out of their house a dead son they raised
with pain, king. With their hair unbound, weeping pitifully, they throw him on
the pyre like a piece of wood. Another enjoys the wealth of the dead man. The
birds and the fire enjoy the elements of his body. He goes to the next world
with two things only, wrapped in his merit and his sin. Kinsmen, friends and
sons leave him and turn back. But the deeds he did himself follow the man thrown
into the fire.

"Above this world, and below that one, lies a great darkness without light. That
is the great deluder of the senses. Understand it, king, and do not let it seize
you. If you can hear all these words and take them in properly, you will win
great fame in the world of the living. You will have no fear, here or hereafter.

"The self is a river, Bharata, with holy fords. Its water is truth, its banks
are steadiness, and its waves are self-control. The man of good deeds who bathes
in it is purified. The self is always holy. Water is only water. Make a boat of
steadiness and cross the hard places of birth, on the river whose water is the
five senses and whose crocodiles are desire and anger. The man who honours and
wins over a kinsman great in wisdom, great in dharma, great in learning and
great in years, and asks him what to do and what not to do, is never confused.

"With steadiness a man should guard his sex and his belly. With his eyes he
should guard his hands and feet. With his mind he should guard his eyes and
ears, and with his deeds his mind and speech.

"A brahmin who always touches water, always wears the sacred thread, always
recites the Veda, avoids the food of outcastes, speaks the truth and serves his
teacher does not fall from the world of Brahma. A kshatriya who has studied the
Vedas, laid out the fires, worshipped with sacrifices and protected his people,
whose inner self is purified by weapons, and who is killed in battle for the
sake of cows and brahmins, goes to heaven. A vaishya who has studied, and in
good time shared his wealth with brahmins, kshatriyas and his dependants, smells
the holy smoke purified by the three fires and enjoys the pleasures of the gods
in heaven after death. A shudra who honours brahmins, kshatriyas and vaishyas
properly, in turn, is untroubled when they are pleased. His sins burnt away, he
leaves his body and enjoys the pleasures of heaven.

"I have told you the dharma of the four classes. Now hear why I tell it. The son
of Pandu is falling away from the dharma of a kshatriya. Set him to the duties
of a king, king."

"It is as you always teach me," said Dhritarashtra. "My mind too is as you say,
gentle one. But though I make up my mind like this about the Pandavas, it always
turns again when I meet Duryodhana. No mortal can pass beyond fate. I think fate
alone decides. Human effort is useless."

---

"If there is anything you have not said, Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "tell it
to me. I want to hear it. You speak wonderfully."

"Dhritarashtra," said Vidura, "the ancient, eternal youth, Sanatsujata, said
that there is no death, Bharata. He is the best of all the wise. He will tell
you all the secret things and the open ones that lie in your heart, great king."

"Do you not know what that eternal one would tell me?" said Dhritarashtra. "Tell
me yourself, Vidura, if you have any wisdom left."

"I was born of a shudra woman," said Vidura. "I dare not say more than I have.
But I know the youth's understanding to be eternal. One who has come to a
brahmin's birth may speak even of the deepest secret and not be blamed by the
gods. That is why I tell you this."

"Tell me, Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "how I may meet that ancient, eternal one
here, in this body."

Vidura called to mind that seer of strict vows. Knowing that he had been called
to mind, the seer showed himself, Bharata. Vidura received him with the rites
the rule lays down. When he was seated comfortably and rested, Vidura spoke to
him.

"Lord, there is a doubt in Dhritarashtra's mind that I cannot speak to. You
should speak to it for him. When he hears it, this king of men may pass beyond
happiness and sorrow. Then gain and loss, the loved and the hated, old age and
death, fear and impatience, hunger and thirst, pride and its rising, discontent,
sloth, desire and anger, decline and growth, may not overcome him."

---

Then wise King Dhritarashtra honoured the words Vidura had spoken. Wanting the
highest understanding, the great man questioned Sanatsujata in private.

"Sanatsujata," said Dhritarashtra, "I hear that your teaching is that there is
no death. Yet gods and asuras lived the celibate life so as not to die. Which of
these is true?"

"Some say there is freedom from death through deeds," said Sanatsujata, "and
others say there is no death. Listen to me, king, as I explain this, and do not
doubt it. Both are true, kshatriya, from the first. The wise hold that death is
delusion. I say that carelessness is death, and that being always careful is
deathlessness. Through carelessness the asuras were defeated. Through care, men
become one with Brahman. Death does not eat creatures like a tiger. Its form is
never seen.

"Some say Yama is death, something other than this. But the celibate life, lived
in the self, is deathless. That god rules the kingdom of the ancestors, kind to
the kind and unkind to the unkind. Death comes out of men's own mouths as anger,
carelessness and delusion. Deluded by these, living under their power, men die
out of this world and fall again there. Then the gods follow after them. And so
death takes the name of dying. Clinging to the fruits of their deeds as the
deeds rise, they follow them and do not cross over death.

"The man who meditates on objects and does not understand, and kills the rising
desires by disregard, becomes death to them, and eats them as death eats. The
wise man who strikes down desires does this. The man who runs after desires
perishes with them. The man who throws desires aside shakes off whatever dust
there is. This hell is seen to be the darkness of beings, without light. They
run as if seized, falling headlong into a pit.

"First longing kills a man. Then desire and anger seize him. These bring fools
to death. But the steady cross death by their steadiness. To the man who thinks
of nothing else, kshatriya, death is no more than a tiger made of straw. The one
inside whose self is made of delusion, out of anger and greed, that is death,
here in your body. Knowing that death is born in this way, and standing in
knowledge, a man has no fear of death here. When death comes into his power, it
perishes, as a mortal perishes when he falls into the power of death."

"Some here do not practise dharma," said Dhritarashtra, "and some here do. Is
dharma struck down by sin, or does dharma strike down sin?"

"Both bear their fruit," said Sanatsujata, "dharma and the other. But here the
wise man drives out adharma with dharma. Know that dharma is the stronger."

"They speak of the eternal worlds of the twice-born who do good," said
Dhritarashtra, "the worlds of their own dharma. They tell of the ways to them,
and of other worlds. Learned one, I do not know this, nor have I done the deed."

"Those who do not compete in strength," said Sanatsujata, "as the strong compete
in strength, those brahmins shine in the world of heaven after they leave this
one. Where a brahmin thinks food and drink are plentiful, like grass in the
rainy season, he should live there and not fret about it. Where fear and harm
come to him though he says nothing, as if he were doing too much, he is better,
and not the other man. The food of the man who does not fret about himself
though he says nothing, and who does not enjoy what belongs to brahmins, is
approved by the good. As a dog always eats its own vomit, to its harm, so those
who live by their own power eat their own vomit.

"A brahmin should think, 'My life must always be hidden.' Living among his kin,
he should be known for nothing. What brahmin would want to kill the self within
like this? So the kshatriya who lives like this sees Brahman. He should be
untiring, take nothing, be honoured and untroubled. Though he is trained, he
should not act as though he were. That brahmin knows Brahman and is a seer.
Those twice-born who are poor in human wealth and rich in the Vedas are hard to
overcome and hard to shake. Know them to be the body of Brahman.

"Whoever here knows all the gods who do the sacrifice well is not equal to a
brahmin in what he himself strives for. The man who is honoured without striving
for it is truly honoured. When he is honoured he should not think much of it,
nor fret when he is not. He should think, when he is honoured, 'The learned
honour men here.' When he is not honoured, he should think, 'Fools who do not
know dharma, though skilled in worldly learning, will not honour one who
deserves it.'

"Honour and silence never go together. This world is for honour, and the other
for silence. Fortune is the home of comfort here, and it is also the highwayman.
The fortune of Brahman is very hard to win, kshatriya, for a man without wisdom.
The good say its doors are many and hard to guard: truth, straightness, shame,
self-control, purity and learning. These six keep out pride and delusion."
