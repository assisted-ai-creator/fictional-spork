# The Sleepless King

Wise Dhritarashtra, lord of the earth, spoke to the doorkeeper. "I want to see
Vidura. Bring him here, and don't be long."

Sent by Dhritarashtra, the messenger said to the steward, "The lord, the great
king, wants to see you, wise one."

When he was told this, Vidura came to the king's house and said, "Doorkeeper,
tell Dhritarashtra I am here."

"Vidura has come at your command, king of kings," said the doorkeeper. "He wants
to see your feet. Command me. What should he do?"

"Bring in wise, far-seeing Vidura," said Dhritarashtra. "I am never too busy to
see Vidura."

"Go into the inner rooms of the wise great king, steward," said the doorkeeper.
"The king tells me he is never too busy to see you."

Then Vidura went into Dhritarashtra's house. With his hands joined, he spoke to
the king, who sat brooding. "It is Vidura, wise one. I have come at your
command. If there is anything to be done, here I am. Command me."

"Sanjaya has come, Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "and he has gone away after
reproaching me. Tomorrow he will give Ajatashatru's message in the middle of the
hall. I have not learned today what the Kuru hero said. It burns my limbs. It
has kept me awake. Tell me what you see is best for a man who lies awake and
burning, my son, for you are skilled in dharma and in what is useful. Since
Sanjaya came back from the Pandavas, my mind has had no proper peace. All my
senses have left their natural state. All I can think of today is, 'What will he
say?'"

"Sleeplessness comes," said Vidura, "to a weak man without resources who has
been attacked by a strong one, to a man who has lost his property, to a lover
and to a thief. I hope you have not been touched by these great faults, king. I
hope you are not tormented by greed for other people's wealth."

"I want to hear your words of dharma," said Dhritarashtra, "for my highest good.
In this line of royal seers you alone are counted wise."

---

"A man who does what is praised and does not do what is blamed," said Vidura,
"who believes and has faith, has the mark of a wise man. A man whom anger and
delight, pride and shame, stubbornness and self-regard do not pull away from his
purpose is called wise. A man whose plans and counsels others do not know, who
know only what he has done, is called wise. A man whose work is not hindered by
cold or heat, by fear or love, by plenty or by want, is called wise. A man whose
understanding moves along with dharma and purpose, who chooses purpose over
pleasure, is called wise. The wise want to do what they can, and do what they
can, bull of the Bharatas. They despise nothing.

"He understands quickly and listens long. When he understands a thing, he acts
on it, not out of desire. He does not meddle in others' affairs unasked. That is
the first sign of a wise man. Men of wise mind do not long for what cannot be
had, and do not wish to grieve over what is lost. They are not confused in
trouble. The man who makes up his mind before he starts, who does not stop
halfway through his work, who does not waste his time and is master of himself,
is called wise. The wise delight in noble work, bull of the Bharatas, and do
what brings prosperity. They do not sneer at what is good for them. The man who
is not elated by honour or pained by insult, who cannot be stirred, like a pool
of the Ganga, is called wise. The man who knows the truth of all beings, how all
actions are done, and how to deal with men, is called wise.

"The man whose speech flows, who talks well and variously, who can reason, who
is quick-witted and can explain a text at once, is called wise. The man whose
learning follows his wisdom and whose wisdom follows his learning, who does not
break the bounds of the noble, has earned the name of wise.

"The wise call a man a fool who is proud without learning, haughty in poverty,
and wants wealth without work. The man who gives up his own business to do
another's, and acts falsely for his friends' sake, is called a fool. They call a
man foolish who wants what does not want him, hates those who want him, and
hates a stronger man. They call a man foolish who makes his enemy his friend,
hates and harms his friend, and starts on evil work. The man who drags out his
tasks, doubts everything, and is slow over what should be quick is a fool, bull
of the Bharatas. A man of foolish mind, the lowest of men, goes in uninvited,
talks a great deal unasked, and trusts the untrustworthy. The man who blames
another for a fault he has himself, and the man who gets angry when he has no
power, is the greatest fool. The man who does not know his own strength, who
gives up dharma and purpose, and wants what cannot be had without doing
anything, is called foolish here. They call a man foolish, king, who teaches the
unteachable, serves an empty man, and attaches himself to a miser.

"The man who wins great wealth, or learning, or power, and goes about without
pride, is called wise.

---

"Who is crueller than the man who eats fine food alone, and wears fine clothes,
and does not share with those who depend on him? One man does evil, and many
enjoy the fruit. Those who enjoy it go free, and the doer is stained by the
fault. An arrow shot by a bowman may kill one man, or none. But the wit let
loose by a clever man can kill a kingdom and its king.

"With one, settle two. Bring three under your control with four. Conquer five,
know six, give up seven, and be happy.

"Poison kills one man. A weapon kills one. But a leaked counsel kills a king,
with his kingdom and his people. A man should not eat a sweet thing alone, nor
think out his affairs alone, nor go on a journey alone, nor stay awake alone
among sleepers.

"That one thing without a second, king, which you do not understand, is truth.
It is the stair to heaven, like a boat across the sea. There is one fault in the
patient, and no second is found: people think a patient man is weak. Dharma
alone is the highest good. Patience alone is the best peace. Knowledge alone is
the highest sight. Not harming alone brings happiness.

"The earth swallows these two, as a snake swallows creatures living in holes: a
king who does not fight, and a brahmin who does not travel. A man shines in this
world by doing two things: speaking no harsh word, and not courting the wicked.
These two, tiger among men, act on others' judgment: women who desire whoever is
desired, and people who honour whoever is honoured. These two are sharp thorns
that waste the body: a poor man who desires, and a powerless man who is angry.
These two stand above heaven, king: a lord who is patient, and a poor man who
gives. Two misuses of honestly gained wealth should be known: giving to the
unworthy, and not giving to the worthy.

"Three courses are spoken of among men, bull of the Bharatas: the lowest, the
middle and the best, as those who know the Veda know. There are three kinds of
men, king, the best, the worst and the middling. They should be set to three
kinds of work, each as fits. Three have no wealth of their own, king, a wife, a
slave and a son. Whatever they earn belongs to the man they belong to.

"The wise say a mighty king should avoid four things, and a wise man should know
them. He should not take counsel with men of little wisdom, with those who drag
things out, with the lazy, or with flatterers. Let four live in your house, my
son, when fortune favours you as a householder: an old kinsman, a man of good
family fallen on hard times, a poor friend, and a sister without children.
Brihaspati, asked by the lord of the thirty gods, named four things that act at
once, great king. Hear them from me: the resolve of the gods, the power of the
wise, the humility of the learned, and the ruin of evildoers.

"A man should tend five fires with care, bull of the Bharatas: father, mother,
fire, himself and his teacher. By honouring five a man wins fame in the world:
the gods, the ancestors, men, mendicants, and guests as the fifth. Five will
follow you wherever you go: friends, enemies, those in the middle, those you
depend on, and those who depend on you. A mortal has five senses. If one sense
leaks, his wisdom runs out through it like water from the foot of a leather bag.

"A man who wants to prosper here should give up six faults: sleep, sloth, fear,
anger, idleness and procrastination. A man should give up these six, like a
broken boat at sea: a teacher who does not teach, a priest who has not studied,
a king who does not protect, a wife who speaks unkindly, a cowherd who wants to
live in the village, and a barber who wants to live in the forest. A man should
never give up six virtues: truth, giving, energy, freedom from envy, patience
and steadiness. The man who masters the six that are always inside him, and so
conquers his senses, is not touched by sins, let alone by misfortunes. These six
live on six, and no seventh is found: thieves live on the careless, doctors on
the sick, women on lovers, priests on those who sacrifice, the king on those who
quarrel, and the wise always on fools.

"A king should always give up seven faults that bring ruin, for by these kings
are mostly destroyed, even when they are firmly rooted: women, dice, hunting,
drink, harsh speech as the fifth, great harshness in punishment, and the misuse
of wealth.

"There are eight early signs of a man who is going to perish. First he hates
brahmins, and quarrels with brahmins. He takes brahmins' property, and wants to
kill brahmins. He enjoys hearing them blamed, and is not glad when they are
praised. He does not remember them at the rites, and he is resentful when they
ask. A wise man should see these faults with his understanding and avoid them.
There are eight things, Bharata, that are the fresh butter of joy. They are seen
in the present, and they are the greatest happiness: meeting friends, the coming
of great wealth, a son's embrace, union in love, kind words at the right time,
standing high among one's own people, getting what one wants, and honour in the
assembly.

"This house has nine doors, three pillars and five witnesses, and the knower of
the field lives in it. The learned man who knows it is the highest seer.

"Ten do not know dharma, Dhritarashtra. Hear them from me: the drunk, the
careless, the mad, the tired, the angry, the hungry, the hasty, the coward, the
greedy and the lustful. Those are the ten. So a wise man should not get caught
up in these states.

---

"On this they tell an old story, sung by the lord of the asuras and by
Sudhanvan, about a son.

"The king who gives up desire and anger, who sets wealth on worthy men, who
knows distinctions, is learned and quick to act, is taken by all the world as
its measure. The man who knows how to win men's trust, who gives punishment
where the fault is known, who knows measure and also patience, is the one whom
fortune favours whole. The steady man despises no one, however weak. He deals
with his enemy carefully and with thought. He does not choose war with the
strong, and he shows courage at the right time. The man who never trembles when
trouble comes, but looks to his efforts with care, who masters himself and bears
sorrow in its time, bears the yoke, and his rivals are beaten.

"The man who does not leave home for no purpose, does not make peace with
evildoers, does not touch other men's wives, and does not practise deceit,
theft, slander or drink, is always happy. He does not start on his affairs in a
temper. When asked, he tells only the truth. He does not like a quarrel over a
trifle. He is no fool, and he does not get angry when he is not honoured. The
man who is not envious and who is kind, who does not stand surety when he is
weak, who does not say too much and bears with a quarrel, is praised everywhere.

"The man who never dresses to show off, who does not boast to others of his
courage, who does not speak bitter words even when he is roused, is always liked
by people. He does not stir up enmity that has died down. He does not mount up
in pride, nor does he sink. He does not grow angry thinking, 'I am in trouble.'
They say that man of noble character is the very first. A good man of noble
character does not rejoice at his own happiness, nor take pleasure in another's
pain. When he has given, he does not regret it afterwards, nor boast.

"The man who wants to know the customs of countries, their agreements and the
dharma of each caste, who knows the high and the low, wins mastery over great
peoples wherever he goes. The wise man who avoids deceit, delusion, envy, evil
deeds, what the king hates, slander, enmity with a crowd, and quarrels with the
drunk, the mad and the wicked, is the chief. The gods favour the efforts of a
man who practises self-control, purity, worship, lucky rites, penances and the
many customs of the world every day.

"The wise man who marries among his equals and not beneath him, who makes
friendship, business and talk with his equals, and puts those greater in virtue
before him, has his policies well guided. Misfortune leaves the self-possessed
man who eats in measure after sharing with his dependants, sleeps in measure
after doing work beyond measure, and gives even to enemies when they ask. When
no one else knows anything of what a man means to do, or of what he has done,
and his counsel is kept secret and carried out well, not even the smallest of
his affairs goes wrong.

"The man who is set on the peace of all beings, true, gentle, generous and pure
in heart, stands out among his kin like a great jewel of fine quality, clear and
bright. The man who is deeply ashamed before himself becomes the teacher of the
whole world. Of boundless energy, cheerful and collected, he shines with his own
light like the sun.

---

"The five sons of Pandu, the king who was burnt by a curse, were born in the
forest. They are like five Indras. You raised them as children and taught them.
They keep your command, son of Ambika. Give them their fitting kingdom, my son,
and be happy and glad with your sons. Then, king of men, neither gods nor men
will have reason to doubt you."

**Choices.**

* The title of this sub-book in the CE is *Prajāgara*, "the sleepless". The sayings are kept in the text's order and none is dropped.
* 5.33.43: the number riddle is kept as the text gives it. Ganguli, following the commentators, explains it: with one (understanding) settle two (right and wrong); bring three (friend, enemy, neutral) under control with four (conciliation, gifts, division, force); conquer five (the senses); know six (the measures of policy); give up seven (the vices of 5.33.74). The novel does not add the gloss to the story.
* 5.33.49 *aviroddhāram*: "a king who does not fight"; *apravāsinam*: "a brahmin who does not travel".
* 5.33.65 *dṛteḥ pādāt*: "from the foot of a leather bag", the water-skin's leg.
* 5.33.78 *navanītāni*: "the fresh butter of joy", the text's image.
* 5.33.81: the "house with nine doors" is the body; the "three pillars" and "five witnesses" are not explained by the text. "The knower of the field" (*kṣetrajña*) is the self.
* 5.33.84: the "old story" of the asura king and Sudhanvan is told in full later (5.35). The verses that follow here are general.
* 5.33.103 *śāpadagdhasya*: Pandu "burnt by a curse", the deer-sage's curse.
