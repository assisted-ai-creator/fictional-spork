# Amba Seeks Rama

"'As she went out of the city she thought, Bharata,'" said Bhishma, "'"There is
no young woman on earth in a worse plight than I am. I have lost my kin, and
Shalva has rejected me. I cannot go back to the City of the Elephant, for
Bhishma let me go for Shalva's sake. Should I blame myself, or Bhishma, who is
hard to approach, or my foolish father, who held my bridegroom choice? This is
my own fault, that I did not jump down from Bhishma's chariot long ago, when the
cruel fight was going on, for Shalva's sake. This is its fruit, that I have come
to grief like a fool. Shame on Bhishma, and shame on my dull, foolish father,
who put me up like a woman for sale, with courage as my price. Shame on me,
shame on the king of the Shalvas, and shame on the Ordainer too, by whose bad
policy I have come to the worst misfortune. A man always gets his own lot. But
the head of this wrong to me is Bhishma, Shantanu's son. Now I see that I must
take my revenge on Bhishma, by austerity or by battle, for I think him the cause
of my grief. But what king would dare to beat Bhishma in battle?"

"'Having decided this, she went out of the city to a hermitage of great ascetics
of holy conduct. She spent that night there, surrounded by ascetics. The girl of
bright smiles told them everything that had happened to her, in full and in
detail, strong-armed Bharata: how she was carried off and let go, and how Shalva
sent her away. There was a great brahmin there of strict vows, Shaikhavatya,
grown old in austerity, a teacher of the science of the forest. The sage
Shaikhavatya, of great austerity, spoke to the good young girl in her pain,
sighing and given over to grief and sorrow. "Since things are so, good lady,
what can ascetics living in a hermitage do, however fortunate, great and devoted
to austerity?"

"'"Do me a kindness," she said to him, king. "I want to leave the world here. I
will practise hard austerity. Surely I did evil deeds in my folly in former
bodies, and this is surely their fruit. I cannot go back to my own people,
ascetics, rejected and joyless, sent away by Shalva. I want to be taught the
ascetic life here by you, who are free of sin and like gods. Have pity on me."
He comforted the girl with examples, scripture and reasons, consoled her, and
with the twice-born promised to help her.

---

"'Then all the ascetics set about the task, thinking what should be done about
the girl, men of dharma. Some ascetics said, "Let her be taken to her father's
house." Some of the best of the twice-born thought of reproaching me. Some
thought they should go to the lord of the Shalvas and press him. "No," some
decided, "for he has rejected her." Then all those ascetics of strict vows said
again, "Since things are so, good lady, what can the wise do? Enough of leaving
the world here, good lady. Listen to good advice. Go from here to your father's
house, bless you. Your father the king will decide what to do next. There you
will live happily, lovely one, with every good thing. There is no other proper
refuge for you, good lady, like your father. A woman's refuge is her husband or
her father, lovely one. Her husband is her refuge when things go well, but when
they go badly her father is. The ascetic's life is very painful, above all for a
tender girl, a princess by nature, as you are, lady. There are many dangers,
good lady, if you live in a hermitage, which there would not be in your father's
house." Then the brahmins said to the ascetic girl, "When they see you here
alone in the empty, deep forest, the lords of kings will court you. So do not
set your mind on this."

"'"I cannot go back to the city of Kashi, to my father's house," said Amba. "I
would be despised by my kin, no doubt of it. I lived otherwise in my father's
house as a child, ascetics. I will not go where my father is, bless you. I want
to practise austerity, protected by the ascetics, so that such great misfortune
may not be mine in the next world too. That is why I will practise austerity,
best of brahmins."

"'While the brahmins were thinking it over this way and that, the royal seer
Hotravahana, an ascetic, came to that forest. All the ascetics honoured the king
with greetings, a seat and water. When he had sat down and rested, and was
listening, the forest dwellers talked again about the girl. When he heard the
story of Amba and of the king of Kashi, Bharata, he rose trembling, for he was
her mother's father, and took the girl on his lap and comforted her, lord. He
asked her about the whole cause of her misfortune from the beginning, and she
told him what had happened in detail. Then the royal seer was filled with grief
and sorrow, and in his mind that great ascetic took up her cause. Trembling and
deeply grieved, he said to the girl in her pain, "Do not go to your father's
house, good lady. I am your mother's father. I will cut away your grief. Stay
with me, little daughter. Your mind is strong enough, my girl, though you are
wasting away like this. At my word, go to Rama son of Jamadagni, the ascetic.
Rama will take away your great grief and sorrow. He will kill Bhishma in battle
if he does not do as he says. Go to that best of the Bhargavas, whose splendour
is like the fire of time. That great ascetic will set you on the right road."

"'Then, weeping aloud again and again, she said to her mother's father
Hotravahana, "I will bow my head to him and go at your command. Shall I really
see that noble man, famous in the world, today? And how will the Bhargava
destroy my bitter grief? I want to hear that, and then I will go there."

"'"You will see Rama son of Jamadagni, child, in the great forest," said
Hotravahana, "engaged in fierce austerity, true to his word and very strong. On
Mahendra, best of mountains, seers who know the Veda, gandharvas and apsarases
always wait on Rama. Go there, bless you, and give him my message. First bow
your head to him, grown old in austerity and firm in his vows. Then tell him,
good lady, what you want done. When I am named, Rama will do all of it for you.
Rama son of Jamadagni is my friend, child, a loving friend who wishes me well, a
hero, the best of all who bear weapons."

"'As King Hotravahana was speaking to the girl, Akritavrana appeared, Rama's
dear follower. Then all the sages rose in their thousands, and the king, old in
years, Hotravahana the Srinjaya. Then the forest dwellers asked after each other
properly and sat down together around him, best of the Bharatas. They told one
another stories, lovely, heavenly tales, full of affection and joy, king of
kings. At the end of the talk, the great royal seer Hotravahana asked
Akritavrana about Rama, the best of great seers. "Where is mighty, strong-armed
Jamadagni's son now, Akritavrana? Can the best of those who know the Veda be
seen?"

"'"Rama always speaks of you, lord," said Akritavrana. "'The royal seer Srinjaya
is my dear friend,' he says, king. I think Rama will be here tomorrow morning.
You will see him come here, wanting to see you. But why has this girl come to
the forest, royal seer? Whose is she, and what is she to you? I want to know."

"'"She is my daughter's daughter, lord," said Hotravahana, "the lovely daughter
of the king of Kashi. She stood at the bridegroom choice, the eldest, with her
two sisters, sinless one. She is the eldest daughter of the lord of Kashi, known
as Amba. Ambika and Ambalika are her younger sisters, rich in austerity. The
kshatriyas of the earth gathered in the city of Kashi, and there was a great
festival for the girls, brahmin seer. Then, they say, mighty Bhishma son of
Shantanu, of great energy, threw down the kings and carried off the three girls.
When he had beaten the lords of the earth, Bhishma, pure in soul, came to the
City of the Elephant with the girls, Bharata. He told Satyavati, and the lord
gave orders for their marriage to his brother Vichitravirya. When she saw the
wedding preparations, this girl said to Ganga's son among his counsellors, bull
of the twice-born, 'In my mind I chose the lord of the Shalvas as my husband,
hero. You who know dharma should not give me away when my heart is another's.'
When he heard her, Bhishma took counsel with his counsellors, decided, and
following Satyavati's wish he let her go. With Bhishma's leave, the girl went
gladly to Shalva, lord of Saubha, and said to him at the right time, brahmin,
'Bhishma has let me go. Lead me to dharma. I chose you in my mind before, bull
among kings.' But Shalva rejected her, suspecting her conduct. So she came to
the ascetics' forest, and she is set on austerity. I knew her when she told her
lineage. She thinks Bhishma is the cause of this grief."

"'"Lord, it is just as the king says," said Amba. "Srinjaya Hotravahana gave my
mother her body. I cannot go back to my own city, rich in austerity, for fear of
contempt and for shame, great sage. Whatever the blessed Rama tells me, best of
the twice-born, I think that is what I must do above all, lord."

---

"'"You have two griefs, good lady," said Akritavrana. "For which of them do you
want revenge? Tell me, child. If you think the lord of Saubha should be pressed,
good lady, great Rama will press him, wanting your good. Or if you want to see
Bhishma, son of the river, beaten in battle by wise Rama, the Bhargava would do
that too. When he has heard Srinjaya's words and yours, lady of bright smiles,
let what is to be done next be decided today."

"'"Bhishma took me away without knowing, lord," said Amba. "For Bhishma did not
know, brahmin, that my heart had gone to Shalva. Think this over in your mind
and decide it yourself, as is right, and let it be done so. Against Bhishma,
tiger of the Kurus, or the king of the Shalvas, or both, brahmin, do what is
fitting. I have told you the root of my grief as it truly is. You should make
the arrangement wisely, lord."

"'"What you say is fitting, good lady, lovely one," said Akritavrana, "speaking
in keeping with dharma. Hear my words too. If the son of the river had not taken
you to the City of the Elephant, Shalva would take you on his head, timid one,
at Rama's urging. But because you were beaten and taken by him, lovely one, the
king of the Shalvas has doubts about you, slim one. Bhishma is proud of his
manhood and flushed with victory. So it is right that you have revenge taken on
Bhishma."

"'"This great wish is always in my heart too, brahmin," said Amba, "that I might
have Bhishma killed in battle. Bhishma or the king of the Shalvas, whichever you
find at fault, strong-armed one, punish the one because of whom I suffer so."

"'While they were talking like this, the day passed, and the night, best of the
Bharatas, with pleasant, cool and warm winds. Then Rama appeared, as if blazing
with splendour, surrounded by his pupils, king, a sage wearing matted hair and
bark cloth, with a bow in his hand and an undaunted spirit, carrying a sword and
an axe. Free of passion, tiger among kings, he came up to King Srinjaya. When
they saw him, the ascetics and the king of great austerity all stood with joined
hands, and so did the ascetic girl. They honoured the Bhargava calmly with the
honey-mixture, and when he had been worshipped as was fitting he sat down with
them. Then the two of them, the royal seer Srinjaya and Jamadagni's son, talked
of times gone by, Bharata. At the end of the talk the royal seer spoke sweet
words full of meaning, at the right moment, to mighty Rama, best of the Bhrigus.
"Rama, this is my daughter's daughter, daughter of the king of Kashi, lord. Hear
her cause truly, you who are skilled in causes."

"'"Let it be told," Rama said to her, and she went up to Rama, who blazed like a
fire. The lovely girl bowed her head to Rama's feet, touched them with hands
like lotus petals, and stood before him. She wept, full of grief, her eyes
clouded with tears, and took refuge with the Bhrigu's son, the refuge of all.

"'"You are to me what you are to this Srinjaya, princess," said Rama. "Tell me
the grief in your heart. I will do as you say."

"'"Lord of great vows, I take refuge with you today," said Amba. "Lift me out of
this terrible ocean of the mire of grief, lord."

"'When he saw her beauty, her fresh youth and her great tenderness, Rama was
filled with thought. "What will she say?" the best of the Bhrigus wondered, and
Rama thought for a long time, overcome with pity. "Let it be told," Rama said to
her again, and the girl of bright smiles told the Bhargava everything as it
truly was. When he heard the princess's words, Jamadagni's son decided on the
matter and said to the lovely girl, "I will send word to Bhishma, the best of
the Kurus, lady. When he hears my words, that lord of men will do what is in
keeping with dharma. If Jahnavi's son does not do as I say, I will burn him in
battle with his counsellors by the power of my weapons, good lady. Or if your
mind turns back to it, princess, I will set the hero, the lord of the Shalvas,
to this task first."

"'"Bhishma let me go as soon as he heard, son of Bhrigu, that my heart had gone
to the king of the Shalvas, as I had wished before," said Amba. "I went to the
king of Saubha and spoke words hard to speak, and he would not take me,
suspecting my conduct. Decide all this by your own wisdom, son of Bhrigu, and
think what means should be used here. Bhishma of great vows is the root of this
misfortune of mine, for he brought me under his power, lifting me up by force.
Kill Bhishma, strong-armed one, because of whom I have come to such grief, tiger
of the Bhrigus, and live in the greatest unhappiness. He is greedy and proud and
flushed with victory, Bhargava. So it is right that you take revenge on him,
sinless one. When the Bharata was carrying me off, lord, this resolve arose in
my heart: I would have that man of great vows killed. So fulfil my wish today,
sinless Rama. Kill Bhishma, strong-armed one, as Puramdara killed Vritra."

---

"'When she said, "Kill Bhishma," lord, Rama spoke to the weeping girl, who urged
him again and again. "Daughter of Kashi, lovely one, I do not willingly take up
weapons except for the sake of those who know Brahman. What else can I do for
you? By my word, Bhishma and Shalva will both obey me, queen. I will do that,
faultless one. Do not grieve. But I will not take up a weapon in any way, lady,
except at the command of brahmins. That is the agreement I have made."

"'"My grief must be taken away by the lord, however it may be," said Amba. "But
it was born of Bhishma. Kill him, lord, without delay."

"'"Daughter of Kashi, say it again," said Rama. "At my word Bhishma will clasp
both your feet with his head, though he is worthy of honour himself."

"'"Kill Bhishma in battle, Rama, if you want to please me," said Amba. "And if
you made a promise, you should make it true."

"'While Rama and Amba were talking like this, king, Akritavrana said this to
Jamadagni's son. "You should not abandon a girl who has come for refuge,
strong-armed one. Kill Bhishma in battle, Rama, like a roaring asura. If you
challenge Bhishma to battle, Rama, great sage, he will either say, 'I am
beaten,' or do as you say. Then this girl's purpose will be done, son of Bhrigu,
and your word will be made true, hero, lord. And this was your vow then, Rama,
great sage, which you promised the brahmins when you had conquered all the
kshatriyas: 'If a brahmin, a kshatriya, a vaishya or a shudra becomes a hater of
brahmins, I will kill him in battle. I will never abandon, while I live, those
who come to me for refuge, afraid and wanting to live. And the man who conquers
the whole gathered kshatriya class in battle, proud as he is, I will kill him
too,' Bhargava. Bhishma, the upholder of the Kuru family, is such a conqueror,
Rama. Meet him and fight him in battle, son of Bhrigu."

"'"I remember the vow I made before, best of seers," said Rama, "and I will act
so that it is won by conciliation alone. This task in the mind of the daughter
of Kashi is a great one, brahmin. I will go myself, taking the girl, to where he
is. If Bhishma, proud in battle, does not do as I say, I will kill that arrogant
man. That is my firm resolve. For the arrows I loose do not stick in the bodies
of living things here. You know that from the battle with the kshatriyas long
ago."

"'When he had said this, great-minded Rama made up his mind to set out and rose
with those who spoke of Brahman. The ascetics spent that night there, offered to
the fire, said their prayers, and set out wanting to kill me. Then Rama came to
Kurukshetra with those bulls among brahmins and the girl, great king, Bharata.
All those great ascetics, with the best of the Bhrigus at their head, made camp
beside the Sarasvati.'"
