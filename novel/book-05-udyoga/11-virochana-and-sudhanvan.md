summary: Vidura goes on counselling the sleepless king, on weighing the results of deeds, on governing by dharma, on mastering the senses and on the wounds of words; then he tells how the asura prince Virochana and the brahmin Sudhanvan staked their lives over the maiden Keshini, and how Virochana's father Prahlada judged truly against his own son; Vidura ends by warning Dhritarashtra not to lie for land, and not to put his power in the hands of Duryodhana, Shakuni, Duhshasana and Karna.

# Virochana and Sudhanvan

"Tell me what you see should be done," said Dhritarashtra, "for a man who lies
awake and burning. You are skilled in dharma and in what is useful, my son, and
pure. Guide me rightly, Vidura. Tell me wisely all that Ajatashatru wants. Tell
me what you think wholesome, you who are never mean in spirit, what will do the
Kurus good. I fear evil, and I see only evil. I ask you with a troubled mind.
Wise one, tell me exactly what Ajatashatru intends."

"Whether it is good or bad, hateful or pleasant," said Vidura, "a man should say
it, even unasked, to anyone whose defeat he does not want. So I will tell you,
king, wanting the Kurus to prosper. Listen to me as I tell you what is good and
right.

"Do not set your mind on deeds that succeed by falsehood, Bharata, or that are
done by the wrong means. In the same way, if a deed that was properly planned
and done by the right means does not succeed, king, a wise man should not lose
heart over it. Where deeds have consequences, a man should look at the
consequences. He should think them over and then act, and not rush at them. A
steady man looks at the consequences, at what deeds will lead to, and at his own
strength, and then acts or does not act.

"A king who does not know the measure of things, in standing still, in growth
and in loss, in his treasury, his country and his army, does not last in his
kingdom. But the man who sees these measures as they are taught, and gives
himself to knowing dharma and purpose, wins a kingdom. He should not behave
badly just because he has a kingdom. Bad conduct kills good fortune, as old age
kills beauty.

"The fish, drawn by the look of it, swallows the iron hook hidden under the best
of bait, and does not see what follows. A man who wants to prosper should eat
what can be swallowed, what will digest once swallowed, and what does him good
when it is digested. The man who picks unripe fruit from a tree gets no juice
from it, and its seed is wasted. But the man who takes ripe fruit in its season
gets juice from the fruit, and fruit again from the seed. As the bee takes honey
and leaves the flowers unharmed, so a king should take wealth from men without
harming them. He should pick flower by flower, like a garland maker in a garden,
and not cut the roots, like a charcoal burner.

"'What will become of me if I do this? What will become of me if I do not?' A
man should think his deeds over like this, and then act or not act. Some things
should never be started. They are always like that, and even the effort a man
makes over them is wasted. A wise man quickly starts on things that have small
roots and great fruit, and does not put obstacles in their way.

"The people love the king who looks at everything straight, as if drinking it in
with his eyes, even when he sits silent. The world is gracious to the man who
pleases the world in four ways, with his eyes, his mind, his speech and his
deeds. The man whom creatures fear, as deer fear the hunter, loses the earth
even when he has won it to the edge of the sea. A man who has won his father's
and grandfather's kingdom by his own power, and then stands on bad policy,
throws it away, as the wind scatters a cloud. For a king who follows dharma, the
way the good have followed from the first, the earth grows full of wealth and
makes prosperity grow. But for a king who gives up dharma and follows adharma,
the earth shrivels up, like leather laid on a fire.

"The same effort that is made to crush another's kingdom should be made to
protect one's own. A king should win his kingdom by dharma and guard it by
dharma. When he has won fortune rooted in dharma, he does not lose it, and it
does not leave him.

"A man should take what is of worth from everywhere, even from a madman's babble
or a crawling child, as gold is taken from stones. A steady man should sit
gathering the good sayings and good deeds of the wise from here and there, like
a gleaner gathering grain. Cows see by smell, brahmins by the Vedas, kings by
spies, and other people with their eyes.

"A cow that is hard to milk gets more trouble, king. A cow that is easy to milk
is left alone. What bends without heating is not heated. Wood that bends of its
own accord is not bent. By this image a steady man bows to one who is stronger.
The man who bows to one who is stronger bows to Indra.

"Rain clouds are the protectors of cattle, friends are the protectors of kings,
husbands are the protectors of women, and the Vedas are the protectors of
brahmins. Dharma is guarded by truth, learning by practice, beauty by
cleanliness and a family by conduct. Grain is guarded by measuring it, horses by
exercise, cows by being looked at often, and women by plain clothes. I think a
family is no proof of worth in a man without good conduct. Even among those born
low, conduct is what counts.

"The man who envies others their wealth, their beauty, their courage, their
family, their happiness, their good fortune and their honour has a sickness
without end. A man should not drink what would make him drunk, for fear of doing
what should not be done, of failing to do what should be done, and of letting
out counsel at the wrong time. The pride of learning, the pride of wealth and,
third, the pride of family are prides to those who are puffed up with pride, and
the same things are self-restraint to the good.

"When the good ask the wicked for anything, the wicked think themselves good,
and think the famous man wicked. The good are the refuge of the self-possessed.
The good are the refuge of the good. The good are the refuge of the wicked, but
the wicked are not the refuge of the good. The man with fine clothes wins the
assembly. The man with cows wins the hope of sweet things. The man with a
carriage wins the road. The man of good character wins everything. Character is
the chief thing in a man. If it is lost, he has no use for life, wealth or kin.

"The food of the rich is mostly meat, bull of the Bharatas, of the middling
mostly milk, and of the poor mostly salt. But the poor always eat the better
meal, for hunger gives it its sweetness, and that is very rare among the rich.
In the world the rich mostly have no power to eat, king of kings, while the poor
can digest even wood. The lowest fear for their living, the middling fear death,
but the highest of men fear insult most of all. Of all the kinds of drunkenness,
drink and the rest, the drunkenness of power is the worst. A man drunk with
power does not come to his senses until he falls.

"The world is tormented by the senses, running unchecked after their objects, as
the stars are by the planets. The man who is beaten by the band of five that are
born with him and wear him down finds his troubles grow like the moon in the
bright half of the month. The man who has not conquered himself, but wants to
conquer his counsellors, and has not conquered his counsellors but wants to
conquer his enemies, is helpless and falls away. The man who first conquers
himself, as if he were a country, does not try in vain to conquer his
counsellors and enemies after. Fortune always favours the steady man who has
mastered his senses and conquered his counsellors, who keeps punishment ready
for wrongdoers and acts after careful thought.

"A man's body is the chariot, king, the self is the driver, and the senses are
the horses. With those good horses well broken, a man who is careful and skilled
goes happily, like a steady chariot fighter. Unchecked, they can even kill him,
as unruly, unbroken horses kill a bad charioteer on the road. A fool led by his
senses sees harm as good and good as harm, and thinks great sorrow is happiness.
The man who gives up dharma and purpose and follows the will of his senses soon
loses fortune, life, wealth and wife. A man who is master of wealth but not of
his senses falls from his mastery because he does not master his senses.

"A man should seek himself by himself, with mind, understanding and senses held
in check. For the self is its own friend, and the self is its own enemy. Desire
and anger, king, like two big fish caught in a net of small mesh, break through
wisdom. The man who looks to dharma and purpose here before he gathers his means
has his means gathered and always thrives in happiness. The man who has not
conquered the five enemies inside him that destroy the mind, and wants to
conquer other enemies, is overcome by his enemies. Wicked men are seen being
destroyed by their own deeds, and kings by the troubles of their kingdoms,
because they are not masters of their senses.

"Because the wicked are not cast off, the same punishment touches the innocent
too, since they are mixed together. The wet burns with the dry when they are
mixed. So a man should not make peace with the wicked. The man who in his folly
does not hold down the five enemies of his own that rise up with their five aims
is swallowed by misfortune.

"Freedom from envy, straightness, purity, contentment, kind speech,
self-control, truth and calm are not found in the wicked. Knowledge of the self,
calm, patience, constancy in dharma, guarded speech and giving are not found in
the low, Bharata. Fools hurt the wise with abuse and slander. The speaker takes
on the sin, and the one who bears it is set free. Violence is the strength of
the wicked. Punishment is the strength of kings. Service is the strength of
women. Patience is the strength of the virtuous.

"Restraint of speech is thought the hardest thing of all, king. It is not
possible to say much that is both meaningful and fine. Well spoken, many kinds
of speech bring good. The same speech, badly spoken, king, leads to harm. A
forest pierced with arrows or cut with an axe grows back. A wound made by words,
foul and badly spoken, does not heal. Barbed arrows, reed arrows and iron arrows
can be drawn out of the body. But the dart of words cannot be drawn out, for it
lies in the heart. Arrows of words fly out of the mouth, and a man struck by
them grieves day and night. They fall nowhere but on another's vital places. A
wise man should not loose them at others.

"When the gods mean a man to be defeated, they take away his understanding, and
he sees things the wrong way round. When the understanding is clouded and ruin
is at hand, bad policy looks like good policy and does not leave the heart. That
understanding has taken hold of your sons, Bharata, through their enmity with
the Pandavas, and you do not see it.

"Yudhishthira, who has every mark of a king, and could be king of the three
worlds, is your pupil. Let him be your ruler, Dhritarashtra. He stands far above
all your sons, favoured by fortune, full of energy and wisdom, and he knows the
truth of dharma and purpose. That best of those who keep dharma bears many
troubles, king of kings, out of kindness, out of pity, and out of respect for
you."

---

"Tell me more words of dharma and purpose, wise one," said Dhritarashtra. "I
cannot have enough of listening. You speak wonderfully."

"To bathe at every holy ford," said Vidura, "and to be straight with all beings,
may be equal. Or straightness may be better. Be straight with your sons always,
lord. You will win the highest fame here, and heaven after death. As long as a
man's good fame is sung in the worlds, tiger among men, so long is he honoured
in the world of heaven.

"On this they tell an old story, the dispute between Virochana and Sudhanvan
over Keshini.

"'Are brahmins better, Virochana,' Keshini asked, 'or the sons of Diti? And why
should Sudhanvan not sit on the couch?'

"'We children of Prajapati are the best, Keshini,' said Virochana, 'the very
best. These worlds are ours. Who are the gods? Who are the twice-born?'

"'Let us wait here in the hall, Virochana,' said Keshini. 'Sudhanvan will come
in the morning. I want to see you both together.'

"'I will do as you say, good lady, timid one,' said Virochana. 'You will see
Sudhanvan and me together in the morning.'

"When Sudhanvan came, he said, 'Son of Prahlada, I touch your golden seat. I
have come to it as an equal, but I will not sit on it with you.'

"'Let them bring a plank,' said Virochana, 'or a bundle of grass, or a mat,
Sudhanvan. You are not fit to share a seat with me.'

"'Your father himself,' said Sudhanvan, 'would sit lower than me when I was
seated, and wait on me. You are a child brought up in comfort at home. You
understand nothing.'

"'Let us stake the gold, the cows and the horses, all the wealth we have among
the asuras, Sudhanvan,' said Virochana, 'and ask those who know.'

"'Let the gold, the cows and the horses stay yours, Virochana,' said Sudhanvan.
'Let us stake our lives, and ask those who know.'

"'Where shall we go, when we have staked our lives?' said Virochana. 'I will
never stand before the gods, nor before men.'

"'We shall go to your father, when we have staked our lives,' said Sudhanvan.
'Prahlada would not tell a lie, even for his son's sake.'

"'Here are two who never went about together,' said Prahlada, 'come here by one
road like two angry poisonous snakes. Why do you go about together now, when you
never did before? I ask you this, Virochana. What friendship have you with
Sudhanvan?'

"'I have no friendship with Sudhanvan,' said Virochana. 'We have staked our
lives. Prahlada, I ask you this. Do not give a false answer to the question.'

"'Let them bring water and the honey drink for Sudhanvan,' said Prahlada. 'You
must be honoured, brahmin. A white cow has been fattened for you.'

"'Water and the honey drink were given to me on the way,' said Sudhanvan.
'Prahlada, give us a true answer to the question we ask.'

"'Brahmin,' said Prahlada, 'one is my son, and you stand here as a witness. How
can a man like me answer the question of two people who are quarrelling? And I
ask you this, Sudhanvan. Where would a man live who gives no answer, true or
false, or who answers wrongly?'

"'A man who answers wrongly,' said Sudhanvan, 'lives that night as a wife lives
the night her husband takes a second wife, or a man the night he is beaten at
dice, or a man whose limbs ache under his load. He lives it as a man shut out of
the city, starving outside the gate, seeing enemies everywhere. A man who lies
about cattle destroys five, about cows ten, about horses a hundred, and about
men a thousand. A man who lies for gold destroys those born and those not yet
born. A man who lies about land destroys everything. Never lie about land.'

"'Angiras is better than I am,' said Prahlada, 'and Sudhanvan is better than
you, Virochana. His mother is better than your mother. So you are beaten by him.
Virochana, Sudhanvan is now master of your life. Sudhanvan, I want you to give
Virochana back to me.'

"'Because you chose dharma,' said Sudhanvan, 'and did not lie out of love, I
give you back your son, Prahlada, though he is hard to get back. Here is your
son Virochana, Prahlada, given back by me. But let him wash my feet in front of
the girl.'

---

"So, king of kings," said Vidura, "you must not lie for the sake of land. Do not
follow your sons and go to ruin with your sons and counsellors.

"The gods do not take a stick and guard a man like a herdsman. When they want to
guard a man, they give him understanding. As a man sets his mind on what is
good, so all his aims succeed, no doubt of it. The Vedas do not save a deceiver
who lives by deceit from his sin. They leave him at his end, as fledged birds
leave the nest.

"They say a man should avoid drinking bouts, quarrels, feuds with crowds, coming
between husband and wife, divisions among kin, what the king hates, disputes
between a man and a woman, and a road that is bad. A man should not make these
seven witnesses: a palm reader, a merchant who was once a thief, a cheat at
dice, a doctor, an enemy, a friend and an actor. The fire offering made for
pride, silence kept for pride, study for pride and sacrifice for pride: these
four take away fear, but they bring fear when they are done wrongly.

"The man who burns houses, the poisoner, the son of a woman's lover, the seller
of soma, the arrow maker, the astrologer, the betrayer of friends, the
adulterer, the killer of an embryo, the man who violates his teacher's bed, the
brahmin who drinks, the man who is too harsh, the crow, the unbeliever, the man
who reviles the Vedas, the man who takes the sacrificial ladle without being
fit, the outcaste, the miser even when he is rich, and the man who kills one who
begs him for protection: these are all equal to the killers of brahmins.

"Gold is known by a burning straw, a good man by his dealings, a brave man in
danger, a steady man when wealth is hard to come by, and friends and enemies in
hard misfortune. Old age takes beauty, hope takes steadiness, death takes life,
envy takes the practice of dharma, anger takes fortune, serving the low takes
good character, desire takes shame, and pride takes everything.

"Fortune is born of good acts, grows with boldness, takes root through skill,
and stands firm through self-control. Eight qualities make a man shine: wisdom,
good family, self-control, learning, courage, not talking too much, giving as
far as he can, and gratitude. One quality, my son, takes hold of all these great
qualities by force. When the king honours a man, that quality outshines all the
rest.

"These eight, king, in the world of men, are signs of the world of heaven. Four
of them are followed by the good, and the good follow the other four. Sacrifice,
giving, study and austerity, these four are followed by the good. Self-control,
truth, straightness and kindness, the good follow these four.

"That is no assembly where there are no elders. Those are no elders who do not
speak dharma. That is no dharma where there is no truth. That is no truth that
is mixed with deceit. Truth, beauty, learning, knowledge, good family, good
character, strength, wealth, courage and fine speech: these ten are the sources
of company.

"A man who does evil gets an evil name and eats only evil fruit. A man who does
good gets a good name and eats only good fruit. Evil done again and again
destroys wisdom. A man whose wisdom is destroyed always starts on evil. Good
done again and again makes wisdom grow. A man whose wisdom has grown always
starts on good. The envious man, the biter, the harsh man, the maker of enmity,
soon comes to great trouble by doing evil. The man without envy, of trained
mind, who always does fine things, comes to happiness without trouble and shines
everywhere. The man who gets wisdom from the wise is learned. A wise man, having
gained dharma and purpose, can thrive in happiness.

"In the daytime a man should do what will let him live happily at night. In
eight months he should do what will let him live happily in the rains. In youth
he should do what will let him live happily when old. All his life he should do
what will let him live happily after death.

"People praise food that has been digested, a wife whose youth is gone, a hero
when the battle is over, and an ascetic who has reached the far shore. A gap
covered with wealth gained by adharma stays uncovered, and another opens beyond
it. The teacher is the ruler of the self-possessed. The king is the ruler of the
wicked. Yama, son of Vivasvat, is the ruler of those who hide their sins. The
origins of seers, of rivers, of great families, of women and of bad conduct
should not be looked into.

"A kshatriya who delights in honouring the twice-born, who is generous, and
straight with his kin, has a share in heaven, king, and rules the earth long.
Three men gather the earth's golden flowers: the brave man, the learned man, and
the man who knows how to serve. The best deeds are those of the mind, the
middling ones those of the arms, the lowest those of the legs, and the very
lowest those of carrying loads.

"You have set your power in Duryodhana and Shakuni, in foolish Duhshasana and in
Karna. How can you hope to prosper? The Pandavas have every virtue, bull of the
Bharatas. They behave towards you as towards a father. Behave towards them as
towards sons."
