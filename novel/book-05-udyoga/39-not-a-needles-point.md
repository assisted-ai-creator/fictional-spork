# Not a Needle's Point

"Lord, it is just as you say, Narada," said Dhritarashtra. "I want it too, lord.
But I am not master."

Then he spoke to Krishna, Bharata. "Keshava, what you have told me leads to
heaven and to the good of the world. It is right and just. But I am not my own
master, my son. What is being done does not please me. Come, Krishna, try to win
over my foolish son Duryodhana, who goes beyond the teachings. Strong-armed one,
best of men, you will have done a great service to your friends, Janardana."

Then the Varshneya, who knew the truth of all dharma and purpose, turned to
impatient Duryodhana and spoke sweet words.

"Duryodhana, listen to these words of mine, best of the Kurus. They are good
above all for you and all who are bound to you, Bharata. Very wise one, born in
a good family, rich in learning and good conduct, with every virtue, you ought
to do this well. Men of bad family, wicked, cruel and shameless, would do the
kind of thing you are thinking of, my son. In this world the conduct of the good
is seen to keep to dharma and purpose. The conduct of the bad is seen to be the
opposite, bull of the Bharatas. That opposite conduct is seen in you again and
again. The adharma that is bound up with it is terrible, great, and takes away
life. You have caused it many times, and it brings no glory, Bharata. Avoid that
harm and you will do yourself good. You will free your brothers, your servants
and your friends from a deed that is against dharma and brings no glory,
scorcher of enemies.

"Make peace with the Pandavas, tiger among men, bull of the Bharatas. They are
wise, brave, bold, self-possessed and very learned. That is good, and it is what
wise Dhritarashtra wants, and the grandfather, Drona, very wise Vidura, Kripa,
Somadatta, wise Bahlika, Ashvatthama, Vikarna and Sanjaya, lord of the people,
and most of your kin and friends, scorcher of enemies. In peace there would be
happiness for the whole world, my son. You are modest, of good family, learned
and kind. Keep to the rule of your father and your mother, bull of the Bharatas.
People think what a father teaches is best, Bharata. Everyone in the worst
trouble remembers his father's teaching. Your father wants you and your
counsellors to be joined with the Pandavas, my son, best of the Kurus. Let that
please you too.

"A man who hears the teaching of his friends and does not take it is burnt by it
in the end, like a man who has eaten the kimpaka fruit. A man who in his folly
does not take advice for his good is slow, loses his aim, and is joined with
regret afterwards. But a man who hears good advice and takes it at once, setting
aside his own opinion, thrives happily in the world. A man who will not bear the
words of one who wants his good, because they go against him, and listens to
things against his good, falls into the power of his enemies. A man who passes
over the opinion of the good and follows the opinion of the bad will soon have
his friends grieving over his misfortune. A man who leaves his chief counsellors
and serves low ones meets terrible trouble and finds no way across. A man who
serves the bad, behaves for nothing, never listens to his friends, chooses
outsiders and hates his own, the cow curses, Bharata.

"You have quarrelled with those heroes, and you want protection from others,
from people who are ill-bred, incapable and foolish, bull of the Bharatas. What
man on earth but you would pass over kinsmen who are great chariot fighters,
equal to Shakra, and hope for protection from others? Since their birth you have
always cheated the sons of Kunti, and they are never angry with you, for the
Pandavas' soul is dharma. Though they have been treated falsely since birth, my
son, the famous Pandavas have behaved well to you, strong-armed one. You too
should behave the same way to them, bull of the Bharatas. Do not fall into the
power of anger against your own chief kin.

"The undertakings of the wise are joined with the three aims, bull of the
Bharatas. When all three cannot be had, men follow dharma and purpose. When they
are separate, the steady man follows dharma, the middling man purpose, and the
fool, the man of strife, only pleasure. The man who, led by his senses, gives up
dharma out of greed, and tries to win pleasure and purpose by the wrong means,
perishes. The man who wants pleasure and purpose should practise dharma first.
Neither purpose nor pleasure ever leaves dharma. They say dharma is the means to
the three aims, lord of the people. The man who seeks by it grows quickly, like
fire in dry brush.

"You, my son, want to win by the wrong means a great sovereignty, blazing and
famous among all kings, bull of the Bharatas. The man who behaves falsely to
those who behave well cuts himself down, king, as an axe cuts a forest. A man
should not break the mind of one whose defeat he does not want. When the steady
man's mind is not broken, it is set on good. A man should not harm one who has
given up his life, in the three worlds, Bharata, not even some common man, let
alone those bulls of the Pandavas. A man in the grip of impatience understands
nothing. Everything that is spread out is cut down. See the proof, Bharata.
Union with the Pandavas is better for you than union with bad men, my son. If
they are pleased with you, you will have all your wishes.

"You enjoy the earth the Pandavas conquered, best of kings, and you put the
Pandavas behind you and hope for protection elsewhere. You set your power in
Duhshasana, in Durvishaha, in Karna and in Subala's son, and you hope to
prosper, Bharata. They are not enough for you in knowledge of dharma and
purpose, nor enough in courage against the Pandavas, Bharata. All these kings
together with you are not enough to look at the face of Bhimasena in anger in
battle. Here is the whole army of the earth gathered, my son. Here are Bhishma
and Drona, Karna and Kripa, Bhurishravas son of Somadatta, Ashvatthama and
Jayadratha. All of them are unable to fight Dhananjaya. Arjuna in anger cannot
be beaten by all the gods and asuras, by men or by gandharvas. Do not set your
mind on war. Let some man be found in the whole army of the earth who could meet
Arjuna in battle and go home safe.

"What good to you is the destruction of the people, bull of the Bharatas? Let
the one man be found whose defeat would mean your victory. Who would fight the
man who beat the gods, the gandharvas, yakshas, asuras and serpents at
Khandavaprastha? There is also the great wonder told of Virata's city, one
against many. That is proof enough. You hope to beat in battle Jishnu, unbeaten,
unassailable, unfallen, the hero Arjuna, so mighty. Who could challenge the
Partha with me as his second, coming against him in battle, even Purandara
himself? The man who could beat Arjuna in battle could lift the earth with his
arms, burn up these creatures in his anger, and throw the gods down from heaven.

"Look at your sons and brothers, your kin and your relations. Let them not
perish because of you, best of the Bharatas. Let something be left of the
Kauravas. Let this family not be defeated. Do not let them call you the
destroyer of the family, king, your fame lost. The great chariot fighters will
set you up as heir, and your father Dhritarashtra, lord of the people, as great
king. Do not despise fortune when it comes to you and stands ready, my son. Give
half to the Parthas and you will win great fortune. Make peace with the
Pandavas, do as your friends say, and you will enjoy good things for a long
time, beloved by your friends."

---

When he heard Keshava's words, Bhishma, son of Shantanu, spoke to impatient
Duryodhana, bull of the Bharatas. "Krishna has spoken to you, wanting peace
among friends. Look at it, my son. Do not fall into the power of anger. If you
do not do what great Keshava says, my son, you will never have good or happiness
or welfare. Strong-armed Keshava has told you what is right and useful, my son.
Take it. Do not destroy the people, king. Through your wickedness you will make
this blazing fortune of the Bharatas fall among all the kings while
Dhritarashtra still lives. With your evil mind you will make yourself fall from
life, with your counsellors, your sons, your cattle, your kin and your friends,
if you pass over Keshava's true and sensible words, and your father's, best of
the Bharatas, and wise Vidura's. Do not take the bad road, you destroyer of the
family, last of your line, you of evil mind. Do not give your old father and
mother over to grief."

Then Drona spoke to Duryodhana, who had fallen into the power of impatience and
sighed again and again. "Keshava has spoken to you words of dharma and purpose,
my son, and so has Bhishma, Shantanu's son. Take them, king of men. They are
wise and intelligent, self- controlled, wanting your good, very learned. They
have spoken good words to you. Take them, scorcher of enemies. Do what Krishna
and Bhishma have said, very wise one. Do not follow the words of the
light-minded, scorcher of enemies. Those who egg you on will never do the work.
In battle they will hang the enmity round others' necks. Do not have all the
Kurus killed, your sons and your brothers too. Know that where Vasudeva and
Arjuna are, that army cannot be beaten. This is the true opinion of your friends
Krishna and Bhishma. If you do not take it, my son, you will regret it
afterwards, Bharata. Arjuna is greater even than Jamadagnya said. And Krishna,
son of Devaki, is hard to bear even for the gods. What is the use of telling you
what you would like to hear, bull of the Bharatas? I have told you all of this.
Do as you wish. I cannot bring myself to speak to you again, best of the
Bharatas."

In the middle of this, Vidura the steward too spoke, looking at impatient
Duryodhana, son of Dhritarashtra. "Duryodhana, I do not grieve for you, bull of
the Bharatas. I grieve for these two old ones, Gandhari and your father. With
you, a bad friend, as their protector, they will wander without protectors,
their friends and counsellors killed, like birds with their wings cut. They will
wander this earth as beggars, grieving, having brought forth such an evil,
wicked man, the destroyer of the family."

Then King Dhritarashtra spoke to Duryodhana as he sat with his brothers,
surrounded by kings. "Duryodhana, listen to what great Shauri has said. Take
what is entirely good, safe and lasting. With Krishna, whose deeds never fail,
as our helper, we shall win all we wish among all the kings. Go to Yudhishthira,
my son, joined with Keshava. Do the whole rite of welfare for the Bharatas,
their health. Go to the meeting, my son, with Vasudeva as your ford. I think the
time has come for it. Do not go beyond it, Duryodhana. If you reject Keshava
when he asks for peace and speaks for your sake, there is no defeat for you but
that."

---

When they heard Dhritarashtra's words, Bhishma and Drona backed them, and spoke
to Duryodhana, who went beyond all command.

"While the two Krishnas are not yet in armour, while the Gandiva is still, while
Dhaumya does not yet offer the enemy's army as an oblation in the fire of the
army, while Yudhishthira, the great archer held back by shame, does not yet look
at your army in anger, let the slaughter be stopped. While the Partha is not yet
seen standing among his own ranks, while Bhimasena the great archer is not, let
the slaughter be stopped. While he does not yet roam the roads, cheering his
army, while he does not yet bring down in battle the heads of the elephant
riders with his hero-killing mace, as ripe fruit falls from a tree in its
season, let the slaughter be stopped.

"Nakula and Sahadeva, Dhrishtadyumna son of Prishata, Virata, Shikhandi and
Shishupala's son, in their armour, skilled in weapons and quick to shoot: while
they do not yet plunge in like crocodiles into the great ocean, let the
slaughter be stopped. While fierce vulture-feathered arrows do not yet fall on
the tender bodies of the kings, let the slaughter be stopped. While the great
iron arrows of the great archers, skilled, quick, shooting far and true, do not
yet strike the breasts of the warriors, smeared with sandal and aloe and wearing
necklaces and gold, let the slaughter be stopped.

"When you greet him with your head bowed, let the Dharma King Yudhishthira,
elephant among kings, take you in his hands. Let him of the generous right hand
lay on your shoulder, for peace, his right arm marked with banner, goad and
flag, bull of the Bharatas. When you are seated, let him stroke your back with
his hand, its palm and fingers bright with jewels and with lucky herbs. Let
strong-armed Vrikodara, like a shala trunk, embrace you and speak to you with
kindness, for peace, bull of the Bharatas. When Arjuna and the twins, those
three, greet you, smell their heads and speak to them with love, king. Let the
kings shed tears of joy when they see you joined with your heroic brothers, the
Pandavas. Let it be proclaimed in the royal cities that all is well with the
kings. Let the earth be enjoyed in brotherhood. Be free of your fever."

---

When he heard these unwelcome words in the assembly of the Kurus, Duryodhana
answered strong-armed, famous Vasudeva.

"You should think before you speak, Keshava. You single me out and blame me. Out
of devotion to the Parthas, Madhusudana, you always blame me for no reason. What
strength or weakness have you seen? You and the steward, the king, the teacher
and the grandfather blame only me, and no other king. I see no fault in myself
here. Yet all of you, with the kings, hate me. Thinking it over, Keshava, I do
not see any great offence of mine, not even the smallest, tamer of enemies.

"The Pandavas came gladly to the dicing, Madhusudana, and were beaten by Shakuni
and lost the kingdom. What wrong did I do in that? Whatever wealth the Pandavas
lost there was given back to them then, Madhusudana. It is no offence of ours
that they were beaten at dice and went off to the forest, those unbeaten
Parthas, best of victors. By what blame do they set themselves against us as
enemies, Krishna? The Pandavas are weak, and yet they rejoice like enemies. What
have we done to them, and for what offence do the Pandavas want to kill the sons
of Dhritarashtra with the Srinjayas?

"We will not bow down here out of fear of any fierce deed or word, not even for
fear of Shatakratu. I see no one, Krishna, slayer of enemies, keeping to the
dharma of a kshatriya, who could hope to beat us in war. Not even the gods could
beat Bhishma, Kripa and Drona and their hosts in battle, Madhusudana, let alone
the Pandavas. If we keep our own dharma, Madhava, and meet death by weapons in
battle in our time, that is heaven itself. This is our chief dharma as
kshatriyas, Janardana, that we lie in battle on a bed of arrows. If we come to
the bed of heroes in battle without bowing to our enemies, we shall have no
regret, Madhava.

"Who, born in a good family and living by the dharma of a kshatriya, would bow
down here to anyone out of fear, looking to his livelihood? A man should stand
up and not bow, for effort is manhood. He may break at the wrong joint, but he
should not bow to anyone here. That is the saying of Matanga, which those who
want good seek to follow. One like me should bow only for dharma and to
brahmins. He should behave like that all his life, thinking of no one else. This
is the dharma of kshatriyas, and it has always been my view.

"The share of the kingdom my father granted them before will never be had again
while I live, Keshava. As long as King Dhritarashtra lives, Janardana, either we
or they must lay down our weapons and live on the other, Madhava. If the
kingdom, which should not have been given, was given before by my father, who
was in another's power, out of ignorance or fear, when I was a child, Janardana,
it cannot be had again today by the Pandavas, joy of the Vrishnis, while I live,
strong-armed Keshava. As much as the point of a sharp needle would pierce,
Madhava, not even that much of our land will I give up to the Pandavas."
