# Be Good to Your Kin

"When an old man comes near," said Vidura, "a young man's breath rises up as if
to leave him. He gets it back by rising and greeting him. A steady man should
give a seat to a good man who comes to him. He should bring water and wash his
feet, ask after his comfort and tell of his own state, and then, with thought,
give him food. The noble say that a man lives in vain if a brahmin who knows the
mantras does not accept water, the honey drink and a cow in his house, because
of the man's greed, fear or meanness.

"A doctor, a maker of arrows, a man who has broken his vow of chastity, a thief,
a cruel man, a drinker, the killer of an embryo, a man who lives by soldiering
and a man who sells the Veda do not deserve water, even when they come as very
dear guests.

"These are not to be sold: salt, cooked food, curds, milk, honey, oil, ghee,
sesame, meat, roots and fruit, vegetables, red cloth, all perfumes and molasses.

"The man who is free of anger, to whom a clod of earth and gold are the same,
who has left grief behind, who has given up making peace and war, who has turned
away from blame and praise, and who goes about indifferent to the pleasant and
the unpleasant, is a mendicant. The man who lives on wild rice, roots, ingudi
fruit and greens, who keeps himself in hand, who needs no urging in the rites of
the fire, who lives in the forest and is careful of his guests, is an ascetic, a
bearer of the burden and a doer of good.

"Having wronged a wise man, a man should not rest easy, thinking, 'I am far
away.' A wise man's arms are long. When he is hurt, he hurts with them. A man
should not trust the untrustworthy, nor trust too much the trustworthy. Fear
born of trust cuts even the roots.

"A man should be free of jealousy and guard his wives. He should share with
them, speak kindly, and be gentle and sweet-spoken with women, but he should not
fall under their power. Women are to be honoured. They are fortunate and holy,
the lights of the house. Women are called the fortune of the house, so they must
be guarded above all. A man should give his father the women's quarters, his
mother the kitchen, and the cattle to one like himself. He should go to the
farming himself. He should carry on trade through servants and serve brahmins
through his sons.

"Fire came from water, the kshatriya from the brahmin, and iron from stone.
Their energy reaches everywhere, but it dies down in its own source. Good men
born in good families, with energy like fire, are always patient and show
nothing, like fire lying in wood.

"The king whose counsel is not known by those outside or those inside, and who
has eyes everywhere, enjoys power for a long time. He should not speak of what
he is going to do, but only show what he has done, in matters of dharma, desire
and purpose. Then his counsel is not broken. Counsel should be taken after
climbing a mountaintop or a palace roof, alone, or in a forest with no grass in
it. No one who is not a friend should know the highest counsel, Bharata, nor a
friend who is not wise, nor a wise man who is not master of himself. A minister
should be tested for his greed for money and for keeping counsel. The king whose
counsel is hidden, and whose councillors know of his affairs only when they are
done, is sure to succeed.

"The man who in his folly carries out deeds that are not praised loses his life
too when they go wrong. Doing praiseworthy deeds brings happiness. Not doing
them brings great regret.

"The earth is in the hands of a king who knows standing still, growth and loss,
who has mastered the six measures of policy, and whose conduct is not despised.
The earth is the treasure of a king whose anger and delight are never wasted,
who sees to his affairs himself and whose treasury is known only to him. A king
should be content with his title and his royal umbrella. He should give wealth
to his servants, and not take everything for himself alone.

"A brahmin knows a brahmin, a husband knows his wife, a king knows his minister,
and a king knows a king. An enemy who has fallen into one's hands and is fit to
be killed should not be let go. Danger soon comes from him if he is left alive.
Anger should always be held back with care towards the gods, kings and brahmins,
and towards the old, the young and the sick.

"A wise man should avoid the useless quarrels that fools pursue. He wins fame in
the world and meets no harm. People do not want a master whose favour bears no
fruit and whose anger comes to nothing, any more than women want a eunuch for a
husband. Understanding is not for getting wealth, nor dullness for poverty. The
wise man knows how the world turns, and no one else does.

"A fool always despises those great in learning, character and years, those
great in understanding, and those great in wealth and family. Troubles come
quickly to the man of ignoble conduct, the unwise, the envious and the man
without dharma, to the foul-mouthed and the angry. Keeping one's word, giving,
not breaking an agreement, and well-chosen speech win all creatures over. The
man who keeps his word, who is skilled, grateful, wise and straight, finds
followers even when his treasury is empty.

"Steadiness, calm, self-control, purity, compassion, gentle speech, and never
harming one's friends: these seven are the fuel of good fortune. The lowest of
men, who will not share, who is wicked, ungrateful and shameless, should be
avoided in the world, king. A man who is himself at fault and angers innocent
people close to him does not sleep in peace at night, like a man with a snake in
the house. Those who, if they were offended, could harm a man's getting and
keeping should always be won over, Bharata, like the gods.

"Wealth that is bound up with women, with careless men, or with the base is all
in doubt. Lands where a woman, a gambler or a child rules sink helplessly, like
rafts of stone in a river. I think those are wise who keep to their aims and not
to details, Bharata. Details lead a man astray. The man whom gamblers praise,
whom bards praise, and whom harlots praise does not live.

"You set aside those great archers, the Pandavas of boundless energy, and placed
the great power of the Bharatas in Duryodhana. You will soon see him fall from
it, blinded by the pride of power, as Bali fell from the three worlds."

---

"A man is not master of his rising or his fall," said Dhritarashtra. "He is like
a wooden doll on strings. The Ordainer has put him in the power of fate. So
speak. I am listening."

"Even Brihaspati, if he spoke out of season, Bharata," said Vidura, "would have
his wisdom scorned and be insulted. One man is loved for his gifts, another for
his kind words, another for the strength of his counsel and his support. But the
one who is loved is loved anyway. The one who is hated is not called good, or
clever, or wise. In the one we love, all deeds are good. In the one we hate, all
are evil, Bharata.

"That is no loss, great king, which brings growth. Count that a loss which, when
it comes, destroys much. Some are rich in virtues, others in wealth. Keep away
from those rich in wealth but poor in virtue, Dhritarashtra."

"All you say looks to the future, and the wise approve it," said Dhritarashtra.
"But I cannot give up my son. Where dharma is, there is victory."

"A man rich in good nature and good manners," said Vidura, "will never cause
creatures even the smallest harm. Some men are always busy slandering others,
causing them pain, and setting them against one another. To see them is harmful,
and to live with them is very dangerous. There is great harm in taking from
them, and great danger in giving to them. A man should keep away from those
known to be wicked, who are blamed in company and given to other great faults.

"When the friendship of a low man turns away, his love is lost, and so is the
fruit of the friendship and its happiness. He tries to slander his friend and
works for his ruin. Even for a small wrong, in his folly, he finds no peace. A
learned man, looking hard with his understanding at such a union with low,
cruel, unformed men, should keep away from it from afar.

"The man who helps a kinsman who is poor, wretched or sick prospers in sons and
cattle and wins lasting fame. Those who want their own good should make their
kin prosper. So do what is good for the growth of your family, king of kings.
You will meet good fortune, king, by honouring your kin. Even kin without virtue
must be protected, bull of the Bharatas. How much more those with virtue, who
hope for your favour! Be gracious to the poor Pandavas, lord of the people. Let
them be given a few villages to live on, lord. In this way you will win fame in
the world, king of men.

"You are old, my son. You must protect your sons. And I must tell you what is
good. Know that I wish you well. A man who wants to prosper should not quarrel
with his kin, my son. Pleasures should be enjoyed with kin, bull of the
Bharatas. Eating together, talking together and loving one another should be
done with kin, never quarrelling. Kin carry a man across, and kin sink him. Kin
of good conduct carry him across, and kin of bad conduct sink him. Behave well
towards the Pandavas, king of kings, giver of honour. Surrounded by them, you
will be beyond your enemies' reach. A kinsman who comes to a rich kinsman and
then suffers brings the sin on him, as a wounded deer brings it on the man with
the poisoned hand.

"Later you will feel remorse, best of men, when you hear that they are dead, or
your sons. Think about that. Life is uncertain. A man should not do at the start
what will make him suffer when he lies on his bed. No man ever fails to make
mistakes, except Bhargava. But making good what is left lies only with the wise.
If Duryodhana has done this wrong to them before, you, the elder of the family,
should set it right, lord of men. Set them in their place, and you will be free
of stain in the world, best of men, and honoured by the wise.

"The man who thinks over the good words of the steady by their results, and then
sets his mind to the work, stays long in fame. Good manners kill misfortune.
Courage kills harm. Patience always kills anger. Good conduct kills bad signs. A
family should be tested, king, by its belongings, its land, its house, its
service, and its food and clothing.

"When two minds meet, or two secrets, or two understandings, their friendship
does not wear out. A wise man should keep away from the evil-minded and
untrained man, like a well hidden by grass. Friendship with him is lost. A wise
man should not make friends with the proud, the foolish, the fierce and rash, or
those who have given up dharma. The friend to seek is grateful, dharmic, true
and generous, firm in loyalty, master of his senses, steady in his ways, and one
who does not leave.

"Holding the senses in completely is no better than death. But letting them
loose too far could ruin even the gods. The wise say that gentleness to all
beings, freedom from envy, patience, steadiness, and not slighting friends make
life long.

"The man who, with firm resolve, tries by good policy to win back what bad
policy has lost is keeping a hero's vow. The man who knows how to meet the
future, who is firm in the present, and who knows what is left to do from the
past, does not lose his wealth. Whatever a man often does in act, mind and
speech, that carries him away. So he should do good. Touching lucky things,
discipline, learning, effort, straightness and often seeing the good make for
prosperity.

"Never losing heart is the root of fortune and of happiness when sorrow ends.
The man who does not lose heart becomes great and enjoys lasting happiness.
Nothing brings more fortune, and nothing is better for a man, my son, than
patience everywhere and always, if he is mighty. The weak man should bear with
everyone. The strong man should bear with others for dharma's sake. Patience is
always good for the man to whom gain and loss are the same. A man may enjoy any
pleasure that does not make him fall short in dharma and purpose. He should not
keep a fool's vow.

"Fortune does not live with those tormented by sorrow, the careless, the
unbelievers, the lazy, those without self-control or those without energy. Men
of bad mind think the straight man, who is modest because he is straight, is
weak, and they attack him. Fortune does not come near the man who is too noble,
too generous, too brave, too strict in his vows, or proud of his wisdom. She is
afraid.

"The fruit of the Vedas is the fire offering. The fruit of learning is good
character and conduct. The fruit of wives is pleasure and sons. The fruit of
wealth is giving and enjoying. The man who performs the funeral rites with
wealth gained by adharma does not enjoy their fruit after death, because the
wealth came badly. Those who have something left have no fear in wild forests
and strongholds, in hard misfortunes and panic, or when weapons are raised.

"Effort, restraint, skill, care, steadiness, memory and starting after thought:
know that these are the root of prosperity. Austerity is the strength of
ascetics, Brahman the strength of those who know Brahman, violence the strength
of the wicked, and patience the strength of the virtuous. Eight things do not
break a vow: water, roots, fruit, milk, ghee, what a brahmin wishes, a teacher's
word, and medicine.

"A man should not do to another what he would not like done to himself. That, in
short, is dharma. The rest comes from desire. A man should conquer anger by
calm, the wicked by goodness, the miser by giving, and the liar by truth. A man
should not trust a womaniser, a lazy man, a coward, a fierce man, a man proud of
his manhood, a thief, an ungrateful man or an unbeliever. For the man who greets
others respectfully and always serves the old, four things grow: good name,
life, fame and strength.

"Do not set your mind on wealth won by great hardship, by breaking dharma, or by
bowing down to an enemy. To be pitied are the unlearned man, the childless
couple, the people without food, and the kingdom without a king. Travel ages the
embodied, water ages mountains, lack of love ages women, and darts of words age
the mind. Not being studied is the stain of the Vedas. Breaking vows is the
stain of a brahmin. Curiosity is the stain of a good woman. Living away is the
stain of wives. Silver is the stain of gold, tin the stain of silver, lead the
stain of tin, and dirt the stain of lead.

"A man should not try to beat sleep by sleeping, a woman by desire, fire by
fuel, or drink by drinking. The man whose friend is won by gifts, whose enemies
are beaten in battle, and whose wives are won by food and drink, has a life that
bears fruit. Those with a thousand live, and those with a hundred live.
Dhritarashtra, let go of greed. No one fails to live somehow. The man who sees
that all the rice and barley on earth, the gold, the cattle and the women, are
not enough for one man is not confused.

"King, I tell you again. Behave equally towards your sons, king, your own and
the sons of Pandu alike."
