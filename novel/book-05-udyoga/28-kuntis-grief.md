# Kunti's Grief

Krishna rose at dawn and did all the morning rites. Then, with the brahmins'
leave, he set out for the city. The people of Vrikasthala took leave of the
strong-armed one as he went, king, and all turned back.

All the sons of Dhritarashtra except Duryodhana went out to meet him, finely
dressed, with Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the rest. Crowds of townspeople went out
too, king, eager to see Hrishikesha, some in all kinds of carriages and others
on foot. On the road he met Bhishma, whose deeds never failed, and Drona, and
the sons of Dhritarashtra, and with them all around him he went on to the city.

The city had been decorated to honour Krishna, and the royal roads were strewn
with jewels. No one stayed at home, king, bull among the Bharatas, not woman,
nor old man, nor child, so eager were they all to see Vasudeva. When Hrishikesha
entered, the crowd on the royal road was so great, king, that the people could
not find room to stand on the ground. Even the great houses were so crowded with
fine women that they seemed to sway under the weight, and to be about to fall to
the earth. Vasudeva's horses, swift as they were, could not move, for the road
was full of men.

The destroyer of enemies, lotus-eyed Keshava, entered Dhritarashtra's white
palace, set among its tall buildings. He passed through the three courts of the
king's house, and the tamer of enemies came to King Dhritarashtra, son of
Vichitravirya.

As the Dasharha came in, the famous king, whose eyes were his wisdom, rose with
Drona and Bhishma. Kripa, Somadatta and great King Bahlika all rose from their
seats in honour of Janardana. The Varshneya went up to glorious King
Dhritarashtra and to Bhishma, and at once greeted them with kind words. When the
slayer of Madhu had done them honour in the order that dharma lays down, Madhava
met the kings there, each as his age required. Then Janardana met Drona and his
son, glorious Bahlika, Kripa and Somadatta.

There was a great golden seat there, splendid and polished. At Dhritarashtra's
order Achyuta sat down on it. Dhritarashtra's priests brought Janardana a cow,
the honey mixture and water, in the proper way. When Govinda had been welcomed,
he sat among the Kurus, joking with them all and talking of their ties of kin.
When he had been honoured and received by Dhritarashtra, the famous tamer of
enemies took his leave of the king and went out. When he had met the Kurus in
the Kuru hall, as was fitting, Madhava went to Vidura's lovely house.

Vidura came to Janardana with every good wish and honoured the Dasharha, and
offered him everything he could want. When Govinda had been welcomed, Vidura,
who knew every dharma, asked the slayer of Madhu how the sons of Pandu were.
Vidura was his friend and loved him. He was learned and wise, always keeping to
dharma, and free of faults. The Dasharha, who saw everything as if with his own
eyes, told the steward in full all that the Pandavas had been doing.

In the afternoon, when he had been with Vidura, Janardana went to see his
father's sister. When Pritha saw Krishna coming, bright as the clear sun, she
threw her arms round his neck and wept, remembering her sons. It had been a long
time since she had seen Govinda the Varshneya, her sons' companion, and she
wept. When Krishna, lord of fighters, had been welcomed and sat down, she spoke
to him, her face drawn and her voice choked with tears.

"From their childhood they have loved serving their elders," she said. "They are
friends to each other and they are honoured, and they think alike. They were
thrown out of their kingdom by a trick, though they deserved to live among
people, and they went into the empty forest. They have mastered anger and joy,
and they honour the brahmins and speak the truth. When the sons of Pritha went
to the forest, they left all they loved and all their pleasures, and they left
me weeping, and they tore my heart out by the roots.

"How did the great-souled Pandavas live in the great forest, Keshava, full of
lions, tigers and elephants, my son? They did not deserve it. They were children
when they lost their father, and I always brought them up tenderly. How did they
live in the great forest without seeing their father or their mother? From their
childhood, Keshava, the Pandavas were woken by the sound of conches and drums,
by tabors and flutes. At home they were always woken by the trumpeting of
elephants, the neighing of horses and the rumbling of chariot wheels. They were
honoured by the twice-born with the sound of conches and kettledrums, with
flutes and lutes playing along, and with blessings for a holy day. They honoured
the twice-born with clothes, jewels and ornaments. The great brahmins gave them
blessings, and those worth honouring, when they were honoured, praised them and
welcomed them. They slept on blankets and deerskins at the tops of the palaces,
and were woken there. Surely they cannot sleep, Janardana, when they hear the
cries of the wild beasts in the great forest. They did not deserve that. They
were woken by kettledrums and tabors, by conches and flutes, by women singing
sweetly, slayer of Madhu, and by bards, heralds and sutas singing their praises.
How could they be woken in the great forest by the howling of beasts?

"He is modest, firm in truth and self-controlled. He pities all beings. He has
mastered desire and hate, and he follows the path of the good. He carries the
heavy load that the royal seers of old carried, Ambarisha and Mandhatri, Yayati
and Nahusha, Bharata and Dilipa and Shibi, son of Ushinara. He is rich in good
character and good conduct. He knows dharma and he keeps his word. He has every
virtue, a king who could be king of the three worlds. He has no enemy, his soul
is dharma, and he shines like pure gold. He is the best of all the Kurus in
dharma, in learning and in conduct. He is handsome and long-armed. How is
Yudhishthira, Krishna?

"He has the strength of ten thousand elephants and the speed of the wind. He
cannot bear a wrong, and he is always kind to his brother and dear to him. He
killed Kichaka and his kin, slayer of Madhu. The hero killed the Krodhavashas,
and Hidimba and Baka. In courage he is Shakra's equal, in speed the wind's, in
anger Maheshvara's. Bhima is the best of fighters. He has put away his anger,
his strength and his resentment. He is master of himself, and though he cannot
bear a wrong, the son of Pandu stays under his brother's orders. He is a mass of
fire, a great soul, a flood of strength, and his power has no measure. He is
terrible even to look at. Tell me, Janardana, Varshneya, how is Vrikodara now,
the middle Pandava, with arms like iron bars, Achyuta?

"With his two arms, Krishna, he always rivals the Arjuna of old, who had a
thousand arms, Keshava. He shoots five hundred arrows at a single draw. In
archery the son of Pandu is the equal of King Kartavirya. In splendour he is
like the sun, in self-mastery like a great seer, in patience like the earth, and
in courage like great Indra. By his courage, slayer of Madhu, the Kurus won
their great, shining, famous empire among all the kings. The Kauravas look up to
the terrible strength of his arms. He is the best of all chariot fighters, the
son of Pandu, and his courage is true. He is the Pandavas' refuge, as Vasava is
the gods'. How is Dhananjaya now, your brother and your friend?

"Sahadeva is kind to all beings. Modesty restrains him, and he knows the great
weapons. He is gentle and tender, righteous and dear to me. He is a great
archer, brave and a light in any gathering. He serves his brothers, Krishna, and
he is young and wise in dharma and gain. His brothers always praise the conduct
of good, great-hearted Sahadeva, slayer of Madhu. Tell me, Varshneya, of the
hero Sahadeva, lord of fighters, son of Madri, who honours his elders and serves
me.

"He is tender, young, brave and handsome, the son of Pandu, dear to all his
brothers, Krishna, like their own life walking about outside them. Nakula fights
in many ways. He is a great archer and very strong. Is my child well, Krishna,
who grew up in comfort? He was used to comfort and did not deserve sorrow. He is
tender and a great chariot fighter. Shall I ever see Nakula again, strong-armed
one? I could not be happy, hero, when I was apart from Nakula for as long as it
takes to blink. And now look at me. I am still alive.

"Dearer to me than all my sons, Janardana, is Draupadi. She is well born, of
good character and rich in every virtue. She keeps to the truth. She chose her
husbands' worlds over her sons' worlds, left her dear sons behind, and followed
the Pandavas. She comes of a great family, and she was honoured with everything
she could wish for. She is a queen, and all good things are hers. How is
Draupadi, Achyuta? She has five brave husbands, like fires, fierce fighters and
great archers, and Draupadi has had nothing but sorrow. For fourteen years now,
tamer of enemies, I have not seen truthful Draupadi, worn down with longing for
her sons. Surely a person does not win happiness by good deeds, if Draupadi, who
has lived as she has, does not win lasting happiness.

"Bibhatsu is not dearer to me than Krishnaa, nor Yudhishthira, nor Bhimasena,
nor the twins, since I saw her in the hall. Nothing sadder than that has ever
happened to me. Draupadi was in the sheltered rooms, near her husband's family.
The base man, who follows anger and greed, had her brought out, and all the
Kurus looked at her in the hall in her single garment. Dhritarashtra was there,
and great King Bahlika, Kripa and Somadatta, and the Kurus were weary of it. Of
all in that gathering I honour the steward. A man is noble by his conduct, not
by his wealth or his learning. The character of the steward, Krishna, that deep
and great-minded man, is his ornament, and it holds up the worlds."

She was grieving, and she was glad to see Govinda come. She told him all her
many sorrows.

"The old kings did these things before, tamer of enemies," she said, "the dicing
and the killing of deer. Did they ever bring any of them happiness? It burns me,
Krishna, that Krishnaa was tormented by the sons of Dhritarashtra in the hall,
in front of the Kurus. It was so wrong. The banishment from the city, the exile,
tamer of enemies, the life in disguise, and my children kept from me, Keshava: I
am the home of every kind of sorrow, Janardana. It would not be the worst of my
troubles, tamer of enemies, to be with my sons, cheated by Duryodhana. And this
is the fourteenth year.

"If the fruit of merit is used up by sorrow, then after sorrow there should be
happiness. I never made any difference between the sons of Dhritarashtra and the
Pandavas. By that truth, Krishna, may I see you come out of this war safe, with
the enemy killed and fortune around you, you and the Pandavas. They cannot be
beaten, for their spirit is what it is.

"I blame no one but my father, not myself and not Suyodhana. He handed me over
to Kuntibhoja as gamblers hand over money. I was a child playing with a ball
when your grandfather gave me away to Kuntibhoja, a friend to a great-hearted
friend. So I was cheated by my father, tamer of enemies, and by my husband's
family, and I have been utterly miserable, Krishna. What is the good of my life?

"On the night Savyasachin was born, a voice said to me, 'Your son will conquer
the earth, and his fame will touch heaven. When he has killed the Kurus in a
great battle and won the kingdom, Dhananjaya, son of Kunti, will perform three
great sacrifices with his brothers.' I do not doubt that voice. Homage to
Dharma, the creator, and to great Krishna, always. Dharma holds up all
creatures. If there is dharma, Varshneya, it will come true. And you, Krishna,
will bring it all about.

"Madhava, widowhood, loss of wealth and enmity do not grieve me as much as being
apart from my sons. When I do not see Dhananjaya, the Gandiva bowman, the best
of all who bear weapons, what peace can my heart have? This is the fourteenth
year, Govinda, that I have not seen Yudhishthira, Dhananjaya, the twins or
Vrikodara. People hold funeral rites for those who are lost, as if they were
dead. For all that it matters, Janardana, they are dead to me and I am dead to
them.

"Madhava, tell King Yudhishthira, whose soul is dharma, 'Your dharma is wasting
away. Do not let it go for nothing, my son.' Shame on me, Vasudeva, living as I
do on others. Better to have no footing at all than a living won by begging.

"Then tell Dhananjaya, and Vrikodara, who is always ready, 'The time has come
for which a kshatriya woman bears a son. If you let the time pass now that it
has come, then good men as you are, respected by the world, you will do a very
cruel thing. And if you do a cruel thing, I shall leave you for ever. When the
time comes, a man must give up even his life.'

"And tell the sons of Madri, who always love the kshatriya's dharma, 'Choose
pleasures won by courage over life itself. Wealth won by courage gives lasting
joy to the heart of a man who lives by the kshatriya's dharma, best of men.'

"Go, strong-armed one, and tell Arjuna, the Pandava hero, the best of all who
bear weapons, 'Walk in Draupadi's footsteps.' You know very well that when Bhima
and Arjuna are angry they are like Death himself. They could send even the gods
to their last end. It was an insult to them that Krishnaa was taken into the
hall, and that Duhshasana and Karna spoke harsh words to her. Duryodhana
insulted proud Bhimasena in front of the leading Kurus. He will see what comes
of it. Once Vrikodara has an enemy, he does not calm down. Bhima's enmity does
not die down, however long it takes, until the destroyer of enemies has made an
end of his foes.

"The loss of the kingdom was no sorrow to me, nor the defeat at the dice, nor my
sons' exile. That is not what grieves me. But that the tall, dark woman went
into the hall in a single garment, and heard harsh words: what could be sadder
than that? She was in her period, the lovely woman who always loves the
kshatriya's dharma, and Krishnaa had husbands, but found no protector.

"I and my sons have you as our protector, slayer of Madhu, and Rama, the best of
the strong, and the great chariot fighter Pradyumna. And yet I bear such grief
today, best of men, while Bhima lives, whom no one can hold back, and Vijaya,
who never runs away."

Then Shauri, the friend of Pritha's son, comforted his father's sister Pritha,
who was grieving and drowned in longing for her sons.

"What woman in the worlds is like you, aunt?" he said. "You are the daughter of
King Shura, and you came into the family of Ajamidha. You are of a great family,
and you came from one great house to another, like a stream from one lake to
another. You are a queen, and all good things are yours. Your husband honoured
you above all. You are the mother of heroes and the wife of a hero, and you have
every virtue. A wise woman like you should bear happiness and sorrow alike.

"Sleep and sloth, anger and joy, hunger and thirst, cold and heat: the sons of
Pritha have mastered all these. They are heroes, and they always delight in a
hero's happiness. The sons of Pritha have given up the comforts of common men.
They always love a hero's happiness. They are full of energy and very strong,
and they would not be content with a little. Steady men seek the extremes, the
greatest hardships and pleasures beyond the human. Those who love the comforts
of common men seek the middle. The steady delight in the extremes, not in the
middle. They say that to reach the extreme is happiness, and that what lies
between the extremes is sorrow.

"The Pandavas and Krishnaa greet you. They tell you they are well, and they ask
after your health. Soon you will see the Pandavas well, with all their aims
achieved, lords of the whole world, their enemies killed and fortune around
them."

When she had been comforted like this, Kunti, who had been crushed by longing
for her sons, held back the darkness that came from her grief, and answered
Janardana.

"Whatever is good for them, strong-armed slayer of Madhu, do in whatever way you
think best, Krishna, without losing dharma and without trickery, scorcher of
enemies. I know your power, Krishna, and your truth and your birth, your
judgement among friends, your wisdom and your courage. You are dharma in our
family. You are truth, and you are great austerity. You are the protector, you
are the great Brahman, and everything rests on you. As you say, so it is, and in
you it will come true."

Strong-armed Govinda took his leave of her, walked round her keeping her on his
right, and set out for Duryodhana's house.

**Divergences.**

* **The road into the city.** A long rejected passage (\*394) repeats Duryodhana's rest halls on the road and Krishna passing them by. The CE tells it once, at 5.83.18. Not narrated.
* **"The time has come for which a kshatriya woman bears a son"** is CE, Kunti's message to Arjuna and Bhima (5.88.74).
* **Kunti and Karna.** Kunti says she was handed to Kuntibhoja as a child (5.88.61–62). She says nothing here of Karna.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*395–\*404, which add courtesies, repeat Kunti's sorrows and extend her praise of Krishna.
