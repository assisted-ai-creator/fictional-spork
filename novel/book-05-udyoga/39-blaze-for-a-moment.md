# Blaze for a Moment

Krishna went into her house, greeted her feet, and told her briefly what had
happened in the Kuru assembly.

"Many things were said, full of reasons and worth taking," said Vasudeva, "by
the seers and by me. But he did not take them. All these men who follow
Duryodhana are ripe for death. I take my leave of you. I shall go quickly to the
Pandavas. What shall I say to the Pandavas from you? Tell me, wise one. I want
to hear your words."

"Keshava," said Kunti, "tell King Yudhishthira, whose soul is dharma, 'Your
dharma is wasting away. Do not let it go for nothing, my son. Your mind is like
that of a dull, unwise scholar of the Veda, dazed by recitation. It looks only
to one kind of dharma. My dear, look at dharma as the Self-born made it. The
kshatriya was made from his chest, to live by the strength of his arms, always
for hard work, in the protection of his people.

"'Hear an example I heard from the old. Long ago Vaishravana was pleased with
the royal seer Muchukunda and gave him this earth. But he did not take it. "I
want to enjoy a kingdom won by the strength of my arms," he said. Then
Vaishravana was pleased and amazed. King Muchukunda then ruled the earth well,
won by the strength of his arms, keeping to the kshatriya's dharma.

"'Of the dharma that people practise when their king protects them well, the
king gets a quarter, Bharata. If the king practises dharma, he is fit to become
a god. If he practises adharma, he goes to hell. The science of the rod, applied
properly by its master, holds the four classes to their own dharma and keeps
them from adharma. When the king keeps to the science of the rod fully and
properly, then the best of times, the age called Krita, comes about. Do not
doubt whether time makes the king or the king makes time. The king makes the
time. The king creates the Krita age, the Treta and the Dvapara, and the king is
the cause of the fourth age. For causing the Krita age a king enjoys heaven
without end. For causing the Treta he enjoys heaven, but not without end. For
bringing on the Dvapara he enjoys his share of it. But the king who does evil
lives in hell for years without end. The world is touched by the king's faults,
and he by the world's.

"'Look to the king's dharma, which is fitting for your father and your
grandfather. This is not the way of the royal seers, the way you want to stand
in. No king mixed with weakness and set on gentleness has ever won any of the
fruit that comes from protecting his people. Neither Pandu nor I nor your
grandfather ever wished for you the understanding with which you now act. I
always wished for you sacrifice, gifts, austerity and courage, children,
greatness, strength and vigour. Always, when they were rightly worshipped, the
gods of men gave svaha and svadha, long life, wealth and sons. The ancestors and
the gods always wish for giving, study, sacrifice and the protection of the
people from their sons. Whether it is dharma or adharma, you were born to it.
You are learned men, born in a good family, my son, and you are suffering for
want of a living.

"'What dharma is higher than this, that the hungry who wander the earth come to
a brave lord of gifts, and go away satisfied? A righteous man who has won a
kingdom should win over one man by gifts, another by force, and another by kind
words, and draw them in from every side. The brahmin should beg, the kshatriya
protect, the vaishya earn wealth and the shudra serve them. Begging is forbidden
to you, and farming does not suit you. You are a kshatriya, a protector from
harm, who lives by the strength of his arms. Strong-armed one, raise up again
your father's share, which has sunk, by conciliation, gifts, division, the rod
and good policy.

"'What could be sadder than this, that I, who bore you, gladdener of your
friends, have lost my kin and look to others for my bread? Fight by the king's
dharma. Do not sink your grandfathers. Do not go, with your brothers, your merit
spent, to an evil end.'

"There is an old story they tell about this, scorcher of enemies, a conversation
between Vidura and her son. You should tell him from it what is good, and what
is more. There was a famous woman named Vidura. She was proud and quick to
anger, born of a good family and bright. She loved the kshatriya's dharma. She
was fortunate and far-sighted, known in the assemblies of kings, a woman who had
heard the scriptures and was very learned. This truthful Vidura rebuked her own
son, who had been beaten by the king of Sindhu and lay there downcast. He was no
joy to her. He did not know dharma, and he was a joy to his enemies.

"'You are not my son, nor your father's,' she said. 'Where have you come from?
You have no fire, you are no one worth counting, a man with the means of a
eunuch. You are without hope for as long as you live. Carry your load, for your
good. Do not despise yourself. Do not keep yourself on so little. Make up your
mind to what is good, and do not be afraid. Stand firm. Get up, coward. Do not
lie there like that, beaten, gladdening all your enemies, without honour,
bringing grief to your kin. A little stream fills easily. A mouse's paws fill
easily. A coward is easily content, and pleased with very little.

"'Pull out your enemy's fang and meet your death at once. Or if your life is in
doubt, show your courage. Circle round like a hawk and look for your enemy's
weak place, crying out or silent, fearless in the sky. Why do you lie there like
a corpse, like a man struck by lightning? Get up, coward. Do not lie there like
that, beaten. Do not go down in misery. Make yourself known by your deeds. Do
not be in the middle, or at the back, or down below. Stand up in your strength.

"'Blaze for a moment, like a brand of tinduka wood. Do not be like a fire of
chaff, without a flame, smouldering because you want to live on. It is better to
blaze for a moment than to smoulder for a long time. Let no one be born in any
king's house who is as soft as a donkey. When a man has done the work of a man,
and gone into battle as far as he can, he pays his debt to dharma and does not
blame himself. The wise do not grieve whether they win or not. They go on with
the next thing to do, and do not cling to their lives.

"'Show your courage, or go the sure road. Why do you live, you who have put
dharma behind you, son? Your sacrifices and your good works, eunuch, and all
your fame are gone. The root of your enjoyments is cut. Why do you live? A man
who is sinking should seize his enemy by the leg as he falls. Even when his
roots are cut, he should never despair. He should lift up the load and pull it,
remembering what the thoroughbred does. Be firm and proud. Know your own
manhood. Raise up your family, which has sunk because of you.

"'A man whose great and wonderful deeds people do not talk of only adds to the
count. He is neither woman nor man. A man whose fame is not spread for giving,
for austerity, for courage, for learning or for winning wealth is just his
mother's excrement. The man who surpasses other people by his learning, his
austerity, his fortune or his courage, by his deeds, is a man. You should not
look for a beggar's life, mean, cruel, shameful and wretched, fit for cowards.
Enemies are glad at a man who is thin, despised by the world, poorly fed and
poorly clothed, who is pleased at small gains, wretched, living little and
small. A kinsman does not prosper when he has a kinsman like that.

"'We shall perish for want of a living, driven from the kingdom, without
anything we could wish for or enjoy, thrown out of our place, with nothing. You
are a disgrace among the good and a destroyer of your family and line. Sanjaya,
I gave birth to strife in the name of a son. Let no woman ever bear a son like
this, without resentment, without spirit, without courage, a joy to his enemies.
Do not smoulder. Blaze out. Attack and kill your enemies. Blaze on the heads of
your enemies, even for a moment, for an instant.

"'A man is only a man as long as he cannot bear a wrong and will not forgive it.
The man who forgives and does not resent is neither woman nor man. Contentment
kills fortune, and so does pity, and so do sloth and fear. A man who does not
strive does not win anything great. Free yourself by your own effort from these
evils that bring defeat. Make your heart iron and seek what is yours again. He
is called a man, purusha, because he can bear the city, pura. They say that a
man who lives here like a woman has a name with no meaning. When a brave man,
strong of spirit, who moves with a lion's stride, has gone to his fate, his
people still feast on what he left. The man who puts aside his own pleasure and
comfort and seeks fortune soon brings joy to his ministers.'

"'What good would the whole earth be to you if you could not see me?' said her
son. 'What use would ornaments be to you, or pleasures, or life?'

"'May our enemies go to the worlds of those who ask, "What is there for today?"'
said his mother. 'May our friends go to the worlds of those who are respected.
Do not follow the ways of wretched, spiritless men, who are left by their
servants and live on others' bread. Let brahmins and friends live on you, my
son, as creatures live on the rain, and the gods on Shatakratu. Sanjaya, the
life of a man on whom all beings live, like a tree with ripe fruit, has meaning.
It is good to live, for the hero by whose courage his kin prosper happily, as
the thirty gods by Shakra's. The man who lives by the strength of his own arms
wins fame in this world and a good end in the next.

"'Now, in this state, you want to give up your manhood. You will soon go the
road that the low take. A kshatriya who does not show his fire by his courage,
as far as he can, because he wants to live, is called a thief. Words full of
meaning, fitting and good, do not reach you, any more than medicine reaches a
dying man. The king of Sindhu has many subjects who are not content. They stay
quiet out of weakness, the fools, waiting for a flood of disasters. Others will
turn against him too when they see your manhood, if you gather allies and win
them over here and there. Make a league with them and live in mountain
strongholds, waiting for his time of trouble. He is not free from age and death.

"'Your name is Sanjaya, Victor, and I do not see it in you. Be true to your
name, my son. Do not let it be an empty name. When you were a child, a wise
brahmin who saw clearly said of you, "He will fall into great trouble, and then
he will rise again." I remember his words and hope for your victory. So I speak
to you, my son, and I shall speak again and again. When a man's gains satisfy
others, and he follows his aims by good policy, his success is certain. Whether
there is plenty or want, Sanjaya, knowing that it was so for my ancestors too,
set your mind on war. Do not draw back.

"'Shambara said there is no worse state than one where no meal can be seen,
either today or tomorrow. He said that this is worse than the death of a husband
or a son. What is called poverty is a slow death. I was born in a great family,
and I came from one great house to another like a stream from lake to lake. I
was a queen with every good thing, and my husband honoured me above all. My
friends saw me before in costly garlands and ornaments, in fine clean clothes,
and they have seen me brought very low. When you see me and your wife very weak,
Sanjaya, life will be no use to you. When you see our slaves, workers and
servants, our teachers, our priests and our chaplains leaving us because we have
no living, what use will your life be?

"'If I do not see you do something now, as you used to, worth praise and fame,
what peace can my heart have? If I have to say no to the brahmins, my heart will
break. For neither I nor my husband ever said no to a brahmin. We are those
others rely on, not those who rely on others. If I have to live relying on
others, I shall give up my life. Be a shore for us where there is no shore, a
boat for us where there is no boat. Make a place for us where there is no place.
Bring us back to life from the dead.

"'All your enemies can be beaten, if you do not want to live at any price. But
if you take up this eunuch's way of life, then with a sick heart and a broken
mind, give up this wretched living. A hero becomes famous by killing a single
enemy. Indra became great Indra by killing Vritra alone. He won the great
Indra's cup and became lord of the worlds. When a hero calls out his name in
battle and challenges his enemies in their armour, or breaks the front of an
army, or kills a great man, when he wins great fame by fighting well, then his
enemies tremble and bow to him. Cowards, helpless, fill the brave man who gives
up his life in battle with all the riches he could want. Good men make an end of
the enemy they have tricked, whether they win a kingdom at great risk or their
life is in doubt.

"'Think of the kingdom as a gate to heaven, or as nectar. It is shut up in one
place. Fall like a firebrand on your enemies. Kill your enemies in battle, king.
Keep to your own dharma. Never let a rich enemy see you utterly wretched. Let me
never see you, wretched as I am, in a wretched state, with our people grieving
around you and our enemies shouting. Enjoy yourself with the girls of Sauvira,
and be proud of your wealth as before. Do not fall into the power of the
Saindhava girls, when we have not been there.

"'When a young man like you, handsome, learned and well born, famous and known
in the world, turns aside like an ox that should carry the yoke, I think it is
death itself. If I see you speaking sweetly to another man, or walking behind
him, what peace can my heart have? No one born in this family has ever walked
behind another. You should not live under another man's yoke, my son. I know the
heart of a kshatriya, which lasts for ever, as those who went before and those
before them have told it, and those after and those after them. A kshatriya born
here who knows the kshatriya's dharma should never bow down to anyone out of
fear, or out of concern for his living. He should stand up and not bend, for
effort is manhood. He should break at the joint rather than bend to anyone here.
He should go about like a great elephant in rut, great of heart. He should
always bow to the brahmins, Sanjaya, and to dharma only. He should keep the
other classes in order and strike down all wrongdoers. With allies or without
them, he should be like this all his life.'"

**Divergences.**

* **The mother's name.** The CE names her *vidurā* (5.131.4). Ganguli, following the vulgate, calls her Vidula, the name usually given to this story. The novel follows the CE, and to keep her apart from Vidura, the Kuru steward, calls her "a famous woman named Vidura" when she enters and "the mother" or "his mother" after that.
* **The son's name.** Her son is named Sanjaya (5.131.27; 5.132.7). He is not Sanjaya the bard.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*500–\*506, which add Kunti's image of the kshatriyas burned by Arjuna's fire, the king who brings on Kali, and further lines for the mother.

**Choices.**
