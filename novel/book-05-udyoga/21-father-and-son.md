# Father and Son

When he had heard Sanjaya's words, the king whose eyes were his understanding
began to weigh them for their good and their bad. He weighed the good and the
bad closely, and wisely, as his judgement truly showed him, wanting victory for
his sons. He settled truly the strength and weakness of each side. Then the lord
of men began to reckon their powers. He judged the Pandavas stronger in divine
and human power and in fire, and the Kurus weaker in power. Then he spoke to
Duryodhana.

"Duryodhana, this worry of mine never ceases. I think this is true, and I see it
for myself, not by guessing. All beings love their children most, and do what is
dear and good for them as far as they can. We see that it is mostly the same
with those who have helped us. The good wish to repay a great kindness many
times. Agni will surely be Arjuna's helper in this terrible meeting of Kurus and
Pandavas, remembering what was done at Khandava. And Dharma and the other
dwellers in heaven, called by the Pandavas, will come many times, and with the
love of fathers. I think they will come in fury, to guard them from fear of
Bhishma, Drona and Kripa, a fury like the thunderbolt.

"With the gods beside them, the Parthas cannot be faced by any man. They are
tigers among men, full of courage, masters of weapons. His heavenly bow, the
Gandiva, is the best and cannot be withstood. He has two great heavenly quivers
from Varuna, full of arrows, that never run out. His heavenly monkey banner
moves like smoke and never catches on anything. His chariot has no equal in
splendour on the earth, bounded by the four seas. People hear its roar like a
great cloud, a sound like a great thunderbolt that terrifies enemies. The whole
world holds his courage to be more than human. The kings know him as the
conqueror even of the gods in battle. He is seen, as it were, to sow five
hundred arrows in the blink of an eye, and to strike far.

"Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and Drona's son, and Shalya king of the Madras, and the
men who stand between, all say that the Partha, standing ready for battle, the
tiger of chariot fighters, tamer of enemies, cannot be beaten even by more than
human kings. He shoots five hundred arrows at a single draw, and the strength of
the Pandava's arms is like Kartavirya's. I seem to see Arjuna, the great archer,
guarded by great Indra and by Vishnu, killing in this great, crushing battle.

"Thinking of all this, Bharata, day and night, I cannot sleep and I cannot be
happy, worrying about peace for the Kurus. This great ruin of the Kurus has come
upon us. There is no end to this quarrel but peace. I want peace with the
Parthas always, my son, not war. For I think the Pandavas are always stronger
than the Kurus."

---

When he heard his father's words, the unforgiving son of Dhritarashtra grew very
angry, and spoke again.

"You think the Parthas cannot be beaten because they have the gods as allies.
Let that fear go, best of kings. The gods won their godhood by being free of
desire and hatred, of treachery and greed, and by looking on things without
taking part. So Dvaipayana Vyasa, and Narada, great in austerity, and Rama
Jamadagnya told us long ago. The gods never act as men do, out of desire, greed,
pity or hate, bull of the Bharatas. If Agni and Vayu, Dharma, Indra and the
Ashvins acted out of desire, the Parthas would never have come to sorrow. So you
should never worry about this. The gods always stand apart from such things,
Bharata. And if desire, hatred and greed are seen in the gods, then their divine
authority will not prevail.

"Charmed by me, the fire always speaks well of me, though it wants to burn all
the worlds and ring them round on every side. Know, Bharata, that the highest
power, by which the dwellers in heaven are joined, is mine too, and greater than
the gods', without equal. When the earth is splitting apart, and the peaks of
mountains, I hold them in place by my charms, king, while the world looks on.
The great shower of stones and the terrible wind with its great roar, which rise
to destroy all this, the living and the lifeless, the moving and the still, I
always calm here, while the world looks on, out of pity for creatures. Chariots
and foot soldiers go over waters I have made solid. I alone set going the states
of gods and asuras. Wherever I go with my akshauhinis on some business, the
waters flow for me wherever I wish.

"There are no dangers in my country, king, no snakes and the rest. Terrible
creatures, lulled by me, do no harm. The rain cloud rains as much as the people
of my country want, king. All my people are righteous, and there are no plagues.
The Ashvins, Vayu and Agni, the slayer of Vritra with the Maruts, and Dharma
dare not protect those I hate. If they could protect my enemies by their power,
the Parthas would not have suffered for thirteen years. Neither gods nor
gandharvas nor asuras nor rakshasas can protect the one I hate. I tell you this
truly.

"Whatever I always wish, good or bad, for friend or enemy, has never failed
before. When I say, 'This will be,' scorcher of enemies, it has never turned out
otherwise. They know me as one whose word is true. The world is witness to my
greatness, famous in every direction. I have spoken of it to comfort you, king,
not to boast. I have never been a boaster, king. It is the conduct of the wicked
to praise oneself. You will hear that the Pandavas and the Matsyas, the
Panchalas with the Kekayas, Satyaki and Vasudeva have been beaten by me. As
rivers reach the sea and are lost there entirely, so they and their kin will
perish when they meet me. My understanding is higher, my fire higher, my courage
highest. My learning is higher, my yoga higher. Mine is better than theirs.
Whatever Grandfather, Drona, Kripa, Shalya and Shala know of weapons, all of it
is in me."

When he had said this, Bharata, the tamer of enemies, eager to fight, questioned
Sanjaya again, knowing what the time required.

---

But Karna paid no heed to the son of Vichitravirya as he went on asking so much
about the Parthas, and spoke to the son of Dhritarashtra, gladdening him, in the
assembly of the Kauravas.

"Long ago I took the brahma weapon from Rama by making a false claim. When he
found out, he told me, 'At the time of your death it will not come to your
mind.' Though my fault was great, the great seer, my teacher, cursed me only so
much, for I bowed before him. That great seer of fierce power could burn up the
earth with its seas. I won his favour with my service and my own manliness. So
that weapon is still left to me. So I am able to do this. The burden is mine.

"Having won that seer's favour for as long as a blink of the eye, I will kill
the Panchalas, the Karushas and the Matsyas, and the Parthas with their sons and
grandsons, and I will reach the worlds won by weapons. Let Grandfather stay by
your side, and Drona, and all the chief kings. I will go with the main strength
of the army and kill the Parthas. The burden is mine."

As he said this, Bhishma said to him, "Why do you boast, Karna, your mind
already in the grip of Time? Do you not know that when the chief is killed, the
sons of Dhritarashtra will be killed? When you heard what Dhananjaya did, with
Krishna beside him, when he had the Khandava burned, you and your kin should
have kept yourselves in check. And the spear that great Indra, the blessed lord
of the thirty, gave you, you will see it struck by Keshava's discus, burned to
ashes, fallen and shattered. That arrow of yours with a serpent's mouth, which
gleams there, always honoured carefully with the finest garlands, will perish
with you, Karna, struck by the Pandava's floods of arrows. Vasudeva, the killer
of Bana and of Bhauma, guards the wearer of the diadem, Karna, he who kills
enemies like you and greater ones in the thick of the tumult."

"No doubt the lord of the Vrishnis is as you say," said Karna, "and more than
that, great as he is. But as for the harsh words Grandfather has spoken to me,
let him hear what comes of them. I lay down my weapons. Grandfather will never
see me in battle, only in the hall. When you are at peace, all the kings on
earth will see my power."

When he had said this, the great archer left the hall and went to his own house.
But Bhishma laughed, king, and said to Duryodhana in the midst of the Kurus,
"The suta's son is true to his promises, they say. How then could he bear the
burden? Form your arrays and counter-arrays, split heads, and watch the world
destroyed by Bhimasena. While the king of Avanti, the Kalinga, Jayadratha,
Vedidhvaja and Bahlika stand, I will always kill the enemy's fighters by
thousands and tens of thousands. When the wretch Vaikartana made that weapon,
calling himself a brahmin before the blessed, blameless Rama, his dharma and his
austerity were lost."

---

When King Bhishma had spoken, and Karna had laid down his weapons and gone,
Duryodhana, the foolish son of Vichitravirya's son, spoke to Shantanu's son.

"All men are alike, all are born the same way. How is it that you think victory
is the Parthas' alone? We are all of the same kind, all born of human wombs,
Grandfather. How do you know victory lies with the Parthas? I do not start this
out of courage relying on you, or on Drona, or on Kripa, or on Bahlika, or on
the other kings. Karna Vaikartana and I, and my brother Duhshasana, will kill
the five Pandavas in battle with sharp arrows. Then, king, I will satisfy the
brahmins with great sacrifices of many kinds, with rich fees, with cattle,
horses and wealth."

"Long ago," said Vidura, "a fowler set a net on the ground to catch birds, my
father, as we have heard from the ancients. Two birds were caught in it at once,
equal in strength. The two sky-goers took up the net and flew off with it. When
the fowler saw them rise into the sky, he ran after them without losing heart,
wherever they went. A sage in his hermitage, who had finished his daily rites,
saw the hunter running like that after the birds, and he asked the man on the
ground, who was chasing those two fliers in the air, in this verse, Kauravya:

"'This seems very strange and wonderful to me, hunter. You are on foot, and you
run after two birds flying in the sky.'

"'Those two are carrying off my one net together,' said the fowler. 'When they
quarrel, they will fall into my power.'

"The two birds, their death already fixed, came to quarrel. Fighting, the two
fools fell to the ground. As they fought in their fury, in the power of the
noose of death, the hunter came up unnoticed and took them. So kinsmen who
quarrel over wealth fall into the power of their enemies, like the two birds,
through their quarrel.

"Eating together, talking together, asking each other's advice and meeting
together are the work of kin, never quarrelling. When all of them, with glad
hearts, wait on the old, they cannot be attacked, like a forest guarded by a
lion. Those who have won lasting wealth and still sit as if they were poor, bull
of the Bharatas, give their fortune away to their enemies. Firebrands smoke when
they are apart, Dhritarashtra, and blaze when they are together. So it is with
kin, bull of the Bharatas.

"I will tell you something else, as I saw it on a mountain. When you have heard
it too, Kauravya, do what is best. We were going to the northern mountain with
some Kiratas, and with brahmins like gods who knew spells, charms and remedies.
The whole of Gandhamadana was like a bower all round, glowing with herbs, where
siddhas and gandharvas live. There we all saw honey, not made by bees, set on a
steep cliff above a sheer drop, as much as a pot would hold. It was guarded by
poisonous snakes and very dear to Kubera. A mortal who eats it becomes immortal,
the blind get their sight, and the old become young. So the brahmins who knew
the remedies told us. Then the Kiratas saw it and went for it, lord of the
earth, and they perished in that terrible, snake-filled mountain gorge.

"In the same way your son wants the whole earth for himself alone. In his
delusion he sees the honey, and not the fall. Duryodhana wants to fight the
Left-handed Archer in battle, and I do not see in him such fire or such courage.
The hero who conquered the earth with a single chariot is waiting, and bears
with your looks. Drupada, the king of the Matsyas and Dhananjaya in his fury
would leave nothing in battle, like fires driven by the wind. Take King
Yudhishthira into your lap, Dhritarashtra. When two fight a war, the victory is
never wholly one side's."

**Divergences.**

* **The insect and Karna's thigh.** The story of how Rama found Karna out, when a worm bored into his thigh while Rama slept with his head in his lap, is CE, but it is told later, in Book 12 (12.3.5–12), and the creature is a worm (*kṛmi*), not a bee. Here Karna says only that he took the brahma weapon "by making a false claim", and that Rama told him it would not come to his mind at his death (5.61.2). Bhishma adds that he called himself a brahmin (5.61.17). See ledger L-177.
* **Karna lays down his weapons.** That Karna will not fight while Bhishma fights is CE (5.61.13). See ledger L-177.
* **Vidura on self-control.** A long appendix passage (App. I No. 3) gives Vidura a sermon on self-control before the fable of the birds. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*353–\*358) add details, including Dhritarashtra calling Duryodhana caught in the noose of death (\*356) and the rout at Virata's city (\*358). Not narrated.
