# Uluka's Message

"When the great Pandavas had camped by the Hiranvati, great king, Bharata," said
Sanjaya, "Duryodhana, with Karna, Subala's son and Duhshasana, called Uluka
aside and said this to him, king of kings.

"'Uluka, son of the gambler, go to the Pandavas and the Somakas. When you get
there, tell them what I say, while Vasudeva listens. "This war of the Pandavas
and the Kurus, which frightens the world, and which we have thought of for years
on end, has come. Son of Kunti, the time has come for that great boasting speech
Sanjaya spoke in the middle of the Kurus. Let everything you promised be done.
Remember your anger, the theft of your kingdom, the forest life and Draupadi's
torment, Pandava, and be a man.

"'"The time for which a kshatriya woman bears a child has come. Show strength,
courage, bravery, the greatest skill with weapons and manhood in battle, and pay
back your anger. Whose heart would not break if he had been tormented and
humbled and had lived long in exile, thrown down from his lordship? Whose anger
would not blaze if he were born in a good family and brave, and someone greedy
for another's wealth attacked and seized his kingdom? Show by deeds those great
words you spoke. The good know a man who boasts and does nothing for a coward.
To leave the power of enemies and to win the kingdom back are the two aims of a
man who fights. So show your manhood. Either beat us and rule this earth, or be
killed by us and go to the world of heroes. Remember your banishment from the
kingdom, the hardship, the forest life, Pandava, and Krishnaa's torment, and be
a man. You were told unpleasant things again and again as you went into exile.
Show your anger today, for anger is manhood. Anger, strength, courage,
knowledge, yoga and skill with weapons: let them be seen here, Partha. Be a man
in battle."

"'And Uluka, tell that beardless, stupid glutton, that ignorant little
Bhimasena, my words again and again. "Vrikodara, the oath you swore in the
middle of the hall, powerless as you were: drink Duhshasana's blood, if you can.
The weapons have been brought out. Kurukshetra is free of mud. Your horses are
fed, your fighters are paid. Fight tomorrow, with Keshava."'

---

"When the gambler's son reached the Pandava's camp, he met the Pandavas and
spoke to Yudhishthira. 'You know the words of envoys. When you hear Duryodhana's
command from me, as I speak it as I was told, you should not be angry.'

"'Uluka, you have nothing to fear,' said Yudhishthira. 'Tell us, untroubled,
what greedy, short-sighted Dhritarashtra's son thinks.'

"Then in the middle of the splendid great Pandavas and all the Srinjayas and
famous Krishna, before Drupada and his sons and Virata, and in the middle of all
the kings, he spoke.

"'This is what great-minded King Duryodhana, Dhritarashtra's son, said to you
while the Kuru heroes listened. Hear it, lord of men. "You were beaten at dice,
and Krishnaa was brought to the hall. A man who thinks himself a man can show
his anger. You were banished from your homes to the forest for twelve years, and
you lived a year as Virata's slaves. Remember your anger, the theft of your
kingdom, the forest life and Draupadi's torment, Pandava, and be a man. And the
oath Bhimasena swore, powerless as he was, Pandava: drink Duhshasana's blood, if
you can. The weapons have been brought out. Kurukshetra is free of mud. The road
is level, the fighters are paid. Fight tomorrow, with Keshava.

"'"Why do you boast before you have met Bhishma in battle, like a fool who wants
to climb Mount Gandhamadana? How can you want the kingdom here, Partha, without
beating Drona in battle, the best of fighters, equal to Shachi's husband in war?
Drona is the teacher of both the Veda and the bow, and knows both. He is a
leader in war, unshakeable, holding the army together, unfallen. Your wish to
beat Drona in battle, Partha, is folly and false. We have never heard of the
wind uprooting Mount Meru. The wind would carry Meru, or the sky fall on the
earth, or the age turn round, if things were as you tell me. What elephant,
horse or man who wanted to live would go home safely again after meeting the
enemy-crushing weapons of those two? How could anyone they have marked down, or
touched with something terrible, get free alive from the battle with his feet on
the ground?

"'"Why, like a frog lying in a well, do you not see this gathered army of kings?
It is hard to attack, like an army of the gods, guarded by lords of men as
heaven is by the thirty gods. It has men of the east, the west, the south and
the north, Kambojas, Shakas and Khashas, Shalvas with Matsyas, men of the Kuru
middle country, mlecchas, Pulindas, Dravidas, Andhras and Kanchyas. That flood
of many peoples has swollen for war like the Ganga's current that cannot be held
back. And you want to fight me, fool of little wit, when I stand in the middle
of my strength of elephants?"'

"When he had said this to King Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, Uluka turned
again to Jishnu and spoke.

"'"Fight without boasting. Why do you boast so much, Arjuna? Success comes by
the proper course. It does not come by boasting. If deeds in the world were done
by boasting, Dhananjaya, everyone would have gained his aim, and the wretched
man would boast plenty. I know you have Vasudeva as your ally. I know your
Gandiva is the length of a palm tree. I know there is no fighter like you. And
knowing it, I take your kingdom. But one does not win great success by the
dharma of the proper course alone. The Ordainer brings creatures under his power
by his mind alone. For thirteen years I have enjoyed the kingdom while you
wailed, and I will rule it again, when I have killed you and your kin.

"'"Where was your Gandiva then, when you were won as the stake of slavery? Where
was Bhimasena's strength then, Phalguna? There was no release for you then from
Bhimasena with his club or from Partha with his Gandiva, except through
faultless Krishnaa. That lovely woman freed you when you had fallen into
slavery, and were tied to work that was not fit for men, settled in slaves'
work. What I said, that you were barren sesame seeds, was true. For Partha wore
a braid in Virata's city then, and Bhimasena wore himself out cooking in
Virata's kitchen, Kunti's son. That is my manhood. Kshatriyas have always
punished a kshatriya who runs away in battle like that, by the girdle, the line
and the braid.

"'"I will not give back the kingdom out of fear of Vasudeva, or of you,
Phalguna. Fight, with Keshava. No magic, no conjuring, no frightening trickery
carries roars against me in battle when I have taken up my weapons. A thousand
Vasudevas or hundreds of Phalgunas, meeting me, whose arrows never fail, will
run off in the ten directions. Go to battle with Bhishma. Break a mountain with
your head. Cross with your arms this very deep ocean of men. Kripa son of
Sharadvat is its vast extent, Vivimshati its shoals of fish, Brihadbala its
swell, Somadatta's son its whale. Duhshasana is its flood, Shala and Shalya its
fish, Sushena and Chitrayudha its elephant-crocodiles. Jayadratha is its rock,
Purumitra its depth, Durmarshana its water, Shakuni its falls. It is a flood of
weapons, inexhaustible and greatly swollen. When you have plunged into it and
your mind is lost in weariness, and all your kin are killed, then your mind will
be filled with regret. Then let your mind turn back from ruling the earth,
Partha, as an impure man's from heaven. For the kingdom is very hard for you to
win and rule, as heaven is for a man without austerity who wants it."'

---

"Uluka spoke again to Arjuna as he had been told, stinging him with the needle
of his words, like an angry poisonous snake. When they heard those words, the
Pandavas were furious. They had been very angry before, and the gambler's son
had insulted them. They could not stay in their seats. They threw their arms
about and looked at one another like angry poisonous snakes. Bhimasena, his head
bowed, looked at Keshava with eyes red at the corners, hissing like a poisonous
snake.

"Seeing the son of the wind deeply hurt and struck by anger, the Dasharha, as if
smiling, answered the gambler's son. 'Go quickly, gambler's son, and tell
Suyodhana, "Your words have been heard and their meaning taken. Let it be as you
think." And you must tell Suyodhana more from me. "Show yourself tomorrow, fool.
Be a man. You think, fool, that Janardana will not fight, since the Parthas have
chosen him as charioteer, and so you are not afraid. If it came to the last
hour, I could burn up all the kings in my anger, as fire burns grass. But at
Yudhishthira's command I will drive the chariot for great Phalguna, who knows
himself, as he fights. If you fly up over the three worlds, or enter the earth,
you will see Arjuna's chariot in front of you there in the morning. And as for
Bhimasena's roaring, which you think is empty, know that Duhshasana's blood is
as good as drunk. Neither Partha nor King Yudhishthira, nor Bhimasena nor the
twins, takes any notice of you and your hostile talk."'

"When the bull of the Bharatas heard Duryodhana's words, he looked at the
gambler's son with very red eyes. Glorious Gudakesha looked at Keshava, raised
his great arm, and spoke to the gambler's son.

"'A man who relies on his own courage and challenges others, and fearlessly
lives up to his power, is called a man. A man who relies on another's courage
and challenges others is a kshatriya in name only, because he is powerless, the
lowest of men in the world. You think others' courage is your own. A coward
yourself and a fool, you want to insult others. You have consecrated the old man
of all the kings, whose mind is set on good, master of his senses and very wise,
for death, and you boast. We know your mind, evil-minded disgrace to your
family. You think the Pandavas will not kill Ganga's son out of pity.
Dhritarashtra's son, the man whose courage you rely on as you boast, I will kill
first, Bhishma, while all the archers watch.

"'Go to the Bharatas, gambler's son, and tell Suyodhana, Dhritarashtra's son,
"Arjuna the left-handed archer says, 'So be it.' When the night ends there will
be battle." The man of undaunted spirit, true to his word, said to you in the
middle of the Kurus to cheer you, "I will kill the Pandavas' army and the
Shalveyakas. That is my task. I could kill the world, except Drona. You have
nothing to fear from the Pandavas." That is why you think the kingdom is surely
won and the Pandavas destroyed. Full of pride, you do not see the harm that is
in yourself. So I will kill the eldest of the Kurus first, before your eyes, in
the battle. At sunrise, with your army yoked, look out for him, with banner and
chariot, and guard the man true to his word. For I will bring Bhishma, your
island, down from his chariot with my arrows while you all watch. Tomorrow
Suyodhana will learn what boasting is, when he sees the grandfather battered by
my net of arrows.

"'And what Bhimasena in anger said in the middle of the hall to your brother
Duhshasana, that short-sighted man, ignorant of dharma, always hostile,
evil-minded and cruel in his deeds, you will soon see kept as a true promise,
Suyodhana. You will soon see the bitter fruit of your conceit, your pride, your
anger and harshness, your hardness and arrogance, your self-regard, your cruelty
and sharpness, your hatred of dharma, your lawlessness, your insults, your
disregard for the old, your crooked view and all your wrong policy, Suyodhana.
When I am angry, with Vasudeva beside me, lord of men, what hope can you have of
life or kingdom, fool? When Bhishma is at peace, and Drona, and the suta's son
brought down, you will have no hope of life, kingdom or sons. When you see your
brothers and sons dead, Suyodhana, and are killed by Bhimasena, you will
remember your evil deeds. Keshava will not make a second promise. I speak the
truth, and all of it will come true.'

"Spoken to like this, king, the gambler's son took in those words, and with
leave he went back the way he had come. Turning back from Pandu's sons, the
gambler's son went to Dhritarashtra's son and told all of it in the Kuru
assembly, as he had been told. When he heard the words of Keshava and Arjuna,
the bull of the Bharatas spoke to Duhshasana, Karna and Shakuni. 'Give the order
to the kings, and to the army and the allies' army, so that the whole host
stands yoked before sunrise.' Then messengers, sent by Karna, went quickly round
the whole army in chariots, on camels and she-mules and on fine, swift horses,
at Karna's command, ordering the kings, 'Yoke before sunrise!'"
