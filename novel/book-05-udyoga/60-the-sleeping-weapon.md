# The Sleeping Weapon

"'Then in the morning, king of kings, when the clear sun had risen,'" said
Bhishma, "'the Bhargava's battle with me began again. Standing in his whirling
chariot, Rama, the best of fighters, rained showers of arrows on me, as Shakra
rains on a mountain. My charioteer, my friend, struck by that rain of arrows,
fell on the chariot floor, and my heart sank. Then my charioteer fell into a
deep faint, and from the blows of the arrows he fell to the ground and lost his
senses. Then, tormented by Rama's arrows, my charioteer gave up his life within
a moment, king of kings, and fear came over me.

"'When my charioteer had been killed, king, and I was shooting arrows with my
mind distracted, Rama sent arrows like death. As I was in distress over my
charioteer and reeling, the Bhargava drew his strong bow powerfully and struck
me hard with an arrow. That blood-drinking arrow fell between my collarbones,
king, and went with me to the ground, king of kings. Thinking I was killed, bull
of the Bharatas, Rama thundered aloud like a cloud and rejoiced again and again.
When I had fallen like that, king, Rama, full of joy, gave a great shout with
his followers. The Kauravas who were at my side, and the people who had come
wanting to see the battle, were in great distress when I fell.

"'When I had fallen, lion among kings, I saw eight brahmins like the sun and
fire. They stood round me on every side and held me up in their arms in the
middle of the battle. Guarded by those brahmins, I did not touch the ground. I
stayed in the air, held by those brahmins like kinsmen, as if asleep in the air,
sprinkled with drops of water. Then, king, those brahmins, holding me, all said
together again and again, "Do not be afraid," and "May all go well with you."
Refreshed by their words, I rose at once and saw my mother, the best of rivers,
standing in my chariot. The great river had held my horses in the battle, lord
of the Kurus. I honoured my mother's feet and mounted the chariot, as
Arshtishena did. She had guarded my chariot, my horses and my gear. I joined my
hands and sent her away again.

"'Then I took up those horses swift as the wind myself and fought Jamadagni's
son until the day ended, Bharata. Then, best of the Bharatas, I loosed in the
battle a swift, powerful arrow at Rama that cut to the heart. Struck down by the
force of the arrow, Rama sank to his knees, dropped his bow and fell into a
faint. When Rama, the giver of many thousands, had fallen, clouds covered the
sky, pouring down much blood. Meteors fell in hundreds, with whirlwinds and
earthquakes. Svarbhanu suddenly covered the blazing sun. Harsh winds blew and
the earth shook. Vultures, badas and herons flew about in joy. In the burning
quarter a jackal howled terribly again and again. Drums sounded loudly without
being struck. These were the terrible portents, best of the Bharatas, when great
Rama fell to the earth, all but senseless.

"'Then the sun, its circle of rays dimmed, set, buried in heaps of dust. Night
came on, with pleasant cool winds, and we both withdrew from the battle. So
there was a withdrawal, king, and then again at dawn the terrible fight began.
So it went day after day for twenty days, and three more days besides.

---

"'Then that night, king of kings, I bowed my head to the brahmins, the
ancestors, all the gods, the creatures that move by night and the night itself,
lord of the people. I went to my bed in a lonely place and thought in my mind,
"This terrible battle of mine with Jamadagni's son, so very harmful, has gone on
for very many days now. I cannot beat mighty Rama, the brahmin Jamadagni's son,
of great courage, at the head of the battle. If I can beat mighty Jamadagni's
son, let the gods be pleased and show it to me tonight."

"'Then, king of kings, I slept that night, wounded by arrows, on my right side,
as dawn came near. Then those chief brahmins who had lifted me up when I fell
from my chariot, held me and comforted me, saying, "Do not be afraid," came to
me in a dream, great king, surrounded me and spoke. Hear it, upholder of the
Kurus.

"'"Rise, Ganga's son. Do not be afraid. You have nothing to fear. We protect
you, tiger among men, for you are our own body. Rama son of Jamadagni will never
beat you in battle. You will beat Rama in battle, bull of the Bharatas. You will
recognise this very dear weapon, for you knew it in a former body. It belongs to
Prajapati, made by Vishvakarman, and is named Prasvapa, the sleeper, Bharata.
Not even Rama knows it, nor any man on earth. Remember it, strong-armed one, and
use it with force. Rama will not be destroyed by that weapon, lord of men, and
you will never be touched by sin, giver of honour. Jamadagni's son will sleep,
struck down by the strength of your arrows. When you have beaten him, you will
wake him again with the dear weapon of waking, Bhishma, in the battle. Do this
in the morning, Kauravya, standing in your chariot. We think sleeping and dead
are the same. Rama must never die, lord of the earth. So use this Prasvapa that
has come to you." Having said this, king, all those best of the twice-born
vanished, all eight of the same form, with shining bodies.

---

"'When the night had passed I woke, Bharata, and thinking of that dream I felt
the greatest joy. Then the battle between him and me began, Bharata, tumultuous
and wonderful, making the hair of all creatures stand on end. The Bhargava
rained a shower of arrows on me, and I held it off with a net of arrows,
Bharata. Then the great ascetic, very angry again with yesterday's anger, hurled
a spear at me, striking like Indra's thunderbolt, bright as Yama's rod, blazing
like fire in battle and licking on every side. Like a fiery body in the sky,
tiger of the Bharatas, it struck me quickly on the shoulder, Bharata. Then
terrible blood flowed from me, wounded by Rama, strong-armed one, red-eyed one,
like red ochre from a mountain.

"'Then, filled with anger, I sent an arrow like death, like a snake's poison, at
Jamadagni's son. Struck on the forehead by it, the hero, the best of the
twice-born, shone, great king, like a mountain with a peak. In his fury he drew
powerfully and set to his bow a terrible arrow, like Time the Ender, a destroyer
of enemies. That fierce arrow fell on my chest, hissing like a snake, and I went
to the ground, king, smeared with blood. When I came to myself again, I hurled
at wise Jamadagni's son a pure spear, blazing like the thunderbolt. It fell on
the chest of the chief of the twice-born, and he was shaken, king, and trembling
came over him. Then his friend, the brahmin Akritavrana of great austerity,
embraced him and comforted him in many ways with kind words.

"'Comforted, Rama, filled with anger and fury, the man of great vows, brought
forth the supreme Brahma weapon. To strike it back I used the same supreme
Brahma weapon, and it blazed as if showing the end of the age. The two Brahma
weapons met between us, without reaching Rama or me, best of the Bharatas. Then
pure splendour appeared in the sky, and all creatures were in distress, lord of
the people. The seers with the gandharvas and the gods, Bharata, were in the
greatest torment, oppressed by the splendour of the weapons. Then the earth
shook, with its mountains, forests and trees, and all creatures, burning, fell
into the deepest dismay. The sky blazed, king, the ten directions smoked, and
those who move in the air could not stay in the sky. Then, as the world cried
out, with gods, asuras and rakshasas, I thought, "This is the moment," and I
wanted to use the dear weapon Prasvapa, as the speakers of Brahman had told me.
And the weapon I thought of appeared in my mind.

---

"'Then a great clamour rose in the sky, king: "Bhishma, do not loose Prasvapa,
joy of the Kauravas!" Still I was aiming the weapon at the son of Bhrigu. As I
was using Prasvapa, Narada spoke to me. "These hosts of gods, Kauravya, standing
in the sky, forbid you today. Do not use Prasvapa. Rama is an ascetic, devoted
to brahmins, a brahmin and your teacher. Never insult him, Kauravya, in any
way." Then I saw those eight speakers of Brahman standing in the sky. Smiling,
king of kings, they said this to me softly. "Do as Narada says, best of the
Bharatas. That is best for the worlds, bull of the Bharatas."

"'So I withdrew that sleeping weapon in the battle and kindled the Brahma weapon
in that fight as the rule is. Then Rama, furious, prince, seeing that weapon
turned back, at once let out the words, "I am beaten by Bhishma, slow-witted as
I am." Then Jamadagni's son saw his father, and his father's father, and
another. They stood round him and spoke to him gently then.

"'"Never do such a rash thing again, child, as to go to battle with Bhishma,
least of all with a kshatriya. For this is the dharma of a kshatriya, son of
Bhrigu, to fight. Study and the keeping of vows are the brahmins' highest
wealth. On some occasion before, we urged you to take up the very fierce bearing
of weapons, and you did what was to be done. Enough, child, of this clash with
Bhishma in battle, strong-armed one. Leave the battle. Enough of your
bow-bearing, bless you. Put it aside, unassailable one, and practise austerity,
Bhargava. This Bhishma, Shantanu's son, has been held back by all the gods. They
have urged him again and again, 'Turn back from this battle. Do not fight Rama,
your teacher. Rama is not right for you to beat in battle, upholder of the
Kurus. Honour the brahmin on the battlefield, son of Ganga.' We are your elders,
and so we hold you back. Bhishma is one of the Vasus. By good fortune you are
alive, little son. Ganga's son, Shantanu's son, is the famous Vasu. How could
you beat him in battle, Rama? Turn back. Arjuna, the best of the Pandavas,
mighty son of Puramdara, Nara, the hero, Prajapati, the eternal god of old,
famous in the three worlds as the left-handed archer, mighty, has been ordained
by the Self-born as Bhishma's death in due time."

"'Spoken to like this by his ancestors, Rama said this to them. "I would not
turn back in battle. That is the vow I have taken. I have never turned back
before at the head of a battle. Let the son of the river turn back from the
fight, if you wish, grandfathers. But I will never turn back from this battle."

"'Then, king, the sages led by Richika, together with Narada, came and said this
to me: "Turn back from the battle, my son. Honour the best of the twice-born."
"No," I said to them, looking to the dharma of a kshatriya. "This is my vow in
the world: I will never turn back from battle with my face away, struck by
arrows in my back. Not for greed, nor meanness, nor fear, nor gain would I give
up eternal dharma. That is my firm resolve."

"'Then all those sages led by Narada, king, and my mother the Bhagirathi came
into the middle of the battle. I stood in the battle ready to fight, with arrow
taken up and bow in hand, as firm in my resolve as before. Then they went
together again to the son of Bhrigu in the battle and said to him, "The heart of
a brahmin is soft as butter, Bhargava. Make peace. Rama, Rama, turn back from
this battle, best of the twice-born. Bhishma cannot be killed by you, Bhargava,
nor you by Bhishma." Saying this, they all blocked the battlefield, and the
ancestors made the son of Bhrigu lay down his weapons.

"'Then I saw those eight speakers of Brahman again, shining like eight planets
risen in the sky. They spoke to me lovingly as I stood in the battle. "Go to
Rama, strong-armed one, your teacher, and do what is good for the worlds."
Seeing Rama turned back by the words of his friends, and doing good to the
worlds, I too accepted their words. Then I went to Rama, badly wounded as I was,
and greeted him. Rama, the great ascetic, smiling, said to me lovingly, "There
is no kshatriya on earth equal to you in this world. Go, Bhishma. I am greatly
pleased with you in this battle." And in my presence the Bhargava called that
girl and spoke to her in a sad voice, in the middle of those ascetics.'"

**Divergences.**

* **The end of the duel.** Neither wins. Rama's ancestors stop him, and say Arjuna is to be Bhishma's death (5.186.18–20). Ledger L-183.
* Rejected lines (\*582–\*583) add detail. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.183.16 *ārṣṭiṣeṇam*: "as Arshtishena did"; the sense of the comparison is uncertain.
* 5.183.21 *svarbhānuḥ*: Svarbhanu, Rahu, who eclipses the sun.
* 5.184.12 *prasvāpa*: "the sleeper", a weapon that puts the enemy to sleep; its partner is *saṃbodhana*, "waking" (5.184.15).
* 5.186.9: Rama's father Jamadagni, his grandfather Richika and "another".
* 5.186.17: "Bhishma is one of the Vasus", as Book 1 tells.
* 5.186.29 *nāvanītaṃ hi hṛdayaṃ viprāṇām*: "the heart of a brahmin is soft as butter", that is, a brahmin should be gentle.
