# The Five Villages

"I take my leave of you, god among kings," said Sanjaya. "I shall go, Pandava.
Bless you. I hope no word of mine has said anything wrong, out of the strength
of my feelings. I take my leave of Janardana, of Bhimasena and Arjuna, of the
sons of Madri, Satyaki and Chekitana, and go. Peace and happiness to you. Look
on me kindly, kings."

"You have our leave, Sanjaya," said Yudhishthira. "Go in peace. You have never
done anything to displease us. We know you, and they all know you, to be pure of
heart, standing in the middle in the hall. You are a trusted envoy, Sanjaya, and
very dear to us. Your words are kind, your conduct good, and you see far. Your
mind is never confused, Sanjaya, and you do not get angry even when you are told
the truth. You never speak harshly, words that strike at the vitals, nor bitter
praise, nor sour words. I know your speech, suta. It delights in dharma, it has
sense, and it does no harm. You are the envoy dearest to us. Vidura could come
here too, as a second. We used to see you often before. You are a friend to
Dhananjaya, as dear as his own self.

"When you have gone from here, Sanjaya, go quickly to wait on the brahmins who
deserve it, pure in their strength, good in their conduct, born in good families
and full of every dharma. Greet from me the old brahmins who study the Veda, the
mendicants and the ascetics who always live in the forests. Ask the others after
their health.

"Meet King Dhritarashtra's priest and his teachers and priests, my son. Go to
them, suta, and ask after their health as each deserves. Greet Drona, as he
deserves, and may he be pleased with me. He is our teacher, dear, never
straying, obedient to his own rule, who lived the celibate life wanting the
Vedas, and gave the four-footed science of weapons new life. Ask after the
health of Ashvatthama, who has finished his learning and is good in conduct, who
also gave the four-footed science of weapons new life, as bold as a gandharva's
son. Go to the house of Sharadvata's son, the great chariot fighter, the best of
those who know weapons. Speak of me again and again, Sanjaya, and touch Kripa's
feet with your hand. Hold the feet of Bhishma, best of the Kurus, in whom
courage and kindness are joined with austerity, wisdom, good conduct, learning,
strength and steadiness, and tell him of me.

"Greet the old king, the leader of the Kurus, whose eyes are his wisdom, so
learned, who serves the old, so wise. Tell him, Sanjaya, that I am well. Ask
after the health of Suyodhana, my son, the eldest son of Dhritarashtra, that
slow-witted, foolish, crooked man of evil character, who rules the whole earth.
Ask after the health of Duhshasana too, my son, his younger brother, just as
slow-witted, always of the same character, a great archer, the bravest of the
Kurus. Ask after the health of the son of the vaishya woman, my son, eminent, a
poet, never confused about what matters, very wise, full of every dharma, who
never likes war.

"Ask after the health of Chitrasena, my son, who has no equal at cheating and at
dice, who hides his tricks, who plays well, a master of the dice, hard to beat
with the throw. May the bull of the Bahlikas, so proud, who always wishes for
nothing but peace for the Bharatas, greet me kindly as he used to. Somadatta, I
think, deserves honour. He has many fine virtues, he is learned and never harsh,
and out of affection he always bears with anger. Somadatta's son is the most
worthy of the Kurus, Sanjaya. He is our brother and my friend, a great archer,
the best of chariot fighters. Ask after his health and his counsellors'. And the
other young leaders of the Kurus, sons, grandsons and brothers of ours,
whichever of them you meet, in whatever way, tell them I ask after their health.

"Ask after the health of all the kings Dhritarashtra's son has brought to fight
the Pandavas, the Vasatis, the Shalvakas and the Kekayas, the Ambashthas and the
chief Trigartas, the heroes of the east, the north, the south and the west, and
all the men of the mountains, those who are kind and good in character and
conduct. Ask after the health of all the elephant riders, chariot fighters,
horsemen and foot soldiers, those great hosts of noble men. Tell them I am well,
and ask after the health of them all. Ask after the king's ministers who look
after his affairs, the doorkeepers and those who lead his army, those who count
his income and spending, and those who think about his great concerns.

"Ask after the health of Shakuni, king of Gandhara, the mountaineer, who has no
equal at cheating, the player of dice, my son, who adds to the pride of
Dhritarashtra's son, that man of false mind. Ask after the health of Vaikartana,
my son, the hero who means to beat the Pandavas, whom no one can overcome, with
a single chariot, and who has no equal at leading the confused astray. Ask after
the health of Vidura, my son. He is our devoted one, our teacher, our servant,
our father and mother and friend, of fathomless wisdom and long sight, our
counsellor.

"Meet all the old women who are full of virtue, Sanjaya, who are known as our
mothers, and greet them all together. Say, 'I hope your sons are living, with
living sons of their own, and treat you kindly and well.' Then tell them,
Sanjaya, that Ajatashatru is well, with his sons.

"Ask after the health of all our wives you know of there, my son. 'I hope you
are well guarded, sweet-smelling and faultless, and live in your homes with
care. I hope you behave kindly and well to your fathers-in-law, good ladies.
Keep to conduct that will make your husbands favour you.' Meet our
daughters-in-law you know of there, Sanjaya, who came from good families, who
are full of virtue and have children, and tell them that Yudhishthira greets
them kindly. Embrace the girls in the houses, Sanjaya, and ask after their
health from me. 'May you have good husbands who favour you, and may you favour
your husbands.' Ask after the health of the women of the town, my son, adorned,
well dressed and sweet-smelling, pleasant, happy and well provided for, light to
look at and light of speech.

"Tell the sons of slave women and the slaves of the Kurus, and the many
hunchbacks and lame people who depend on them, that I am well, and ask after
their health last of all. 'I hope the old livelihood still goes on. I hope
Dhritarashtra's son gives you what you need.' Does Dhritarashtra, out of
kindness, support the maimed, the poor and the dwarfs? Tell all the blind and
the old, and the many who live by the work of their hands, that I am well, and
ask after their health last of all. 'Do not be afraid of sorrow, or of a hard
life. No doubt you did some wrong in other worlds. When I have held down my
enemies and helped my friends, I will give you clothes and food.'

"There are things I have done for brahmins, and things to come, and they no
longer carry them on. I see them still as they should be. Tell the king that
very result. And ask, from us, after the health of those who are always without
protectors and weak, who struggle on their own, the fools, those poor people, in
every way, my son. And when you see all the others who have taken refuge with
Dhritarashtra's sons, who have come from every direction, suta's son, and all
who deserve it, ask after their health and their lasting welfare. In this way,
suta, ask after the health of all who have come and come again, the king's
envoys who have come from every quarter, and afterwards tell them I am well.

"There are no fighters on earth like those Dhritarashtra's son has won. But
dharma is eternal. My own dharma is my great strength for destroying my enemies.

"And repeat these words to Suyodhana, son of Dhritarashtra, Sanjaya: 'The desire
that torments the heart in your body, to rule the Kurus without a rival, has no
reason in it. We are not such men that we would do what pleases you. Give me
Shakra's city, or fight, hero, chief of the Bharatas.'"

---

"The Ordainer, Sanjaya," said Yudhishthira, "brings into his power the good and
the bad, the young and the old, the weak and the strong. The Lord gives learning
to the fool and folly to the learned. He gives everything, sending out the seed
before. That is enough to make it known. Tell it as it is, after you have taken
counsel with one another, as if in great delight.

"When you have gone to the Kurus, son of Gavalgana, greet mighty Dhritarashtra,
clasp his feet, and then ask after his health. And when he is sitting surrounded
by the Kurus, tell him: 'It is by your courage alone, king, that the Pandavas
live in happiness. By your grace they won a kingdom when they were children,
tamer of enemies. You set them in the kingdom first. Do not look on while they
perish. All of this is not enough for any one man, Sanjaya. Let us live
together, my son. Do not fall into the power of enemies.'

"Bow your head to Bhishma, Shantanu's son, grandfather of the Bharatas, and
speak my name. When you have greeted him, tell our grandfather: 'You raised up
Shantanu's line once again when it was sinking. So now, grandfather, act by your
own judgment, so that your grandsons may live loving one another.'

"Tell Vidura too, who keeps the counsel of the Kurus: 'Speak against war, gentle
one. Yudhishthira wants what is good.'

"Then speak to Suyodhana, the impatient prince, as he sits among the Kurus, and
plead with him again and again. 'You saw Krishna, alone, brought into the hall,
while I stood by. We bore that sorrow, so as not to kill the Kurus. In this way
the Pandavas bore troubles before and after, though they are the stronger. All
the Kurus know it. You sent us into exile, gentle one, dressed in deerskins. We
bore that sorrow, so as not to kill the Kurus. Duhshasana, with your consent,
broke into the hall and violated Krishna by her hair. We let that pass too.

"'Let us have our own fitting share, scorcher of enemies. Turn your greedy mind
away from another's wealth, bull among men. That way there will be peace, king,
and goodwill between us. We want peace. Give us even one part of the kingdom.
Kushasthala, Vrikasthala, Asandi and Varanavata, and let the fifth be any one
you like. Give five villages to the five brothers, Suyodhana. Let there be peace
for us with our kin, wise one.'

"Sanjaya, let brother follow brother, let father be joined with son. Let the
Panchalas meet the Kurus with smiles. I want to see the Kurus and the Panchalas
unharmed. Let us all be at peace, my son, with glad hearts, bull of the
Bharatas. I am equal to peace, Sanjaya, and equal to war. I am equal to dharma
and to gain, to gentleness and to harshness."

---

With the Pandava's leave, Sanjaya set out, having done all that great
Dhritarashtra had ordered. He reached Hastinapura and went in quickly. He came
to the women's quarters and spoke to the doorkeeper.

"Doorkeeper, tell Dhritarashtra I have come back from the Pandavas. If he is
awake, steward, greet him for me. When the king knows of it, I may go in."

"Lord of the earth, I bow to you," said the doorkeeper. "Sanjaya is here at your
door, wanting to see you. He has come as an envoy from the Pandavas. Command me,
king. What should he do?"

"Tell him I am well and at leisure for him," said Dhritarashtra. "Let him come
in. Welcome to Sanjaya. I am never too busy for him. Why should he stand at the
door, steward?"

Then, with the king's leave, the suta's son went into the great house, guarded
by wise, brave and noble men. With joined hands he came to the king, the son of
Vichitravirya, seated on his throne.

"I am Sanjaya, lord of the earth," said Sanjaya. "I bow to you. I went to the
Pandavas, god among men, and I have come back. Proud Yudhishthira, son of Pandu,
greeted you and asked after your health. He asks gladly after your sons. 'Do
your sons give you joy, and your grandsons, and your friends and counsellors,
king, and those who live on you?'"

"I greet you, my son Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "and I wish Ajatashatru, the
Partha, well. Is that king of the Kauravas well, with his sons, his counsellors
and his younger brothers?"

"The son of Pandu is well, with his counsellors," said Sanjaya, "and wants more
than that, what was his before. He does what is clear in dharma and in purpose.
He is proud, learned, far-seeing and of good character. For the Pandava,
kindness is higher than dharma, and dharma higher than a heap of wealth. The
Partha does not go after pleasures and comforts that lack dharma, Bharata. Know
that of him.

"A man moves as another drives him, like a wooden doll on strings. Seeing the
Pandava held in check like this, I think fate is stronger than human effort. And
seeing the fault in your deeds, terrible and shameful, which will end in evil, a
man is praised only so long as his enemy chooses to wait. Ajatashatru has left
the evil behind, like a snake leaving a worn-out skin it can no longer use.
Steady Yudhishthira shines with conduct no one can take from him, and leaves the
evil with you.

"Look at your own deed, king, which has left the noble way, joined with dharma
and purpose. You have earned blame here, king, and evil may cling to you
hereafter. Following your son's will, you hope to keep this doubtful wealth
without them. The cry of adharma is loud on the earth. This deed is not worthy
of you, first of the Bharatas.

"A man without wisdom, of bad family, cruel, who nurses his hatred long,
unsteady in the skills of a kshatriya, without courage and without manners,
cannot cross over his troubles. A man born in a good family, keeping dharma,
famous, learned, living in comfort, self-controlled, holds on to dharma and
purpose, which are woven together. He does not go astray, unless fate wills it.
How could a wise man, holding the best counsel, who guides dharma and purpose in
times of trouble, a man so endowed and lacking none of the counsel, not a fool,
do such a cruel deed? Your counsellors too sit together, who know counsel and
are always busy in your affairs. This is their firm decision: hell has been
chosen for the destruction of the Kurus.

"The Kurus would have been destroyed before their time if Ajatashatru had ever
wished to return evil for evil, and to let it loose on you. This blame would
have been yours in the world. Where else, but in the domain of the lords, did
the Partha see the other world? He went beyond it, and so he is honoured, no
doubt of it. It is not the doing of men. Seeing these qualities that come from
deeds, being and not being, which never last, King Bali found no end to it, and
thought there was no cause but time.

"The eye, the ears, the nose, the skin and the tongue are the seats of a
creature's knowledge. When thirst is ended, they are content. A man should put
them away, untroubled and free of pain. But I do not think a man's deed works
out exactly as it is well planned. Born by the deed of his mother and father, a
man grows by food as is ordained. The pleasant and the unpleasant, happiness and
sorrow, blame and praise, come to him. Others blame him for an offence, and the
same people praise him for good conduct.

"I blame you for the quarrel among the Bharatas. This will surely be the end of
the people. If not, then because of your offence, this deed will burn the Kurus
as fire burns dry brush. You alone in the whole world, king of men, among those
whose sons are born, have fallen into their power. At the time of the dicing you
praised those ruled by desire. See where that leads, to nothing but peace. By
taking up the untrustworthy, king, and holding down the trustworthy, you are too
weak to guard the wide, rich, endless earth, Kaurava.

"Now, with your leave, I am tired out, shaken by the speed of the chariot. I
shall go to my bed, lion among men. In the morning the Kurus will meet in the
hall and hear the words of Ajatashatru."

**Divergences.**

* **The five villages.** The request is CE: *kuśasthalaṃ vṛkasthalam āsandī vāraṇāvatam / avasānaṃ bhaved atra kiṃ cid eva tu pañcamam* (5.31.19–20). Ledger L-174.
* Rejected lines (\*150–\*165) add detail and epithets. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.30.10–11 *astraṃ catuṣpāt*: "the four-footed science of weapons". Drona and Ashvatthama are said to have "given it new life".
* 5.30.15: Yudhishthira's description of Duryodhana is harsh in the text, and is kept.
* 5.30.18: "Chitrasena" is a Kaurava gambler here, described as an expert at the dice.
* 5.30.19 *bāhlikānām ṛṣabhaḥ*: Bahlika, the old Kuru king.
* 5.30.21 *saumadattiḥ*: Somadatta's son, Bhurishravas.
* 5.30.23: the peoples are kept as the text gives them.
* 5.30.28 *vaikartanam*: Karna.
* 5.30.36 *veśastriyaḥ*: "the women of the town", courtesans.
* 5.30.47 *śakrapuram*: "Shakra's city", Indraprastha.
* 5.31.1–3: the opening verses on the Ordainer are obscure; the plain sense is kept.
* 5.31.19: the four named villages are kept; the fifth is left to Duryodhana.
* 5.32.13, 22–25: these verses of Sanjaya's are hard and their sense is uncertain. The prose keeps close to the words.
* 5.32.23: "King Bali found no end to it" refers to the asura king's teaching that time is the cause of all.
