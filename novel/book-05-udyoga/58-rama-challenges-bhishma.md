# Rama Challenges Bhishma

"'Then on the third day,'" said Bhishma, "'the man of great vows, camped on
level ground, sent word to me, king: "I have come." I heard that the mighty one
had come to the border of the land. I went quickly and gladly to the lord, that
treasure-house of splendour, putting a cow before me, king of kings, surrounded
by brahmins, by priests like gods and by chaplains. When mighty Jamadagni's son
saw I had come, he accepted my honours and said this.

"'"Bhishma, with what thought did you bring this daughter of the king of Kashi
here against her will, and then let her go again? For you have made her fall
from gaining dharma, high or low. Who would come to her, when you have touched
her? Shalva rejected her because you took her, Bharata. So take her back at my
command, Bharata. Let this princess have her own dharma, tiger among men. It is
not right for a king like you to insult her like this, sinless one."

"'Seeing he was not in a good mood, I said to him, "I would never give her to my
brother again, brahmin. She told me before, here, 'I am Shalva's,' Bhargava, and
with my leave she went to the city of Saubha. Not for fear, nor pity, nor greed,
nor desire for gain would I give up the dharma of a kshatriya. That is the vow I
have taken."

"'Then Rama spoke to me, his eyes clouded with anger. "If you do not do as I
say, bull of the Kurus, I will kill you today with your counsellors." So Rama
said again and again in his fury, his eyes clouded with anger. Again and again I
begged that tiger of the Bhrigus, tamer of enemies, with kind words, but he
would not be calmed. Then I bowed my head again to the best of brahmins and
said, "What is the reason you want to fight me? You yourself taught me the four
kinds of archery when I was a boy, strong-armed one. I am your pupil, Bhargava."

"'Then Rama said to me, his eyes red with anger, "You know I am your teacher,
Bhishma, and yet you will not take this daughter of the king of Kashi to please
me, Kaurava, lord of the earth. Otherwise you will have no peace, joy of the
Kurus. Take her, strong-armed one, and protect your own family. For she has been
made to fall by you, and she cannot find a husband."

"'As Rama, the conqueror of enemy cities, spoke like this, I said, "This will
never happen, brahmin seer. Why trouble yourself? Looking to your old place as
my teacher, Jamadagni's son, I ask your grace, lord. I gave her up long ago. Who
that knew the great faults of women would keep in his house a woman whose heart
is another's, like a snake? Not even for fear of Vasava would I give up dharma,
splendid one. Be gracious, or not, or do what you must. Do it quickly. And this
verse, pure-souled lord, is heard in the ancient lore. The great Marutta sang
it, wise one: 'Even a teacher, if he is arrogant, does not know what should and
should not be done, and has taken a wrong road, should be punished.' Out of love
I have honoured you greatly as my teacher. You do not know a teacher's conduct,
so I will fight you. I would not kill a teacher in battle, least of all a
brahmin, least of all one grown old in austerity. So I have borne with you. But
it is settled in the dharmas. If a man in anger kills a brahmin in battle who
has his arrow raised like a kshatriya, fighting and not fleeing, he does not
incur the sin of killing a brahmin. I am a kshatriya, standing in the dharma of
kshatriyas, rich in austerity. A man who treats another as that other treats him
incurs no wrong and finds good. One who is able in purpose or in dharma, who
knows place and time, is better when caught in doubt of harm, than one without
doubt. Since you act as you think right in this doubtful matter, I will fight
you, Rama, in a great battle. See the strength of my arms and my more than human
courage.

"'"Even so, son of Bhrigu, I will do what I can. I will fight you at
Kurukshetra, brahmin. Be ready, Rama, for single combat as you wish, great sage.
There, Rama, killed by me and covered with hundreds of arrows, you will win the
worlds you have conquered, purified by weapons in the great battle. So go and
turn back to Kurukshetra, lover of war. I will come there to fight you,
strong-armed one, rich in austerity. There, where you once made the purifying
offering for your father, Rama, I too will kill you and make the purifying
offering, Bhargava. Go there quickly, Rama, fierce in war. I will take away your
old pride, you who call yourself a brahmin. As for what you boast so often in
the assemblies, Rama, 'I alone conquered the kshatriyas of the world,' hear
this. Bhishma had not been born then, nor any kshatriya like me, to destroy your
pride in war and your desire. I am born now, strong-armed one, Bhishma,
conqueror of enemy cities. I will take away your pride in battle, Rama, no doubt
of it."

---

"'Then Rama said to me, as if laughing, Bharata, "Good fortune, Bhishma, that
you want to fight me in battle. I am going to Kurukshetra with you, Kaurava. I
will do as I said. Come there, scorcher of enemies. There let your mother
Jahnavi see you killed by me, Bhishma, covered with hundreds of arrows, food for
vultures, herons and badas. Let the goddess served by the perfected ones and the
charanas weep today when she sees you, wretched, killed by me, lord of the
earth. She does not deserve it, the fortunate river, daughter of Bhagiratha, who
bore you, a fool, sick and eager for war. Come, go with me, Bhishma. Let the
battle be today. Take everything, Kaurava, chariot and all, bull of the
Bharatas."

"'As Rama, conqueror of enemy cities, spoke like this, I bowed my head, king,
and said, "So be it." When he had said this, Rama went to Kurukshetra, wanting
to fight. I went into the city and told Satyavati. Then, when the rites for my
welfare had been done and my mother had blessed me, I had the twice-born
pronounce the auspicious day and their blessings, splendid one. I mounted a fine
silver chariot with white horses, well fitted and well built, covered with tiger
skins, furnished with great weapons and every implement. It was yoked by a
well-trained charioteer of good family, a hero who knew the lore of horses,
king, and had often seen battle. I wore bright white armour. I took a white bow,
best of the Bharatas, and set out with a white umbrella held over my head,
fanned with white yak-tails, lord of men, in white clothes and a white turban,
with all my ornaments white. Praised with blessings for victory, I went out of
the City of the Elephant to Kurukshetra, the field of battle, bull of the
Bharatas. Urged on by the charioteer, those horses, swift as thought or wind,
carried me fast to the great battle, king.

"'When I reached Kurukshetra, king, mighty Rama and I set to fight each other at
once. I stood in sight of Rama, the great ascetic, took up my fine conch and
blew it loudly. Then brahmins, ascetics and forest dwellers there, king, and the
gods with the hosts of seers, watched that heavenly battle. Heavenly garlands
appeared again and again, and heavenly music, and masses of clouds. Then all the
ascetics who followed the Bhargava stood round the battlefield as onlookers.

"'Then my mother, the goddess who wants the good of all beings, said to me in
her own form, king, "What is this you mean to do? I will go to Jamadagni's son,
best of the Kurus, and beg him again and again, 'Do not fight Bhishma, your
pupil.' Do not be so stubborn, my son, lord of the earth, about fighting the
brahmin Jamadagni's son in battle." So she rebuked me. "Do you not know Rama, my
son, the destroyer of the kshatriyas, equal to Hara in courage, that you want to
fight him?"

"'Then I bowed to the goddess, joined my hands and told her everything as it had
happened at the bridegroom choice, best of the Bharatas. I told her how I had
tried to win Rama's grace before, king of kings, and the old desire of the
daughter of the king of Kashi. Then my mother, the great river goddess, went to
Rama and asked the seer, the Bhargava, to forgive me for my sake. "Do not fight
Bhishma, your pupil," she said. And he said to her as she begged him, "Turn
Bhishma back. He does not do what I want." So she came to me.'"

"Then Ganga, out of love for her son, came back to Bhishma," said Sanjaya, "but
he did not do as she said, his eyes clouded with anger. Then the best of the
Bhrigus appeared, the great ascetic whose soul was dharma, and the best of the
twice-born challenged him to battle again."

**Divergences.**

* **Rama's name.** The text calls him Rama, son of Jamadagni, the Bhargava. Ledger L-62.
* **Bhishma and Rama.** The duel is told only by Bhishma, here in Book 5 (5.178–186). Ledger L-183.
* Rejected lines (\*577–\*580) add detail. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.178.6 *dharmāvāpteḥ parāvarāt*: Amba has fallen "from gaining dharma, high or low", that is, from any proper marriage.
* 5.178.15: Rama taught Bhishma "the four kinds of archery". The text says so here.
* 5.178.21 *vyālīm iva*: a woman whose heart is another's, "like a snake". These are Bhishma's words.
* 5.178.24: the verse of Marutta on punishing an arrogant teacher.
* 5.178.29 is a hard verse; the rendering keeps its words.
* 5.178.34: Rama's "purifying offering for his father", the pools of kshatriya blood at Kurukshetra.
* 5.179.30–31: the CE gives these two verses to Sanjaya.
