summary: Yudhishthira asks Sanjaya what word of his sounds like war; peace is better, but desire is never satisfied, and Dhritarashtra, who has ignored Vidura for his son's sake and listens to Duhshasana, Shakuni and Karna, wants the whole earth; still, out of respect for Sanjaya, he will forgive, if Indraprastha is given back to him; Sanjaya answers that life is short and it would be better to live by begging among the Vrishnis than to win a kingdom by war, that he should have fought when his army was his, and that there is no happiness in killing Bhishma, Drona and the rest; Yudhishthira replies that dharma and its opposite can wear each other's forms, especially in distress, that he wants nothing gained against dharma, and that Krishna, who wishes both sides well, should say whether he would be blameless in giving up or in fighting.
---

# Yudhishthira's Answer

"What word of mine have you heard, Sanjaya," said Yudhishthira, "that sounds
eager for war, that you are so afraid of war? Not fighting is better than
fighting, my friend. Who, if he could have the one, would ever fight, suta? If a
man could have every wish of his heart without doing anything, Sanjaya, he would
not act at all. I know that. Least of all would he go to war, when there is
always something easier than war.

"How could a man who understands ever go to war? Who but one cursed by fate
would choose war? The Parthas want happiness, and so they act. But they act in a
way that does not fall short of dharma, and that is good for the world.

"A man who hopes for happiness as the fruit of action does work that is truly
painful, work whose means are hard. He wants happiness and wants to be rid of
pain, and follows wherever the pleasure of his senses leads. Longing for
pleasure torments his own body. Driven by it, he brings pain on himself. As a
fire, when it is fed and blazes, grows stronger still, so desire is never
satisfied by getting what it wants, any more than a kindled fire is satisfied
with butter.

"Look at this great heap of pleasures that King Dhritarashtra has, and we with
him. The unfortunate do not win wars. The unfortunate do not hear the sound of
song. The unfortunate do not wear garlands and scents, or rub on fragrant
ointments. The unfortunate do not wear fine cloaks. How then could he drive us
out from the Kurus? Here, in his own body, desire torments his heart, and it
ought to be shaken off. The king is in a crooked place himself and wants others
to be straight. That is not good. As he sees his own conduct, so he meets with
others. He is like a man who sets a fire in the hot season, in a deep and
tangled thicket, and then, when the wind has made it grow, grieves and wants to
escape it when the cold season ends.

"Why does King Dhritarashtra, who has all this power, lament now, Sanjaya? He
took the side of his son, who is foolish and deluded, who loves crooked ways and
takes no counsel. Suyodhana ignored the words of Vidura, the most reliable of
all, as if Vidura were not his friend. King Dhritarashtra, wanting to please his
son, knowingly enters into what is against dharma. For love of his son, suta,
King Dhritarashtra did not think of Vidura, the wisest of the Kurus, who wants
their good, who is learned and eloquent and of good character. For the sake of
pleasing his son he saw and still gave up dharma and pleasure. That son is proud
and self-seeking and jealous, violent, and careless of profit and dharma. His
tongue is foul and his temper rules him. He is a man of desire, an evil-hearted
flatterer, unteachable, hopeless, long in his anger, a traitor to his friends,
Sanjaya, and his mind is wicked.

"Even then, Sanjaya, while I was playing at dice, I thought: if this goes on,
ruin will come to the Kurus. Vidura spoke wise words, and got no praise from
Dhritarashtra. As long as they followed the Steward's judgement, trouble never
came to the Kurus. As long as they followed his wisdom, their kingdom grew.

"Now hear from me who the counsellors of the greedy son of Dhritarashtra are,
suta: Duhshasana, Shakuni, and the suta's son. See his folly, son of Gavalgana.
I look, and I cannot see how there can be well-being for the Kurus and the
Srinjayas. Dhritarashtra has taken the kingship from others, and far-seeing
Vidura has been sent away. Dhritarashtra and his sons hope for a great kingdom
on earth without a rival. With him peace cannot be had at all. He thinks what he
has in his hands is already his.

"Karna thinks the Arjuna who takes up his weapon in battle can be withstood.
There were great battles before. How was it that Karna was no island of refuge
for them then? Karna knows, and Suyodhana knows, Drona knows and the grandfather
knows, and the other Kurus who are there know, that there is no archer to match
Arjuna. All the Kurus know it, and the other kings who have gathered there know
it: how Duryodhana did his wrong while Arjuna, the tamer of enemies, was not
there. So the son of Dhritarashtra thinks he can take what belongs to the
Pandavas. And yet he went to that battle and saw what the wearer of the diadem
did there, with nothing but his bow.

"The sons of Dhritarashtra are still alive only because they have not heard the
twang of the Gandiva drawn in battle. Suyodhana thinks his purpose is achieved
because he has not met the rush of angry Bhimasena. Not even Indra, my friend,
would dare to take our sovereignty while Bhimasena lives, and Dhananjaya, and
Nakula, suta, and my brave Sahadeva. If the old king and his son took this to
heart, suta, then the sons of Dhritarashtra would not perish in battle, burned
by the Pandavas' anger, Sanjaya.

"You know the hardships we have suffered, Sanjaya. Out of respect for you I
would forgive them. You know how things stood between us and the Kauravas
before, and how we behaved to the son of Dhritarashtra then. Let it be the same
again now, and I will make peace, as you say. Let Indraprastha be my kingdom.
Let Suyodhana, first of the Bharatas, give it back."

---

"Pandava," said Sanjaya, "your conduct is always in dharma. The world has heard
it, and it can be seen, Partha. Life is short, and it flows away in great
streams. Look at that, Pandava, and do not destroy yourself.

"If the Kurus will not give you your share without war, Ajatashatru, I think it
better to live by begging in the kingdom of the Andhakas and Vrishnis than to
win a kingdom by war. The life of a man is short, it runs away, it is always
sorrowful and unsteady. And war does not suit your age. So do not spread evil,
Pandava.

"Desires cling to a man, king, and they are the root of what stands in the way
of dharma. The steady man kills them first, and wins blameless praise in the
world. The thirst for wealth binds a man in this world, Partha. When a man
pursues it, his dharma suffers. The man who chooses dharma is wise. The man
greedy for pleasure loses out by chasing wealth. When a man makes dharma the
first of all his deeds, my son, he shines like the sun in its great heat. When a
man gives up dharma, even if he wins this whole earth, his mind is evil and he
comes to grief.

"You have studied the Veda. You have lived as a celibate student. You have
sacrificed, and given to brahmins. Thinking of the highest place, you have also
given yourself to comforts for many years. A man who serves pleasure and comfort
beyond measure, and does no work at the practice of yoga, lies in pain when his
wealth runs out and his comforts go, driven by the force of his desire. In the
same way a man who clings to the pursuit of wealth, who gives up dharma and does
what is against it, the fool who has no faith in the next world, suffers after
death, when he has left his body.

"In the next world deeds are not lost, whether good or evil. The good and evil
go before the doer, and the doer follows after. Your deeds are famous, like food
rightly won and purified by faith, rich in fragrance and taste, given to
brahmins at the rites with the finest fees. Here, in this field, work is done,
Partha. After death there is no work to be done. You have done the work for the
next world, great merit, praised by the good. There a man leaves behind death,
old age and fear. There is no hunger or thirst there, and nothing unpleasant to
the mind. There is nothing to be done there, except to please the senses. Such
is the fruit of deeds, king. With a measured love in your heart, Pandava, do not
throw away both worlds for good by deeds born of anger or of joy.

"You have come to the end of the deeds that win praise, truth, self-control,
honesty and kindness. You may offer the horse sacrifice and the Rajasuya. But do
not come again to the end that is an evil deed. If you Parthas are going to do
this evil deed after so long, in this way, then you lived in the forest for
years, Pandavas, a hard life, for dharma's sake, in vain.

"Before you went into exile, you could have used the army that was then your own
to command. These Panchalas were always your counsellors, and Janardana, and
heroic Yuyudhana, and Virata, king of the Matsyas, with his golden chariot and
his sons who strike hard. And all the kings you had conquered before would have
come to you. With great allies, burning bright and strong, with Vasudeva and
Arjuna before you, you could have killed the best of your enemies in the midst
of the arena, and broken the pride of Dhritarashtra's son. Why did you let your
enemy's strength grow? Why did you let your own allies wither? Why did you live
in the forests for years, Pandava, and want to fight now, when the time is past?

"An unwise man who fights, Pandava, or a man who does not know dharma, strays
from the path of prosperity. And a wise man who knows dharma, if he fights in
passion, also strays from prosperity. Your mind is not set on what is against
dharma, Partha. You have never done an evil deed in passion. So what is the
reason, what is the cause, that you want to do this deed against wisdom?

"Anger is a bitter headache that no sickness causes. It robs a man of his fame,
and bears the fruit of evil. The good drink it down, and the bad do not. Drink
down your anger, great king, and be calm. Who would want what binds him to evil?
Patience is better for you than pleasures, where Bhishma, Shantanu's son, would
be killed, and Drona with his son, Kripa, Shalya, Somadatta's son, Vikarna,
Vivimshati, Karna and Duryodhana. When you have killed them, what happiness
would you find? Tell me that, Partha. Even if you win this earth to the edge of
the ocean, you will not escape old age and death, what is dear and what is not,
pleasure and pain. Knowing this, do not make war. If you want to do such a deed
because your ministers desire it, then give them what is yours and go away. Do
not stray today from the road that leads to the gods."

---

"Without a doubt, Sanjaya," said Yudhishthira, "what you say is true: dharma is
the best of deeds. But you should find out first, Sanjaya, and then blame me,
whether what I do is dharma or not.

"There are times when what is not dharma wears the look of dharma, and dharma
looks wholly like its opposite. And there are times when dharma wears its own
form. The learned see which is which with their understanding. In times of
distress, dharma and its opposite, which are bound to a man's way of living,
exchange their marks like this. In ordinary times, a man's measure is the first
mark, the duty of his birth. Know, Sanjaya, that times of distress have their
own dharma.

"When a man's natural way of living is lost, and he is destitute, he should seek
whatever means will let him do his work. But a man who is not in distress and
acts as if he were, and a man in distress who acts as if he were not, are both
to be blamed, Sanjaya. The Creator laid down atonements for brahmins who do not
want to perish. So, Sanjaya, one should blame those who are not in distress and
yet do what is not their work, and those who in distress will not act. The way
of the good is always laid down for thoughtful men who seek to cut through to
the truth. But for those who are not brahmins, and not learned, it is right to
think every means allowed.

"That was the purpose of our fathers, and of the grandfathers before them, and
of those before them still, and of those who sought wisdom and did their work. I
think there is no other way at all. Whatever wealth there is on this earth,
whatever belongs to the thirty gods beyond, the world of Prajapati, heaven, the
world of Brahma: I would not want it, Sanjaya, if it came against dharma.

"Krishna is the lord of dharma. He is skilled and wise in policy. He has served
the brahmins, and he is thoughtful, and he guides many mighty kings of the
kshatriyas and the Bhojas. If I would be blameless in giving this up, or if in
fighting I would give up my own dharma, let the famous Keshava say it. Vasudeva
wants the good of both sides.

"The Shaineyas, the Chaitrakas and the Andhakas, the Varshneyas and the Bhojas,
the Kaukuras and the Srinjayas follow Vasudeva's counsel, crush their enemies
and gladden their friends. The Vrishnis and Andhakas, with Ugrasena and the
others, all led by Krishna, are like Indra. The Yadavas are proud, true in their
courage, mighty and prosperous. Babhru of Kashi has won the highest fortune,
having Krishna for his brother and his master. Vasudeva rains his wishes down on
him as the cloud rains on the people when the hot season ends. That is what
Keshava is, my friend. We know him to be one who understands what deeds must be
done. Krishna is dear to us, and he is the best of the good. I will not go
against Keshava's word."

**Divergences.**

* **Rejected lines** in Yudhishthira's first speech (\*134–\*137) add that no one would want a war that destroys his family, and that Dhritarashtra is deluded by being parted from the Pandavas. Not narrated.
* **Indraprastha.** In the CE Yudhishthira's first offer to Sanjaya is to have Indraprastha back (5.26.28). The five villages come in his parting message (5.31.19–20).

**Choices.**

* 5.26.4 *karmodayaṃ sukham āśaṃsamānaḥ*: Yudhishthira's point that action done for pleasure brings pain is kept in his order.
* 5.26.6–7: the lines on "the unfortunate" say that Dhritarashtra, rich in every pleasure, is not unfortunate. How then could he, needing nothing, drive the Pandavas out? The novel keeps the question.
* 5.26.9: the fire set in the hot season is the text's image for Dhritarashtra's position.
* 5.26.13–14: the list of Duryodhana's faults is kept in full.
* 5.26.15 *tadaiva me saṃjaya dīvyato 'bhūn*: "Even then, while I was playing at dice, I thought". Following Ganguli's reading.
* 5.26.20 *dvīpa*: "island", a refuge.
* 5.26.23 *kirīṭinā tālamātrāyudhena*: literally "the wearer of the diadem with a weapon a *tāla* long". Ganguli has "nothing but a bow four cubits long". The novel says "with nothing but his bow" and refers the line, as Ganguli does, to the battle for Virata's cattle.
* 5.27.2: *bhaikṣacaryām andhakavṛṣṇirājye*, "to live by begging in the kingdom of the Andhakas and Vrishnis", is Sanjaya's own advice.
* 5.27.7 *ātmā datto varṣapūgaṃ sukhebhyaḥ*: read with Ganguli as "you have also given yourself to comforts for many years". It could also be read as the opposite, that he has kept himself from comforts. The next verse, a warning against pleasure, supports Ganguli's reading.
* 5.27.11 *anvāhāryeṣu*: "at the rites", the monthly offerings to the Ancestors.
* 5.27.15 *aśvamedho rājasūyas tatheṣṭaḥ*: "You may offer the horse sacrifice and the Rajasuya", as Ganguli reads it. Yudhishthira has performed the Rajasuya (Book 2) but not yet a horse sacrifice.
* 5.27.23: anger as "a bitter headache that no sickness causes", which "the good drink down", is the text's image.
* 5.28.2–6: Yudhishthira's argument about dharma in distress is compressed and difficult in the Sanskrit. The novel follows Ganguli's reading and keeps each step in order.
* 5.28.11: the Shaineyas, Chaitrakas, Andhakas, Varshneyas, Bhojas, Kaukuras and Srinjayas are listed as the text gives them.
* 5.28.13 *kāśyo babhruḥ*: "Babhru of Kashi", who has Krishna "for his brother and his master".
