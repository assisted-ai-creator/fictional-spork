# Sanjaya's Message

"The Pandavas and the Srinjayas have gathered here," said Yudhishthira, "with
Janardana, Yuyudhana and Virata. Tell us the message Dhritarashtra gave you, son
of Gavalgana, suta's son."

"I greet Ajatashatru and Vrikodara," said Sanjaya, "Dhananjaya and the two sons
of Madri, Vasudeva Shauri, Yuyudhana, Chekitana and Virata, the old lord of the
Panchalas, and Dhrishtadyumna, son of Prishata and grandson of Yajnasena. All of
you, hear these words of mine. I speak them wanting the good of the Kurus.

"King Dhritarashtra welcomes peace. He had my chariot yoked in haste. Let peace
please the Pandavas. Let there be peace for the king, with his brothers, sons
and kin.

"You Parthas have every virtue. You are steady, gentle and straight. You were
born in a noble family. You are kind and generous, you hold back for shame, and
you know how to judge what is to be done. A low deed does not suit you, for your
nature is such, you who have terrible armies. If there were a stain on you, it
would show like a drop of lampblack on a white cloth.

"Who that understands would ever do a deed in which total ruin is seen for all,
where the outcome is evil and the end is hell and nothing, where defeat and
victory are the same? Those are blessed who have done what kinsmen should, your
sons, friends and kin. They would give up a life that is blamed, and then the
Kurus would be sure to prosper. If you Parthas punish the Kurus, and destroy all
your enemies and crush them, the life you would live after that would be like
death. For to live after killing your kinsmen is not good.

"Who could beat you, with Keshava on your side, with Chekitana, with the
protection of Prishata's son's arms, and with Satyaki, even if he had the gods
and Indra for his counsellors? And who could defeat the Kurus in battle, king,
guarded as they are by Drona and Bhishma, by Ashvatthama, Shalya, Kripa and the
others, and by Radha's son, with the kings of the earth? Who could kill the
great army of King Dhritarashtra's son without being destroyed himself? So in
victory or defeat, I can see no good at all. How could the Parthas, like low men
of bad family, do a deed without dharma or purpose?

"So I bow down and seek the grace of Vasudeva and of the old lord of the
Panchalas. With my hands joined I come to you for refuge. How can there be peace
for the Kurus and the Srinjayas? Vasudeva and Dhananjaya would never refuse to
do whatever you say. If they were asked for their lives first, what else would
they refuse? I say this, learned man, for the sake of peace. This is the mind of
the king, with Bhishma at his head: that there should be the greatest peace for
you here."

---

"What words of mine have you heard, Sanjaya," said Yudhishthira, "that want war,
that you should fear war? Not fighting is better than fighting, my son. Who
would ever fight, suta, if he could have that instead?

"If a man could get whatever he wished in his mind without doing anything,
Sanjaya, he would do nothing at all. I know that. And war is far less light a
thing than most. How could a man who understands ever choose war? Who is so
cursed by the gods that he would choose war? The Parthas want happiness, and
they act for it, by means that do not fall short of dharma and that are good for
the world.

"A man who hopes for happiness from his deeds finds that deeds are truly
painful, a hard road. A man who wants happiness and wants to be rid of pain, and
who follows wherever the pleasure of the senses leads him, is tormented in his
own body by his longing. Driven by it, he brings pain on himself. A fire grows
stronger the more it is fed. In the same way desire grows with the getting of
what it wants, and is never satisfied, like a fire fed with butter. Look at this
great heap of pleasures King Dhritarashtra has, and look at us. The unlucky are
not the masters of wars. The unlucky do not hear the sound of singing. The
unlucky do not enjoy garlands and perfumes, or ointments, or fine cloaks. How
then could he push us out from the Kurus? Here too, this desire ought to be
shaken off. It torments the heart inside the body.

"The king stands on crooked ground himself, and he looks for straightness in
others. That is not good. As he sees his own conduct, so he will find others'.
He is like a man who lets loose a fire close by in the hot season, in a deep
thicket, and then grieves when it grows with the wind, wanting to be safe when
the cold season is over. Now that he has all that power, Sanjaya, why does King
Dhritarashtra keep lamenting? Because he took up his evil-minded son, who
delights in crookedness, a slow-witted fool who will take no counsel.

"Suyodhana is untrustworthy, and he scorned the words of Vidura, the most
trustworthy of men. King Dhritarashtra, wanting to please his son, knows what he
is doing, and goes straight into adharma. Out of love for his son, suta, King
Dhritarashtra did not keep in mind Vidura, who is wise, who wants the good of
the Kurus, so learned, so eloquent and of such good character. That son of his
destroys honour. He loves only himself, and he is envious. He is quick to anger,
and he breaks the rules of purpose and of dharma. He speaks harshly, and goes
wherever his anger takes him. He is ruled by desire, he is a bad friend and
wicked, he cannot be led, he will come to no good. His anger lasts long, he
betrays his friends, and his mind is set on evil. King Dhritarashtra, wanting to
please that son, and seeing all this, gave up dharma and desire.

"Even then, Sanjaya, when I was playing at dice, it was coming. Otherwise there
would have been no destruction coming to the Kurus. When Vidura spoke words of
wisdom, he found no praise from Dhritarashtra. While they followed the steward's
advice, no trouble came to the Kurus. As long as they followed his wisdom, their
kingdom grew.

"Hear from me now who the counsellors are of Dhritarashtra's son, so greedy for
wealth: Duhshasana, Shakuni, the suta's son. See how deluded he is, son of
Gavalgana. Look as I may, I cannot see how there could be peace for the Kurus
and the Srinjayas, now that Dhritarashtra has taken his power from others and
has sent far-sighted Vidura away.

"Dhritarashtra and his son hope for a great kingdom on earth, without a rival.
With them, peace is quite out of reach. He thinks that what is mine is already
in his hands. As for Karna, he thinks Arjuna can be beaten in battle when he has
his weapon in his hand. There were great battles before. Why was Karna not an
island of safety for them then? Karna knows, Suyodhana knows, Drona knows, and
the grandfather knows, and all the other Kurus who are there, that there is no
bowman like Arjuna. All the Kurus know it, and the other kings who have gathered
there, how Duryodhana behaved in his wrongdoing while Phalguna, the tamer of
enemies, was away. Because of that, Dhritarashtra's son thinks he can take what
belongs to the Pandavas, his mind bound up in wealth.

"The sons of Dhritarashtra live on because they have not heard the twang of the
Gandiva drawn in battle, nor the wearer of the crown, who knows it, going into
battle with his bow as long as a palm tree. Suyodhana thinks his aim is won
while he does not feel the charge of Bhimasena in his anger. Not even Indra
could dare to take our power from us, my son, while Bhimasena lives, and
Dhananjaya, and Nakula, suta, and my hero Sahadeva.

"If the old king and his son would take this thought to heart, suta, then the
sons of Dhritarashtra would not perish in battle, burnt up by the Pandavas'
anger, Sanjaya. You know the suffering we went through, Sanjaya. Out of respect
for you, I would forgive it. Let things be as they were before between us and
the Kauravas, as we lived with Dhritarashtra's son then. I will make peace as
you say. Let my kingdom be Indraprastha. Let Suyodhana, the first of the
Bharatas, give it."

---

"Your actions are always in dharma, Pandava," said Sanjaya. "It is known in the
world, and it can be seen, Partha. Life is uncertain, Pandava, and it leaks away
fast. See that, and do not destroy yourself.

"If the Kurus will not give you your share without a war, Ajatashatru, I think
it would be better to live by begging in the kingdom of the Andhakas and the
Vrishnis than to have a kingdom by war. A man's life is short. It is full of
leaks, always painful and unsteady. Besides, war does not suit your nature. So
do not let evil loose, Pandava.

"Desires cling to a man, king, and they are at the root of every obstacle to
dharma. A steady man who strikes them down first wins praise in the world that
no one can fault. The thirst for wealth binds a man here, Partha. When he goes
after it, dharma is harmed. The man who chooses dharma is awake. The man greedy
for pleasure falls away, because he chases wealth. When a man makes dharma the
chief of all his deeds, my son, he shines with great glory, like the sun. But if
he lacks dharma, even if he wins this whole earth, a man of evil mind comes to
grief.

"You have studied the Veda and lived the celibate life. You have worshipped with
sacrifices and given to brahmins. Thinking of the highest place, you have given
yourself up to pleasures for years on end. A man who spends too much time on
what is pleasant and dear, and does not do the work of yoga, lies in great pain
when his wealth runs out and his pleasures are gone, driven by the force of his
desires. In the same way, a man caught up in chasing wealth, who gives up dharma
and does adharma, and does not believe in the other world, is a fool. When he
leaves his body, the dull man suffers after death.

"Deeds are not lost in the next world, good or evil. A man's merit and his sin
go before him, and the doer follows after. Your deeds are famous: food fairly
come by, purified by faith, full of fragrance and flavour, given to brahmins at
the rites for the ancestors with the best of fees. Here, in this field, is where
work is done, Partha. Nothing is done after death. You have done what is needed
for the other world, great merit, praised by the good.

"There, a man leaves behind death, old age and fear. There is no hunger or
thirst there, nothing that troubles the mind. There is nothing to be done there
except please the senses. Such is the fruit of deeds, king. So, Pandava, with
what is dear to your heart in measure, do not throw away both worlds for long,
for the sake of anger or of joy.

"You have reached the end of deeds that are praised: truth, self-control,
straightness and kindness. You have performed the horse sacrifice and the royal
consecration. Do not now go on to the evil deed that has no end. If you Parthas
are going to do an evil deed like this, for so long, in the form of a country,
then you should have lived for years more in the forests, Pandavas, a painful
life, for dharma's sake.

"Before you went into exile, you could have used the army that was then in your
own power. These Panchalas were always your allies, and Janardana and the hero
Yuyudhana. The Matsya king Virata with his golden chariot, with his sons who
strike hard, and the kings you had conquered before, would all have come to you.
With great allies, blazing, strong, with Vasudeva and Arjuna before you, you
would have killed your best enemies in the middle of the arena, and taken away
the pride of Dhritarashtra's son.

"Why did you let your enemy's strength grow? Why did you let your own allies
waste away? Why did you live in the forests for years, and now want to fight,
Pandava, when your time is past? A man who fights without wisdom, Pandava, or
without knowing dharma, leaves the road to prosperity. And a wise man too, even
one who knows dharma, leaves prosperity if he acts out of passion. Your mind
does not rest on adharma, Partha. You have done no evil out of passion. What,
then, is the reason you want to do this deed against wisdom?

"Anger is a bitter headache that does not come from sickness. It steals fame and
brings evil fruit. The good drink it down, and the wicked do not. Drink down
your anger, great king, and be at peace. Who would want what is bound up with
evil? Patience is better for you than pleasures. Where Bhishma, Shantanu's son,
would be killed, where Drona and his son would be killed, Kripa, Shalya,
Somadatta's son, Vikarna, Vivimshati, Karna and Duryodhana, what happiness would
you find after killing these? Tell me that, Partha.

"Even if you won this earth to the edge of the sea, you would not be rid of old
age and death, or the pleasant and the unpleasant, or happiness and sorrow,
king. Knowing this, do not make war. If you want to do such a thing because your
counsellors want it, then give them what is yours and go away. Do not leave the
road of the gods today."

**Choices.**

* 5.25.3: Dhrishtadyumna is "son of Prishata and grandson of Yajnasena" (*pārṣataṃ yājñasenim*). The house reading takes Prishata as the family name and Yajnasena as Drupada.
* 5.25.6 *bhīmasenāḥ*: "you who have terrible armies", an epithet of the Pandavas here, not Bhima's name.
* 5.26.5–7: the passage about "the unlucky" is hard. It is read as Yudhishthira's argument that Dhritarashtra, with every pleasure, has no cause to drive the Pandavas out.
* 5.26.8 *viṣamasthaḥ*: "stands on crooked ground".
* 5.26.15: Yudhishthira says the ruin "was coming" already when he played at dice.
* 5.26.20 *dvīpa*: "an island of safety", a common image for a protector.
* 5.26.23 *tālamātrāyudhena*: Arjuna "with his bow as long as a palm tree".
* 5.27.13: Sanjaya's description of heaven as a place where "there is nothing to be done except please the senses" is kept.
* 5.27.16 *deśarūpeṇa*: "in the form of a country", that is, for the sake of land. The sense is uncertain.
* 5.27.27 *devayānāt pathaḥ*: "the road of the gods", the path to heaven.
