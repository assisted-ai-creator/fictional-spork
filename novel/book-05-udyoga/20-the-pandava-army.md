summary: Sanjaya names the Pandavas' allies and tells how Dhrishtadyumna and the Pandavas have shared out the Kaurava leaders among themselves, Shikhandi to fight Bhishma, Arjuna to fight Karna, and so on; Dhritarashtra laments, Duryodhana boasts that the Pandavas are only men and cannot face Bhishma, Drona and Karna, and Dhritarashtra calls him mad and begs him to give back half the earth; Duryodhana answers that he and Karna have made a sacrifice of battle with Yudhishthira as the victim, and will not give up as much ground as a needle's point; Dhritarashtra gives him up; Sanjaya then describes finding Krishna and Arjuna in the inner rooms with Draupadi and Satyabhama, and Krishna's message: sacrifice while you can, for the debt of Draupadi's cry has not left his heart, and no one can face Arjuna with Krishna beside him.

# The Pandava Army

"Whom did you see there, Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra, "gathered against us, who
will fight my son's army for the Pandavas?"

"I saw Krishna there," said Sanjaya, "the chief of the Andhakas and the
Vrishnis, and Chekitana, and Yuyudhana Satyaki. The two of them have joined the
Pandavas, each with an akshauhini, both famous chariot fighters, proud men. The
Panchala king, Drupada, came with an akshauhini. He was surrounded by his ten
sons, the heroes Satyajit and the rest, with Dhrishtadyumna at their head.
Guarded by Shikhandi, adding to his honour, he covered the bodies of all his
soldiers with armour.

"Virata, lord of the earth, has joined the Partha with an akshauhini of troops.
With him are his two sons, Shankha and Uttara, the heroes Suryadatta and the
rest with Madirashva at their head, and his brothers and his sons. Jarasandha's
son, of Magadha, and Dhrishtaketu, king of the Chedis, have come each on his
own, each with an akshauhini. The five Kekaya brothers, all with red banners,
have joined the Pandavas with an akshauhini. These are all I saw gathered there,
who will fight Dhritarashtra's son's army for the Pandavas.

"Great Dhrishtadyumna, who knows the arrays of men, of gods, of gandharvas and
of asuras, is at the head of their army. Bhishma, Shantanu's son, king, has been
made Shikhandi's share, and Virata will follow him with the Matsyas, hard
hitters. The mighty king of the Madras is the share of Pandu's eldest son,
though some there said, 'We think those two are ill matched.' Duryodhana with
his sons, with his hundred brothers, and the kings of the east and the south,
are Bhimasena's share. Karna Vaikartana is thought of as Arjuna's share, and
Ashvatthama, Vikarna and Jayadratha, king of Sindhu. And any on earth who think
themselves heroes and cannot be beaten: Arjuna the Partha has taken them all as
his share.

"The five Kekaya brothers, princes and great archers, will fight the Kekayas,
taking them as their share. The Malavas, the Shalvakekayas, and the two chief
Trigartas who are called the Samshaptakas are theirs too. All the sons of
Duryodhana and of Duhshasana, and King Brihadbala, are the share of Subhadra's
son. The sons of Draupadi, great archers with banners worked in gold, will
attack Drona with Dhrishtadyumna at their head, Bharata. Chekitana wants to
fight Somadatta in single combat, and Yuyudhana wants to fight Kritavarma the
Bhoja. Sahadeva, son of Madri, a hero who roars in battle, has taken for his own
share your wife's brother, Subala's son. Nakula, son of Madri, has taken as his
share Uluka, the gambler's son, and the hosts of the Sarasvatas. And any other
kings, king, who come against them in battle, the sons of Pandu have shared out
by challenge.

"So their armies have been divided into shares. Do without delay whatever you
and your sons must do."

"My foolish sons, gamblers at a wicked game, are all as good as dead," said
Dhritarashtra, "since their war is with mighty Bhima at the head of the battle.
All the kings of the earth, sprinkled for the rite of Death, will enter the fire
of the Gandiva like moths into a flame. I think my army is already routed by
those great men with whom we have made enemies. Who will follow it in battle
when the Pandavas have broken it? They are all great chariot fighters, heroes,
famous and fiery, like the sun and fire in their splendour, victors in battle.
Their leader is Yudhishthira, and their guardian Madhusudana. Their fighters are
the two heroic Pandavas, the Left-handed Archer and Vrikodara. With them are
Nakula and Sahadeva, Dhrishtadyumna son of Prishata, Satyaki and Drupada, and
Dhrishtadyumna's son, Uttamaujas the Panchala, and Yudhamanyu, hard to beat,
Shikhandi, Kshatradeva, and Uttara, Virata's son. With them are the Kashis and
the Chedis, the Matsyas and all the Srinjayas, Virata's son Babhru, and the
Panchalas and Prabhadrakas. Even Indra could not take this earth from these
heroes against their will, men steady in battle who could split mountains. They
have every virtue and more than human fire. And my wicked son wants to fight
them, Sanjaya, while I cry out."

---

"We are both of the same birth," said Duryodhana, "and both walk the earth. Why
then do you think victory will be the Pandavas' alone? Grandfather, Drona,
Kripa, unconquerable Karna, Jayadratha, Somadatta and Ashvatthama, great archers
of clear mind, not even Indra with the immortals could beat in battle, let alone
the Pandavas, my father. The whole earth has been brought forth for me, my
father, to crush the Pandavas, noble, steady and heroic, like fires. The
Pandavas cannot even look at my men. I have the courage to fight the Pandavas
and their sons in battle. All the kings who want to please me, Bharata, will
hold them back as antelopes are held with a snare. With a great array of
chariots and with my nets of arrows, the Panchalas and the Pandavas will be put
to flight."

"My son is raving like a madman, Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra. "He cannot beat
Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, in battle. Bhishma has always known the strength
of the famous Pandavas and their sons, great men who know dharma. That is why I
did not like a war with those great ones. But tell me again, Sanjaya, what they
are doing. Who is stirring up those fierce Pandavas again, the great archers,
blazing as fires are stirred with offerings?"

"Dhrishtadyumna always stirs them up, Bharata," said Sanjaya. "'Fight,' he says.
'Do not be afraid of the war, best of the Bharatas. The son of Dhritarashtra has
gathered kings, who will come to the tumult of battle, to the lake of armour. I
alone will take them all in battle, angry as they are, with their followers, as
the timi fish takes the fish of the water. Bhishma, Drona, Kripa, Karna, Drona's
son, Shalya and Suyodhana, I will hold them all back, as the shore holds back
the home of the sea monsters.'

"When he said this, the soul of dharma, King Yudhishthira, said to him, 'The
Panchalas and the Pandavas all rest on your firmness and your courage. Lift us
out of this war. I know you stand by the kshatriya's dharma, strong-armed one,
and that you alone are able and enough for the Kauravas who want to fight.
Whatever you arrange will be best for us, scorcher of enemies. When men are
routed and running from the battle, looking for refuge, the hero who stands in
front and shows his manhood should be bought with a thousand. That is the saying
of policy. You are a hero and a champion, bull among men, full of courage. You
will surely be the saviour of the frightened in battle.'

"When Yudhishthira, the soul of dharma, son of Kunti, had said this,
Dhrishtadyumna said to me, without fear, 'Suta, speak to all the peoples who are
Duryodhana's fighters. Speak to the Kurus with the Bahlikas, to the descendants
of Pratipa and to Sharadvat's son. Speak to the suta's son and to Drona, to
Jayadratha and Drona's son, to Duhshasana and Vikarna, and to King Duryodhana.
And go quickly and say to Bhishma: "Go to Yudhishthira with good grace. Do not
let Arjuna, guarded by the gods, kill you. Quickly ask the Pandava, the hero of
the world, for peace. There is no fighter on earth like the Left-handed Archer,
the Pandava, the best knower of weapons. The Gandiva bowman's heavenly chariot
was made by the gods. He cannot be beaten by a man. Do not set your hearts on
war."'"

---

"The Pandava has had the fire of a kshatriya and the life of a celibate from his
youth," said Dhritarashtra. "And my fools will go to war with him while I wail.
Duryodhana, turn back from war, best of the Bharatas. They never praise war in
any circumstance, tamer of enemies. Half the earth is enough for you and your
ministers to live on. Give the sons of Pandu their fair share, tamer of enemies.
All the Kurus think it would be right, in keeping with dharma, for you to want
peace with the great sons of Pandu. Look at your own army, my son. Your weakness
is already born, and in your folly you do not see it. I do not want war. Bahlika
does not want it. Neither does Bhishma, nor Drona, nor Ashvatthama, nor Sanjaya,
nor Somadatta, nor Shalya. Kripa does not want war, nor Satyavrata, nor
Purumitra, nor Jaya, nor Bhurishravas. The men on whom the Kurus would lean when
their enemies pressed them do not welcome war. Let that please you too, my son.
You do not do this of your own will. Karna makes you do it, and wicked
Duhshasana, and Shakuni, son of Subala."

"I do not call up this war relying on you, father," said Duryodhana. "Nor do I
rely on Drona, nor on Ashvatthama, nor on Sanjaya, nor on Vikarna, nor on the
Kamboja, nor on Kripa, nor on Bahlika, nor on Satyavrata or Purumitra or
Bhurishravas, or on any other of yours. Karna and I, father, have spread out the
sacrifice of battle, with Yudhishthira as the victim, and we are consecrated,
bull of the Bharatas. The chariot is the altar, the sword the ladle, the mace
the spoon, the armour the shed, my horses the four priests, the arrows the
sacred grass, and fame the offering. We two will offer to Vaivasvata in battle,
king, with the sacrifice of our own selves, and come back victorious, our
enemies killed, full of fortune. Karna and I, father, and my brother Duhshasana,
we three will kill the Pandavas in battle. Either I will kill the Pandavas and
rule this earth, or the sons of Pandu will kill me and enjoy it. I have given up
life, wealth and kingdom, king. I will never live side by side with the
Pandavas, unshaken one. Not so much of the earth as the tip of a sharp needle
could pierce, sir, will I give up to the Pandavas."

"I grieve for all of you, my friends," said Dhritarashtra. "I have given
Duryodhana up. You follow the fool on his way to the house of Vaivasvata. Like
tigers among herds of ruru deer, the Pandavas, best of fighters, will kill the
best of you one after another when they meet in battle. It seems to me already
that the army of the Bharatas is overturned by long-armed Yuyudhana, scattered,
terrified, trampled, like a woman with her parting ruffled. Madhava, Shini's
grandson, will fill the Partha's army, already full, and stand in the battle,
sowing arrows like seed. Bhimasena will be at the head of the army among the
fighters, and they will all take shelter behind him as behind a wall, with
nothing to fear. You will see the elephants struck down by Bhima, their tusks
broken, huge as mountains, their temples split and streaming with blood. When
you see them in battle like mountains torn apart, you will be afraid of Bhima's
touch and remember my words. When you see the army burned up by Bhimasena, its
chariots and elephants killed, like the path of a fire, you will remember my
words. Great fear is coming to you, if you do not make peace with the Pandavas.
Struck down by Bhimasena's mace, you will find peace then. When you see the army
of the Kurus cut down in battle like a great forest, then you will remember my
words."

When he had said this much to all the kings, great king, and spoken to each of
them, the king questioned Sanjaya again.

---

"Tell me what the two great ones, Vasudeva and Dhananjaya, said," said
Dhritarashtra. "I want to hear your words, wise one."

"Listen, king," said Sanjaya, "to how I saw Krishna and Dhananjaya, and I will
tell you what the heroes said, Bharata. With my eyes on my toes, my hands
joined, careful, I went into the inner rooms, king, to speak with those two gods
among men. Neither Abhimanyu nor the twins go to that place where the two
Krishnas were, with Krishnaa and lovely Satyabhama.

"Both were flushed with honey wine, both anointed with sandal, both wearing
garlands, fine clothes and heavenly ornaments. They sat on a great golden seat,
bright with many kinds of jewels, spread with coverlets of every kind. I saw
Keshava's feet in Arjuna's lap, and great Arjuna's in the laps of Krishnaa and
Satya. Then the Partha pointed me to a golden footstool. I touched it with my
hand and sat down on the ground. When the Partha drew his feet back from the
footstool, I saw that the soles of his feet had the lucky marks, lines running
upwards.

"When I saw those two dark, tall young men, like two shala trunks grown up side
by side, sitting on one seat, a great fear came over me. 'The fool does not
understand that these two are like Indra and Vishnu,' I thought, 'because he
relies on Drona and Bhishma and on Karna's boasting. The purpose of the Dharma
King, whose commands these two obey, will surely be achieved in his mind.' That
was my certainty then.

"I was honoured with food and drink, and given gifts. Then I joined my palms
over my head and gave them the message. The Partha, with a hand used to bow and
arrow, drew back Keshava's foot with its lucky marks, and urged him to speak.
Krishna rose up like Indra's banner, adorned with every ornament, like Indra in
his power, and sat and spoke to me. The best of speakers spoke words that were
pleasant and fit to be heard, and that would frighten the sons of Dhritarashtra,
gentle at first and very terrible. I heard those words of his, who is worth
hearing, clear in every syllable, pleasing in their purpose, and afterwards
withering to the heart.

"'Sanjaya,' said Vasudeva, 'say these words to wise Dhritarashtra, in the
hearing of the chief of the Kurus, and of Drona too. "Sacrifice with great
sacrifices. Give fees to the brahmins. Enjoy yourselves with your sons and
wives. A great fear has come to you. Give away wealth to the worthy. Have the
sons you long for. Do what is dear to those you love. For the king is eager for
victory. This debt has grown, and it does not leave my heart: that Krishnaa
cried 'Govinda!' to me when I was far away. Your feud is with the Left-handed
Archer, whose bow is the Gandiva, full of fire and unassailable, and who has me
as his second. Who would want to challenge the Partha, with me as his second,
unless he were caught by Time, even if he were Purandara himself? The man who
could beat Arjuna in battle could lift the earth with his arms, burn up these
creatures in his anger, and throw the gods down from heaven. Among gods, asuras
and men, yakshas, gandharvas and snakes, I see no one who could go against the
Pandava in battle. What is told of that great wonder in Virata's city, of one
man against many, is proof enough. When the Kurus were broken by the single son
of Pandu in Virata's city and fled in every direction, that is proof enough.
Strength, courage and fire, speed, lightness of hand, freedom from despair and
firmness are found in no one but the Partha."'

"So Hrishikesha spoke, cheering the Partha with his words, like Indra, chastiser
of Paka, thundering in the sky and raining in season. When he had heard
Keshava's words, the wearer of the diadem, Arjuna of the white horses, spoke
great words that made the hair stand on end."

**Divergences.**

* **One couch.** Rejected lines (\*346, \*347) have Krishna and Arjuna lying on one couch, and Krishna with his head in Satyabhama's lap. In the CE they sit on one great seat, Krishna's feet in Arjuna's lap and Arjuna's in the laps of Draupadi and Satyabhama (5.58.6–7). See ledger L-176.
* **Sanjaya's wonder.** A rejected passage (\*348) adds Sanjaya's thought that he must be free of sin to be spoken to by the two with their jewels of women. Not narrated.
* **"Govinda!"** Krishna's debt for Draupadi's cry (5.58.21) is CE. The cry itself is not narrated in the CE's Book 2 (ledger L-19).
* **Arjuna's words.** The CE says Arjuna answered with great words (5.58.30), but does not give them here. In the next adhyaya Dhritarashtra weighs what Sanjaya has told him. The novel does not supply Arjuna's reply. Sanjaya gave Arjuna's message to the hall earlier (5.47).
* **Shveta.** A rejected line (\*343) adds Shveta, Virata's son, to Dhritarashtra's list. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*344, \*345, \*349–\*352) add details. Not narrated.
