# Draupadi's Hair

"Yudhishthira has said all that needs saying, Janardana," said Arjuna. "But when
I hear what you say, scorcher of enemies, it seems to me that you do not think
peace easy to win here, my lord, either because of Dhritarashtra's greed or
because of our present weakness. You think a man's courage bears no fruit, and
yet that without deeds effort brings no fruit either. What you have said is so,
and it is not so. Nothing should be seen as impossible. Or do you think that our
trouble is an evil fixed from the start? They are doing deeds that bear no
fruit. A deed properly carried out may well succeed, my lord. So act, Krishna,
in such a way that there may be peace with the other side.

"You are the best friend of both the Pandavas and the Kurus, hero, as Prajapati
is to the gods and the asuras. Bring about the good of the Kurus and the
Pandavas. I do not think it will be hard for you to do what is good for us. If
this is what is to be done, Janardana, you will do it simply by going there, no
doubt of it. And if you mean to do something else with that wicked man, hero, it
will all come about just as you mean it. Peace with them, or whatever else you
mean to do, Krishna, whatever you decide on is law to us.

"It is not that the wicked man does not deserve death, with his sons and his
kin. He could not bear to see that fortune in the son of Dharma. When he saw no
lawful way, slayer of Madhu, he took it by a cruel way, playing at crooked dice.
How could a man born a kshatriya, and a bowman, turn away when he is challenged,
even when his life is at stake? When I saw us beaten against dharma and sent off
to the forest, Varshneya, Suyodhana deserved death from me.

"It is no wonder, Krishna, that you want to act for your friends. But how is the
main thing to be done, gently or otherwise? Or if you think their death is
better, at once, let it be done quickly. There is nothing more for you to think
over. You know how that wicked-minded man tormented Draupadi in the middle of
the hall, and we endured even that. I cannot believe, Madhava, that he would
behave properly to the Pandavas. It would be like seed sown in salt ground. So
do quickly, Varshneya, what you think right and good for the Pandavas, and
whatever we must do next."

"It is as you say, strong-armed son of Pandu," said the Lord. "But all this
depends on two kinds of work, Bibhatsu. A field may be rich and clean, and
worked by the farmer, but without rain it will never bear fruit, son of Kunti.
People may say that watering it is the work of human effort, but even then the
field will surely dry out by the work of fate. So the great men of old decided
that the world turns on the divine and the human joined together. I shall do the
most that human effort can do, but I can never do the work of fate.

"That fool goes about having given up dharma and truth, and he feels no remorse
at what he does. His counsellors, Shakuni, the suta's son and his brother
Duhshasana, feed that wicked mind of his. Suyodhana will not make peace by
giving up the kingdom, son of Pritha, short of death to him and all his
followers. And the Dharma King does not want to give it up by bowing down. Even
if he is begged, that fool will not give the kingdom back. I do not think he
should even be told the Dharma King's message, Bharata, the purpose that the
Dharma King has set out. That wicked Kaurava will not do any of it. And when he
does not do it, the world will have the right to kill him.

"He deserves death from me too, Bharata, and from the whole world. When you were
all children he wronged you. The cruel, wicked man robbed you of your kingdom.
And the evil man is still not at peace when he sees Yudhishthira's fortune. Many
times, son of Pritha, he tried to turn me against you, and I did not accept his
wicked plans. You too know his mind, strong-armed one, and you know that I want
to do what the Dharma King loves. You know his heart, and you know my own mind.
So why do you suddenly doubt me, Arjuna, as if you did not know? And you have
understood the highest divine plan too, son of Pritha, which has been laid down.
How could there be peace with the enemy?

"I shall do whatever I can by word and deed, son of Pandu, but I do not hope for
peace with the enemy. How could he talk of peace like that, at the cattle raid,
when the year's road was done? Even Bhishma begged him then. They were beaten
then, as soon as you set your mind to it. And still Suyodhana was not content to
give up a scrap, even for a moment. But in every way I must do the Dharma King's
bidding. And the evil deeds of that wicked man must be shown up once more."

"The Dharma King has said many things, Madhava," said Nakula, "and he knows
dharma and is generous, and he has spoken for dharma and truly. Bhimasena too
has understood the king's mind, Madhava, and has spoken for peace and of the
strength of his own arms. You have heard what Phalguna said as well, and you
have told us your own mind more than once, hero. Set all this aside. You have
heard the other side's mind, best of men. Do whatever you think is right for the
time.

"In one situation and another, Keshava, a man's mind changes. A man must do for
himself what the time calls for, tamer of enemies. A thing is planned one way
and turns out another. Men's minds are not steady in this world, best of men.
Our thoughts were one thing, Krishna, when we lived in the forest, another when
we were hidden, and another again when we were seen. When we wandered in the
forest, Varshneya, we did not care for the kingdom as we do now. Now that we
have finished our time in the forest, hero, and people have heard of it, these
seven armies have come to us, Janardana, by your grace.

"Who here would not be frightened to see these tigers among men in battle with
their weapons in their hands, their strength and courage beyond thought? So
speak in the middle of the Kurus with gentle words first, and with fear in them,
so that foolish Suyodhana is not alarmed. What mortal of flesh and blood could
fight in battle against Yudhishthira, Bhimasena and unbeaten Bibhatsu, against
Sahadeva and me, against you and Rama, Keshava? Or against mighty Satyaki,
Virata and his sons, Drupada with his ministers and Dhrishtadyumna of the
Prishata line, the brave king of Kashi and Dhrishtaketu of the Chedis?

"Just by going there, strong-armed one, you will surely win what the Dharma King
wants, and only that. Vidura, Bhishma, Drona and Bahlika will be able to see
what is good when you tell them, blameless one. They will bring round King
Dhritarashtra and wicked Suyodhana with his ministers. When Vidura is the
listener and you the speaker, Janardana, what matter going astray would the two
of you not set back on the road?"

"What the king has said is the eternal dharma," said Sahadeva. "But you must act
so that there is war, tamer of enemies. Even if the Kurus want peace with the
Pandavas, Dasharha, bring them into war all the same. I saw Panchali tormented
like that in the hall. How could my anger against Suyodhana be calmed except by
his death? If Bhima and Arjuna, Krishna, and the righteous Dharma King hold to
dharma, I shall put dharma aside and fight him in battle."

"The wise Sahadeva has spoken the truth, strong-armed one," said Satyaki. "My
anger will be calmed only when Duryodhana is killed. You know how anger rose in
you too, when you saw the Pandavas in the forest, suffering, wearing bark and
deerskins. So what the brave son of Madri has said, that bull among men, is the
mind of all the fighters, best of men."

As wise Yuyudhana spoke, a terrible lion's roar went up from all the fighters
there. All the heroes on every side praised what he had said. "Well said! Well
said!" they cried, eager for battle, and made the grandson of Shini glad.

Krishnaa, the daughter of King Drupada, had heard the king's words, in keeping
with dharma and gain, and she was worn down by grief. Her hair was long and very
black. She honoured Sahadeva and the great chariot fighter Satyaki. Then she saw
that Bhimasena had grown calm, and she was deeply distressed. With her eyes full
of tears, the proud woman spoke to the Dasharha as he sat there.

"You know, strong-armed slayer of Madhu, you who know dharma, how the Pandavas
were cast out of their happiness by a trick, by the son of Dhritarashtra and his
ministers, Janardana. You know too, Dasharha, what King Yudhishthira told
Sanjaya in private, and you heard all that was said to Sanjaya. 'Let us be given
five villages, sir, Kushasthala, Vrikasthala, Asandi and Varanavata, and any
place at all as the fifth.' That was to be said to Duryodhana and his friends,
Keshava. And when Suyodhana heard it, Krishna, he would not do even that, though
Yudhishthira, a modest man, wanted peace.

"If Suyodhana wants peace without giving back the kingdom, Krishna, do not go
there and make it on any account. The Pandavas and the Srinjayas will be able to
stand against the terrible, angry army of Dhritarashtra's sons. Nothing can be
gained from them by conciliation or by gifts. So do not pity them, slayer of
Madhu. Krishna, when enemies are not calmed by conciliation or gifts, the rod
must be let loose on them by a man who wants to protect his life. So the great
rod must be thrown at them quickly, Achyuta, by you, strong-armed one, and by
the Pandavas and the Srinjayas. That is fitting for the sons of Pritha, Krishna,
and it will bring you fame. If it is done, it will bring happiness to the
kshatriyas.

"A kshatriya who has given himself up to greed should be killed by a kshatriya,
Dasharha, or by one who is not a kshatriya, if he is keeping to his own dharma.
The one exception, sir, is a brahmin, even one sunk in every sin, for the
brahmin is the teacher of all classes and eats first of every offering. As there
is fault in killing a man who should not be killed, Janardana, so there is fault
in not killing a man who should be. That is what those who know dharma say. Act
so that this fault does not touch you, Krishna, with the Pandavas, Dasharha, and
with the Srinjayas and their soldiers.

"I shall say again what I have said, Janardana, trusting you. What woman on
earth is like me, Keshava? I am the daughter of King Drupada, who rose from the
middle of the altar. I am the sister of Dhrishtadyumna and your dear friend,
Krishna. I came into the family of Ajamidha. I am the daughter-in-law of great
Pandu, and the queen of the sons of Pandu, who are like five Indras in their
splendour. By the five heroes I have borne five sons, great chariot fighters,
who are to you, Krishna, by right, just what Abhimanyu is.

"And I was dragged by the hair and tormented, and taken into the hall, while the
sons of Pandu watched, and while you were alive, Keshava. While the Kauravas
lived, and the Panchalas and the Vrishnis, I was made a slave of evil men,
standing in the middle of the hall. When the Pandavas looked on and did not stir
or show any anger, I longed for you in my mind, Govinda: save me! There the
blessed king, my father-in-law, said to me, 'Choose a boon, Panchali. You
deserve a boon, and I honour you.' I said, 'Let the Pandavas be free, with their
chariots and their weapons.' And they were set free, Keshava, to go and live in
the forest.

"You know sorrows like these, Janardana. Save me, lotus-eyed one, and my
husbands, my kinsmen and my family. Am I not by rights the daughter-in-law of
both Bhishma and Dhritarashtra, Krishna? And I was made a slave. Shame on
Bhimasena's strength! Shame on the son of Pritha's bowmanship, Krishna, that
Duryodhana lives even for a moment! If I deserve your favour, if you have any
pity for me, Krishna, let all your anger fall on the sons of Dhritarashtra."

When she had said this, the black-eyed woman took in her left hand her mass of
hair. It was soft and gathered up, curling at the ends, and beautiful to see. It
was very dark and scented with sweet perfumes, fine in every mark, and as glossy
as a great snake. With her eyes full of tears, lotus-eyed Krishnaa, who walked
like an elephant, went up to lotus-eyed Krishna and spoke.

"This hair, lotus-eyed one, was pulled by Duhshasana's hand. Remember it at all
times when you want peace with the other side. If Bhima and Arjuna, Krishna, are
so weak that they want peace, my old father will fight, with his sons, the great
chariot fighters. And my five mighty sons will fight the Kurus, slayer of Madhu,
with Abhimanyu leading them. If I do not see Duhshasana's dark arm cut off and
lying in the dust, what peace can there be for my heart? Thirteen years have
gone by while I waited, keeping my anger in my heart like a blazing fire. My
heart is breaking, pierced by Bhima's words like a dart, now that the
strong-armed man looks only to dharma."

When she had said this, the long-eyed woman wept, trembling and sobbing aloud,
her throat choked with tears. The hot tears fell from her eyes onto her breasts,
as if something in her had melted.

Strong-armed Keshava comforted her. "Soon, Krishnaa, you will see the Bharata
women weeping," he said. "Timid one, they will weep just as you do, their
kinsmen dead, their friends dead and their armies dead, those with whom you are
angry, proud woman. And I shall do it, with Bhima, Arjuna and the twins, at
Yudhishthira's bidding, and by what fate has ordained. If the sons of
Dhritarashtra, ripe for death, do not listen to what I say, they will lie killed
on the ground, food for dogs and jackals. The Himalaya might move, the earth
might break into a hundred pieces, and the sky might fall with its stars, but my
words will not fail. I promise you truly, Krishnaa. Hold back your tears. Soon
you will see your husbands with their enemies killed and their fortune
restored."

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi's hair.** The CE has no vow to leave her hair unbound until it is washed in Duhshasana's blood. She holds out her hair, asks Krishna to remember Duhshasana's hand on it, and says she will have no peace until she sees Duhshasana's arm cut off and lying in the dust (5.80.36–39). See ledger L-22.
* **Draupadi's cry in the hall.** Draupadi says that when the Pandavas looked on without stirring, "I longed for you in my mind, Govinda: save me!" (5.80.26, *manasā kāṅkṣito 'si me*). The CE does not say the garments at the dicing came from Krishna. See ledger L-19.
