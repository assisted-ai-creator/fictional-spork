# Rukmin

At that very time the son of great Bhishmaka came, famous in every quarter as
Rukmin. His father was Hiranyaloman, a king who was a friend of Indra himself,
lord of the Akritis, the very famous Bhoja, lord of the south. Rukmin had been
the pupil of the lion of the kimpurushas who lived on Gandhamadana, and had
learned the whole science of the bow with its four parts. The strong-armed one
had won the great Indra's bow, equal to Gandiva in power and matched with
Sharnga, heavenly and imperishable.

There are only three heavenly bows of those who move in the sky: Varuna's
Gandiva, the great Indra's bow Vijaya, and Sharnga, which they call Vishnu's, a
heavenly bow full of splendour. Krishna carried it, and it brought fear to enemy
armies. Indra's son won Gandiva from the fire at Khandava. Rukmin of great
energy won Vijaya from Druma. Hrishikesha won the excellent bow Sharnga when he
had cut the nooses of Muru, killed Muru by his strength, beaten Naraka son of
the earth and brought back the jewelled earrings, with sixteen thousand women
and jewels of many kinds.

Rukmin, having won the bow Vijaya, which thundered like a cloud, came to the
Pandavas, as if to frighten the world. Long before, proud of the strength of his
arms, that hero had not borne it when wise Vasudeva carried off Rukmini. He had
made a vow, "I will not turn back until I have killed Keshava," and set off
after the Varshneya, the best of all who bear weapons. He went with a great army
of four branches, which could strike from afar, with bright weapons and armour,
like the Ganga in flood. But when he met the Varshneya, the mighty lord of yoga,
he was beaten and shamed, king, and he did not go back to Kundina. On the very
spot where Krishna beat that slayer of enemy heroes in battle, he built a fine
city named Bhojakata. With that great army, rich in elephants and horses, that
city became famous on earth by the name Bhojakata, king.

That king of the Bhojas, of great courage, surrounded by a great army, an army
of one akshauhini, came to the Pandavas. In armour, with sword, arrows, bow, arm
guard and chariot, with a banner the colour of the sun, he entered the great
army. He was known to the Pandavas, and wanting to please Vasudeva, King
Yudhishthira went out to meet him and honoured him. Honoured by Pandu's sons as
was fitting, and well received, he honoured them all in return. When he and his
soldiers had rested, he spoke among the heroes to Kunti's son Dhananjaya.

"If you are afraid, Pandava, I am here to help you in the war. I will give you
help in battle that your enemies cannot bear. There is no man here equal to me
in courage. I will kill your enemies in battle and give them to you, Phalguna."

Spoken to like this in the presence of the King of Dharma and Keshava, while the
lords of kings and all the others listened, wise Kunti's son looked at Vasudeva
and at the Pandava King of Dharma, laughed, and spoke as a friend.

"When I fought the mighty gandharvas, hero, on the cattle expedition, what
friend was my helper then? In that fearsome fight at Khandava, crowded with gods
and danavas, who was my helper then, as I fought? When I fought the
Nivatakavachas and the danava Kalakeyas, who was my helper then, as I fought?
And in Virata's city, when I fought many Kurus in battle, my friend, who was my
helper? I have relied in battle on Rudra, Shakra, Vaishravana, Yama, Varuna and
Fire, on Kripa, Drona and Madhava. I hold Gandiva, a heavenly bow full of
splendour and strong. I have inexhaustible arrows and am strengthened by
heavenly weapons. I was born in the family of the Kauravas, and above all I am
Pandu's son. I name Drona as my teacher and have Vasudeva as my ally. How could
someone like me say, 'I am afraid,' tiger among men, words that bring disgrace,
even to the wielder of the thunderbolt himself? I am not afraid, strong-armed
one, and I have no need of help. Go, or stay elsewhere, as you wish and as suits
you."

Then Rukmin turned back his army, which was like the ocean, and went to
Duryodhana in the same way, bull of the Bharatas. He went to him and spoke to
him in the same way, and was refused by him too, who thought himself a hero. So
only two left that war, great king: Rohini's son the Varshneya, and King Rukmin.
When Rama had gone on his pilgrimage, and Bhishmaka's son as well, the Pandavas
sat down again to take counsel. The council of the King of Dharma, crowded with
kings, shone like the sky bright with stars and the moon, Bharata.

---

"When the armies had been drawn up at Kurukshetra, bull of the twice-born," said
Janamejaya, "what did the Kurus do, driven by time?"

"When the armies had been drawn up and were ready, bull of the Bharatas," said
Vaishampayana, "Dhritarashtra said this to Sanjaya, great king.

"'Come, Sanjaya, and tell me everything, leaving nothing out, all that has
happened in the camps of the Kuru and Pandava armies. I think fate is supreme
and a man's effort useless. For though I know the faults of war, which brings
destruction, I cannot hold back my son, whose wisdom is trickery and who plays
at wicked dice, nor do what is good for myself. My mind sees the faults, suta,
but when it meets Duryodhana it turns round again. Since things have come to
this, Sanjaya, what is to be will be. Giving up the body in battle, they say, is
honoured as the dharma of a kshatriya.'

"'This question is fitting for you, great king, as you deserve,' said Sanjaya.
'But you should not fasten this fault on Duryodhana. Listen, lord of the earth,
as I tell it all. A man who meets misfortune through his own bad conduct should
not blame fate or time for his sin. Great king, a man who does among men
everything that is blamed deserves death from the whole world, for doing what is
blamed. The Pandavas and their counsellors, cheated at the dice, bore insults,
best of men, waiting on you. Hear from me all of the slaughter of horses,
elephants and kings of boundless energy that happened in battle. Be steady,
great king, as you hear how it was in the great war, the destruction of the
whole world, and do not lose heart. For a man is not the doer of his good and
evil deeds. A man is not free. He is made to act, like a wooden puppet. Some are
directed by the Lord, some by chance, others by their past deeds. It is pulled
three ways.'"
