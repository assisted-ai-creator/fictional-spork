# The Elders Plead

"Lord Narada," said Dhritarashtra, "it is just as you say. I want the same
myself. But I am not the master, my lord."

Having said this, Bharata, he spoke to Krishna. "Keshava, what you have told me
leads to heaven, and serves the world, and it is in keeping with dharma and
right. But I am not my own master, my friend. What is being done is not what I
want. Please, Krishna, strong-armed best of men, try to win over my foolish
Duryodhana, who goes beyond the books. It would be a great service to a friend,
Janardana."

Then the Varshneya, who knew the truth of all dharma and gain, turned to
resentful Duryodhana and spoke to him in a sweet voice.

"Duryodhana, best of the Kurus, listen to what I say. It is for your good above
all, Bharata, and for your followers'. You are very wise and born in a great
family. You are rich in learning and conduct, and you have every virtue. You
should do what is right. It is men of low birth, wicked, cruel and shameless,
who would do the sort of thing you have in mind, my friend. In this world the
conduct of the good is seen to follow dharma and gain. The conduct of the bad is
seen to be the reverse, bull among the Bharatas. And that reverse conduct is
seen in you again and again. The adharma in this, and what comes of it, is
terrible and great, and it will take lives. It has brought you disgrace many
times, Bharata. If you avoid this harm you will do yourself good. You will free
your brothers, your servants and your friends from a deed against dharma and
without honour, scorcher of enemies.

"Make peace with the Pandavas, tiger among men, bull among the Bharatas. They
are wise, brave and full of energy. They are masters of themselves and very
learned. That is good and dear to wise Dhritarashtra, to the grandfather, to
Drona and to great-minded Vidura, to Kripa, Somadatta and wise Bahlika, to
Ashvatthama, Vikarna and Sanjaya, lord of the people, and to most of your
kinsmen and friends, scorcher of enemies. In peace, my friend, the whole world
will have peace.

"You are modest and well born, learned and not cruel. Stand by your father's
command, my friend, and your mother's, bull among the Bharatas. People think
best what a father commands, Bharata. Everyone who falls into the worst trouble
remembers his father's command. Your father wants you to join with the Pandavas,
my friend, and so do his ministers. Let it please you too, best of the Kurus.

"A mortal who hears his friends' counsel and does not follow it is burned by it
at last, like a man who has eaten the kimpaka fruit. The man who in his folly
does not accept good advice is slow, loses what he wants, and ends in regret.
But the man who hears good advice and takes it at once, giving up his own
opinion, prospers happily in the world. The man who will not bear the words of
one who wants his good, because they go against him, and listens instead to
those who go against him, falls into the power of his enemies. The man who
passes over the view of the good and follows the view of the bad will soon leave
his friends grieving at his fall. The man who sets aside his chief ministers and
keeps company with base men falls into terrible trouble and finds no way out.
The man who keeps bad company, behaves idly and never listens to his friends,
who chooses outsiders and hates his own people, is cursed by the earth, Bharata.

"You are quarrelling with those heroes, bull among the Bharatas, and you look
for protection to others, ill-bred, weak and foolish. What man on earth but you
would pass over kinsmen like Shakra, great chariot fighters, and hope for
protection from others? From their birth you have always cheated the sons of
Kunti, and they have never grown angry, for the Pandavas are righteous. You have
treated the Pandavas falsely from their birth, my friend, and those famous men
have treated you well, strong-armed one. You should do the same, bull among the
Bharatas. Do not give way to anger against your own chief kin.

"The undertakings of the wise are joined to all three aims, bull among the
Bharatas. When all three cannot be had, men hold to dharma and gain. When they
are pursued apart, the steady man holds to dharma, the middling man to gain, and
the fool to desire, which is strife. The man who is carried away by his senses
and gives up dharma out of greed, and seeks desire and gain by wrong means, is
destroyed. A man who wants desire and gain should first practise dharma. For
neither gain nor desire ever goes far from dharma. They say dharma is the means
to the three aims, lord of the people. A man who seeks by it grows quickly, like
fire in dry grass.

"But you seek by wrong means, my friend, bull among the Bharatas, a great,
shining empire, famous among all kings. The man who behaves falsely towards
those who behave well cuts himself down, king, as a man cuts down a forest with
an axe. You should not break the spirit of one you do not want to see ruined.
When a steady man's spirit is not broken, his mind is set on good. A man should
not harm one who has given up hope of life, in the three worlds, Bharata, not
even some common man, let alone those bulls, the Pandavas. The man who gives way
to resentment understands nothing. Everything stretched too far snaps. See the
proof of it, Bharata.

"It is better for you to join with the Pandavas, my friend, than with bad men.
If they are glad of you, you will get all you want. You enjoy the land the
Pandavas won, best of kings, and you turn your back on the Pandavas and look for
protection elsewhere. You have handed your power to Duhshasana, to insufferable
Karna and to Saubala, and you hope for prosperity, Bharata. But they are not
your equals in knowledge, or in dharma and gain, Bharata, and they are not equal
to the Pandavas in courage. All these kings together with you could not bear to
look Bhimasena in the face when he is angry in battle.

"Here is the whole army of the earth gathered, my friend. Here are Bhishma and
Drona, and Karna, and Kripa too, Bhurishravas, son of Somadatta, Ashvatthama and
Jayadratha. None of them can stand against Dhananjaya. Arjuna is unconquerable
when he is angry, even by all the gods and asuras, men and gandharvas. Do not
set your mind on war. Or let some man be found in all the army of the earth who
could meet Arjuna in battle and go home safe. What good will it do you, bull
among the Bharatas, to have people slaughtered? Let one man be found by whose
defeat you would win.

"He beat the gods, with the gandharvas, yakshas, asuras and snakes, at
Khandavaprastha. What man could fight him? And there is the great wonder we hear
of from Virata's city, of one man against many. That is proof enough. You hope
to beat in battle the unconquerable Jishnu, whom no one can touch, unfailing
Arjuna, the mighty hero. And with me beside him, who could challenge the son of
Pritha, even Purandara himself, when he comes against him in battle? The man who
could beat Arjuna in battle could lift the earth with his arms, burn up these
creatures in his anger and throw the gods down from heaven.

"Look at your sons and brothers, your kinsmen and your relations. Do not let
them perish because of you, best of the Bharatas. Let some of the Kauravas be
left. Do not let this family be ruined. Let no one call you a destroyer of your
family, lord of men, whose fame is lost. The great chariot fighters will make
you crown prince, and your father Dhritarashtra great king. Do not scorn the
fortune that is coming to you, my friend, and that is held out to you. Give half
to the sons of Pritha, and you will win great fortune. Make peace with the
Pandavas and do what your friends say. Then you will be glad with your friends
and have good things for a long time."

Then Bhishma, son of Shantanu, spoke to resentful Duryodhana when he had heard
Keshava's words, bull among the Bharatas.

"Krishna has spoken to you, my friend, wanting peace among friends. Heed it. Do
not give way to anger. If you do not do what the great Keshava says, my friend,
you will never have good, or happiness, or anything fortunate. Strong-armed
Keshava has told you what is right and what serves your purpose, my friend. Take
it up, king. Do not destroy the people. In your wickedness you will lose this
blazing fortune of the Bharatas among all kings, while Dhritarashtra still
lives. By your evil thinking you will lose your life, and your ministers, your
sons, your cattle, your kin and your friends, if you pass over the true and
useful words of Keshava, of your father, and of wise Vidura, best of the
Bharatas. Do not be the destroyer of your family, the last of your line, a man
of evil mind on an evil road. Do not give your old father and mother up to
grief."

Then Drona spoke to Duryodhana there, as he sighed again and again in his
resentment.

"Keshava has told you what is right and useful, my friend, and so has Bhishma,
son of Shantanu. Accept it, lord of men. These two are wise and clever,
self-controlled, learned and wanting your good. They have told you what is good
for you. Take it, scorcher of enemies. Do what Krishna and Bhishma have said,
you who are very wise. Do not follow the words of shallow minds, scorcher of
enemies. Those who egg you on are never any use for the work. In battle they
will hang the enemy's hatred round someone else's neck. Do not have all the
Kurus killed, your sons and your brothers too. Where Vasudeva and Arjuna are,
know that the army cannot be beaten.

"This is the true view of your two friends, Krishna and Bhishma. If you do not
take it, my friend, you will be sorry later, Bharata. Arjuna is even greater
than Jamadagnya said, and Krishna, son of Devaki, is hard for the gods
themselves to withstand. What good is there in telling you what you like to
hear, bull among the Bharatas? I have told you all this. Do as you please. I
cannot speak to you again, best of the Bharatas."

In a pause in the talk, the steward Vidura too spoke, looking at resentful
Duryodhana, son of Dhritarashtra.

"Duryodhana, bull among the Bharatas, I do not grieve for you. I grieve for
these two old people, Gandhari and your father. They will wander without a
protector, with you, their evil-hearted protector, their friends dead and their
ministers dead, like two birds whose wings are clipped. They will wander this
earth as beggars, grieving, for giving birth to such an evil man, a destroyer of
his family."

Then King Dhritarashtra spoke to Duryodhana, who sat with his brothers,
surrounded by kings.

"Duryodhana, listen to what the great Shauri has said. Accept it. It is good,
and it is lasting and unfailing, and it brings security and gain. With Krishna,
whose deeds never fail, as our ally, we shall win all we want among all the
kings. Join with Keshava and go to Yudhishthira, my son. Bring about the good
fortune and the health of all the Bharatas. Go to the meeting, my son, with
Vasudeva as your way across. I think the time has come for it. Do not let it
pass, Duryodhana. If you refuse Keshava when he asks for peace and speaks for
your sake, you will not escape defeat."

When they heard what Dhritarashtra said, Bhishma and Drona both agreed, and they
spoke to Duryodhana, who went beyond all commands.

"As long as the two Krishnas are not armed, as long as the Gandiva rests, as
long as Dhaumya does not pour the enemy's army into the fire of battle, let the
slaughter end. As long as Yudhishthira, the great archer whom modesty restrains,
does not look in anger on your army, let the slaughter end. As long as
Bhimasena, son of Pritha, the great archer, is not seen standing among his
troops, let the slaughter end. As long as he does not roam the lines, heartening
his army, as long as he does not strike off the heads of the elephant fighters
in battle with his hero-killing mace, like fruit ripened by time from a tree,
let the slaughter end.

"Nakula and Sahadeva, Dhrishtadyumna of the Prishatas, Virata, Shikhandi and
Shishupala's son, armoured and skilled in weapons, shooting fast: as long as
they do not plunge in like crocodiles into the great ocean, let the slaughter
end. As long as the fierce vulture-feathered arrows do not fall on the tender
bodies of kings, let the slaughter end. As long as great iron arrows, shot fast
by great archers skilled in weapons, who hit far and never miss the mark, do not
strike the warriors' chests, smeared with sandal and aloe and hung with
necklaces and gold, let the slaughter end.

"Let Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, elephant among kings, take you in his hands
as you bow your head to him. Let that generous man lay his right arm, marked
with the banner, the goad and the flag, on your shoulder, bull among the
Bharatas, for peace. Let him stroke your back as you sit, with his hand, its
fingers bright with jewels and healing gems. Let strong-armed Vrikodara, with
shoulders like a shala tree, embrace you and speak to you gently, for peace,
bull among the Bharatas. When Arjuna and the twins, the three of them, greet
you, kiss them on the head and speak to them with love, king. Let the kings shed
tears of joy to see you with your brothers, the heroic Pandavas. Let it be
proclaimed in the royal cities that the kings have every fortune. Enjoy the
earth as brothers, and be free from your fever."

When Duryodhana heard these unwelcome words in the Kuru assembly, he answered
strong-armed, famous Vasudeva.

"You should think it over before you speak, Keshava. You speak of me alone, and
you blame me. You always blame me, slayer of Madhu, for no reason, out of your
devotion to the sons of Pritha. What have you weighed up, what strength or
weakness? You, the steward, the king, the teacher and the grandfather, all of
you blame me and no other king. And I see no fault of mine here. Yet all of you
and the kings hate me. I have thought it over, Keshava, tamer of enemies, and I
can see no great wrong I have done, not even the smallest.

"The Pandavas came gladly to the dicing, slayer of Madhu, and Shakuni won their
kingdom. How was that my wrongdoing? Whatever wealth the Pandavas lost there,
slayer of Madhu, they were allowed to take back at the time. It is not our
fault, best of victors, that the unconquerable sons of Pritha were beaten at the
dice and sent to the forest. On what charge do they set themselves against us,
Krishna, as if we were their enemies? The Pandavas are weak, Krishna, yet they
are glad to fight. What have we done to them? For what wrong do the Pandavas and
the Srinjayas want to kill the sons of Dhritarashtra?

"We shall not bow down here in fear of any fierce deed or word, not even from
fear of Shatakratu. And I do not see the kshatriya, Krishna, killer of enemies,
who would dare to beat us in battle. Not even the gods could beat Bhishma, Kripa
and Drona in battle with their followers, slayer of Madhu, let alone the
Pandavas. If we keep to our own dharma, Madhava, and meet death by the sword in
battle when the time comes, that will be heaven for us. This is our chief dharma
as kshatriyas, Janardana, that we should lie in battle on a bed of arrows. If we
win a hero's bed in battle, without bowing to our enemies, we shall have no
regret, Madhava.

"Who that is born in a good family and lives by the kshatriya's dharma would bow
down to anyone here out of fear, thinking only of his living? A man should stand
up and not bend, for effort is manhood. He should break at the joint rather than
bend to anyone here. So say those who want what is good, following the words of
Matanga. A man like me should bow only to dharma and to the brahmins. He should
think of no one else and behave so as long as he lives. This is the dharma of
kshatriyas, and it has always been my view.

"The share of the kingdom that my father once allowed them will never be theirs
again while I live, Keshava. As long as King Dhritarashtra lives, Janardana, we,
or they either, must lay down our weapons and live on him, Madhava. If the
kingdom, which should not have been given, was given away before, when I was a
child and in others' power, out of ignorance or fear, Janardana, the Pandavas
shall not have it again now, joy of the Vrishnis, while I live, strong-armed
Keshava. As much of our land as the point of a sharp needle can pierce, Madhava,
even that shall not be given up to the Pandavas."

**Divergences.**

* **The needle's point.** Duryodhana's refusal to give up as much land as the point of a sharp needle can pierce is CE (5.125.26). He says it to Krishna in the Kuru assembly, after refusing to give back the half kingdom his father once granted. See ledger L-180.
* **Arjuna and Shiva.** A rejected line (\*475) adds, among Arjuna's deeds, that he satisfied Mahadeva himself in battle. Not narrated here.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*472–\*474, \*476–\*477, which repeat Dhritarashtra's helplessness, add a saying on enemies, and repeat the refrain.

**Choices.**

* 5.122.20 *kiṃpākam*: the kimpaka fruit, pleasant to eat but poisonous.
* 5.122.26 *taṃ gauḥ śapati*: "is cursed by the earth", taking *go* as the earth.
* 5.122.33 *kaliṃ bālaḥ kāmam*: "the fool to desire, which is strife".
* 5.122.39 *na tasya hi matiṃ chindyāt*: "You should not break the spirit of one you do not want to see ruined."
* 5.122.40 *tyaktātmānam*: "one who has given up hope of life".
* 5.122.41 *chidyate hy ātataṃ sarvaṃ pramāṇam*: "Everything stretched too far snaps. See the proof of it."
* 5.122.59: the offer of the crown prince's place for Duryodhana, and the great kingship for Dhritarashtra, is Krishna's.

* 5.124.7 *śaiśupāliḥ*: "Shishupala's son", Dhrishtaketu.
