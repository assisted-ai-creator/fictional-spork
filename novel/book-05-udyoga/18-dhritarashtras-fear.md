# Dhritarashtra's Fear

"All these you have named are very bold," said Dhritarashtra. "But they are all
on one side, and Bhima is on the other. My fear of Bhimasena is greater, my son,
of his anger and his impatience, as a great ruru deer fears a tiger. I lie awake
all night, sighing long and hot, afraid of Vrikodara, my son, like a weak beast
afraid of a lion. I see no one in this army who could stand against him in
battle, that strong-armed one with the energy of Shakra.

"The son of Kunti is impatient. The Pandava's enmity is firm. He does not laugh
in jest, he is wild, he looks sidelong and he roars. He is very swift, very
bold, strong in arm and strong in body. He will make an end of my foolish sons
in battle. Vrikodara, the bull of the Kurus, holding his mace, will be like
Death with his staff in his hand in the battle, against my sons who are gripped
by the seizer of thighs. In my mind I see his terrible mace of iron set with
gold, raised like the staff of Brahma. As a lion that has come into its strength
goes among herds of ruru deer, so will Bhima go among my forces.

"Of all my sons, he alone was cruel in his strength. He ate a great deal and he
was always contrary, rough even as a child. My heart trembles when I think how
Duryodhana and the others, even as children, fought with him and were crushed as
if by an elephant. My sons were always hurt by his strength. That Bhima,
terrible in his courage, is the cause of the split. I see Bhima before me, mad
with anger in battle, swallowing the ranks of men, elephants and horses. He is
equal to Drona and Arjuna in weapons, and to the wind in speed. Tell me,
Sanjaya, about the hero Bhimasena, so impatient.

"I think it a great gain that the proud slayer of enemies did not kill all my
sons then and there. How will any man bear his charge in battle, when he has
struck down yakshas and rakshasas of terrible strength? He was never under my
control, Sanjaya, even as a boy. How much less now, the Pandava wronged by my
wicked sons! He is harsh, and he would break before he would bend for his
harshness. Looking sidelong, his brows knitted together, how would Vrikodara be
calmed?

"He is broad-shouldered, matchless in strength, fair, as tall as a palm tree. In
size Bhimasena is a span taller than Arjuna. He passes horses in speed and
elephants in strength. He talks indistinctly and his eyes are the colour of
honey. That is the middle Pandava, the strong one. I heard this long ago, when
he was a boy, from the mouth of Vyasa, the Pandava's true form and courage.

"With his iron staff, Bhima, the best of fighters, in his anger, will kill
chariots, elephants, horses and men in battle. He is impatient, always ready to
flare up, fierce and cruel in his courage. He was insulted by me before, my son,
when I did him wrong. How will my sons stand his thick iron mace, studded and
well jointed, plated with gold, the hundred-killer that roars a hundred times?
The fools want to cross the shoreless ocean of Bhimasena, with no raft, too deep
to wade, full of the speed of arrows, a stronghold. The children think they are
wise, and they do not listen to me when I cry out. Seeing only the honey, they
do not see the drop.

"Those who give battle to the wind in human form are surely driven by the
Ordainer, like great deer by a lion. How will my sons stand the mace, my son, of
ironwood, four cubits long, six-sided, of boundless force, painful to touch,
when it is hurled? He will whirl his mace and split the heads of elephants,
licking the corners of his mouth and breathing out steam again and again. He
will rush at his targets with terrible cries and roar back at the maddened
elephants that charge him. He will plunge into the paths of the chariots and
strike down the best of them. Could my children escape, any more than from a
blazing fire? Making lanes through my army and putting it to flight, the
strong-armed one, mace in hand, will show the end of an age, as if dancing.

"Like an elephant in rut breaking flowering trees, Vrikodara will go into the
army of my sons in battle. He will strip chariots of their men and their
banners, break their poles and smash the chariot fighters and horsemen, that
tiger among men. Like the force of the Ganga sweeping away the trees of every
kind that grow along its banks, he will go into the great army of my sons,
Sanjaya. Surely my sons and my servants and my kings, Sanjaya, will fall into
his power, tormented by Bhimasena's strength.

"It was he who, with Vasudeva's help, went into the inner rooms of the palace
long ago and struck down mighty King Jarasandha. Wise Jarasandha, the strong
lord of Magadha, had brought this whole goddess earth under his power and burnt
it. The Kurus by Bhishma's power, and the Andhakas and Vrishnis by policy, did
not fall into his power, or perhaps it was only fate. That man went to him and
was killed by the son of Pandu, swiftly, by the strength of his arms, unarmed.
What more could there be than that? Like a poisonous snake with poison stored
for a long time, he will let his fire loose on my sons in battle, Sanjaya. As
great Indra, best of the gods, killed the danavas with his thunderbolt,
Bhimasena, mace in hand, will kill my sons. I seem to see Vrikodara coming at
me, unbearable, unstoppable, fierce in his speed and courage, his eyes deep red.
Who could stand before him, even when he has no mace, no bow, no chariot and no
armour, and fights with his arms alone?

"Bhishma, Drona the brahmin and Kripa, son of Sharadvat, know this as I do, for
I know the courage of that wise man. Knowing the noble vow, and not wanting to
break from the battle, those bulls among men will stand at the front of my army.
Fate is stronger than everything, and above all for a man. Even though I see
their victory, I do not hold back my sons. Those great archers have taken the
ancient road of Indra. They will give up their lives in the battle, guarding the
glory of kings.

"As these are mine, my son, so are the Pandavas too. They are Bhishma's
grandsons and the pupils of Drona and Kripa. Whatever we have given and offered
with our support, Sanjaya, the three old men will repay out of their nobility.
For a brahmin who takes up weapons and wants the dharma of a kshatriya, they say
death in battle is the best. I grieve for all who want to fight the Pandavas.
What Vidura cried out at the start has come, this fear.

"I do not think knowledge destroys sorrow, Sanjaya. When the sorrow is very
great, knowledge itself strikes. Even the seers who are freed, seeing the
gatherings of the world, are happy with happiness and sorrowful with sorrow. How
much more am I, bound in a thousand ways here and there, to sons, to kingdom, to
wives, to grandsons and to kin? In this great doubt, what is best for me?
Thinking about the Kurus, I see only destruction. The great disaster of the
Kurus seems to begin with the dicing. The fool did this evil out of greed,
wanting power. I think this is the dharma of the turning of time, which goes on
for ever. Like the rim fixed to a wheel, it cannot be escaped.

"What should I do? How should I do it? Where should I go, Sanjaya? These foolish
Kurus are perishing, fallen into the power of time. Helpless, my son, when my
hundred sons have been killed, I shall hear the wailing of the women. How will
death touch me? As a blazing fire in the hot season, driven by the wind, burns
dry brush, so the Pandava with his mace, together with Arjuna, will kill mine.

---

"We have heard that no word he has spoken has ever been false. The man who has
Dhananjaya to fight for him could have the three worlds. Thinking day and night,
I see no one who could face that Gandiva bowman in battle in a chariot. When he
shoots his barbed and reed arrows and his shafts that split the heart, there is
no one equal to the Gandiva bowman in battle who could stand up to him. If Drona
and Karna, those two heroes, bulls among men, faced him, there would be doubt in
the world because of their greatness, but there would be no victory for me.

"Karna is soft-hearted and careless. The teacher is old, and a guru. The Partha
is able and strong, with a firm bow, and he has conquered weariness. There would
be a tumultuous battle, with no defeat on any side. They are all skilled in
weapons and brave, and all have won great fame. They would give up the lordship
of all the immortals, but not victory. Peace would surely come only with the
death of those two, or of Phalguna.

"But there is no one who can beat Arjuna, and no one who can kill him. How would
his anger be calmed, now that it has risen against the fools? Others know
weapons too, and they lose and they win. But we hear only of victory for
Phalguna. He called out the thirty-three gods at Khandava and fed the fire, and
beat all the gods. I know of no defeat of his. The man whose driver is
Hrishikesha, equal to him in character and conduct in battle, is sure of
victory, my son, as Indra is of victory.

"We have heard that the two Krishnas are ready in one chariot, with the Gandiva
bow strung. Three energies have come together at once. We have no such bow, no
such fighter, no such driver. And the fools who follow Duryodhana's will do not
know it. A blazing thunderbolt falling on a man's head might leave something
over, Sanjaya. But the arrows shot by the wearer of the crown would leave
nothing, my son. I seem to see Phalguna shooting and killing, plucking heads
from bodies with showers of arrows. I seem to see the blazing fire of his
arrows, burning everywhere, kindled by the Gandiva, burning up the army of my
sons in battle. I seem to see the great army of the Bharatas terrified by the
rattle of the Left-handed Archer's chariot, scattered in panic.

"As a great fire, rising high and fanned by the wind, burns dry brush as it
moves everywhere, so he will burn mine. When the wearer of the crown stands in
battle with his weapon raised, pouring out hosts of sharp arrows, he will be as
hard to stop as Death, who takes all, let loose by the Ordainer. When I hear
again and again of all those many kinds of things in the dwelling of the Kurus,
and all round them at the front of the battle, it is the destruction that is
coming to the Bharatas.

---

"All the Pandavas are bold and eager to win," said Dhritarashtra, "and so are
their followers, who have given up their lives and are set on victory. You
yourself told me of my enemies' courage, the Panchalas, the Kekayas, the
Matsyas, the Magadhas and the kings of the Vatsa land. Krishna, the best in the
world, who could bring these worlds with Indra under his power if he wished, is
set on the Pandavas' victory. Satyaki quickly learned all of Arjuna's knowledge.
Shini's grandson will stand in battle sowing arrows like seed. Dhrishtadyumna of
the Panchalas, the great chariot fighter, cruel in his deeds, who knows the
highest weapons, will do battle with my forces. I am afraid, my son, of
Yudhishthira's anger and of Arjuna's courage, of the twins and of Bhimasena.

"The kings of men will spread a net more than human between the armies and kill
my army. That is why I cry out, Sanjaya. The son of Pandu is handsome and proud,
fortunate, with the splendour of Brahman. He is wise, of trained understanding,
and his soul is dharma. He is rich in friends and counsellors, rich in those who
yoke and are yoked, with brothers, fathers-in-law and sons who are great chariot
fighters. The Pandava, tiger among men, has steadiness and discretion. He is
kind and generous, modest and truly brave, very learned, self-possessed, a
servant of the old, master of his senses. Who is so foolish that he would fall
like a moth into that Pandava fire, blazing and scorching, rich in every virtue,
that cannot be held back, unless he wanted to die?

"The king is like a slender, tall flame, bright as pure gold. He will make an
end of my foolish sons in war. Kurus, I think it good not to fight them. Hear
me. In war the whole family will surely be destroyed. This is my highest peace,
by which my mind is calmed. If you want no war, let us strive for peace.
Yudhishthira would not ignore us if we tried. He hates adharma, and he holds me
as its cause."
