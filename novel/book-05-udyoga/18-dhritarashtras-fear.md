summary: Dhritarashtra confesses that he lies awake at night in fear of Bhima, whose strength and anger he has known since the boys were children, and foresees him breaking the Kaurava army with his mace; he fears Arjuna even more, with Krishna as his driver, and fears all the Pandava allies; he says it would be good not to fight, and that he would work for peace; Sanjaya answers that it is not the time for regret, for the king himself wronged the Parthas from the start, smiled like a child at the dice, and let the wicked Duryodhana have his way, and his lament now is empty.
---

# Dhritarashtra's Fear

"All those you have named are full of spirit," said Dhritarashtra. "But they are
all on one side, and Bhima is on the other. I have a greater fear of Bhimasena,
my son, the angry, unforgiving one, than of anything, as a great ruru deer fears
a tiger. I lie awake every night, sighing long and hot, afraid of Vrikodara, my
son, like a weak beast afraid of a lion. I see no one in this army who could
stand against that strong-armed one in battle, whose power is like Shakra's.

"The son of Kunti is unforgiving, the Pandava holds his feuds fast. He does not
laugh in jest. He is wild, he looks askance, and his voice is loud. He is of
great speed and great spirit, with mighty arms and mighty strength. He will make
an end of my foolish sons in battle. Vrikodara, bull of the Kurus, bearing his
mace, will be like Death with his staff in hand among those who are seized by
the crocodile of his thighs. In my mind I see that terrible mace of iron from
Shaikya, adorned with gold, raised like the rod of Brahma. As a lion grown to
his strength roams among herds of ruru deer, so Bhima will roam among my armies.

"Of all my sons he alone was cruel in his courage. He ate a great deal and went
against everyone, and even as a child he was always violent. My heart trembles
when I remember Duryodhana and the others fighting him as children, crushed as
if by an elephant. My sons were always oppressed by his strength. Bhima of
terrible courage was the cause of the split. I seem to see Bhima in front of me
in the battle, beside himself with anger, swallowing the ranks of men, elephants
and horses. In weapons he is the equal of Drona and Arjuna. In speed he is the
equal of the wind. Tell me, Sanjaya, of that hero, the unforgiving Bhimasena.

"I think it a great gain that the proud killer of enemies did not kill all my
sons then. How will a man bear his rush in battle, when he struck down yakshas
and rakshasas of terrible strength? He never stayed in my power even as a boy,
Sanjaya. How much less now, the Pandava, when my wicked sons have wronged him?
He is harsh, and he would break before he would bend from his harshness. He
looks askance, and his brows are knit. How could Vrikodara be calmed?

"Broad in the shoulder, matchless in strength, fair, tall as a palm tree,
Bhimasena is taller than Arjuna by a span. The middle Pandava outruns horses and
overpowers elephants. His speech is indistinct, and his eyes are the colour of
honey. So I heard of the Pandava long ago, in childhood, from Vyasa's own mouth,
his looks and his courage, just as they are.

"Angry in battle, with his rod of iron, Bhima, best of fighters, will kill
chariots, elephants, horses and men. He is unforgiving and always enraged,
fierce and cruel in his courage. I opposed him before, my son, and he was
slighted. How will my sons bear that mace, iron, thick, well jointed, golden,
dotted with studs, a hundred-killer, roaring like a hundred? My fools want to
cross an ocean made of Bhimasena, without a shore, without a raft, too deep to
wade, full of the rush of arrows. They do not listen to me when I cry out. They
are children who think themselves wise. They see the honey, and not the fall.

"Those who fight the wind in the form of a man are surely driven by the Creator,
like deer driven by a lion. How will my sons bear that iron mace from Shaikya,
four cubits long, six-sided, of boundless power, painful to touch, when it is
hurled? He will whirl the mace and split the heads of elephants. He will lick
the corners of his mouth and let out his hot breath again and again. He will
charge, aiming his blows and making terrible cries. He will roar back at the
maddened elephants that come against him. He will plunge along the chariot roads
and kill the best, aiming at them. Could my people escape him, any more than a
blazing fire?

"Strong-armed, mace in hand, he will cut a path and drive my army before him. He
will seem to dance, and he will show them the end of an age. Like an elephant in
rut breaking down trees in flower, Vrikodara will go into my sons' army in
battle. He will leave chariots without men, without banners, with their poles
broken, and cut down chariot fighters and horsemen, that tiger among men. As the
rush of the Ganga takes the many kinds of trees that grow on the banks, he will
go into my sons' great army, Sanjaya. Crushed by Bhimasena's strength, my sons,
my servants and the kings, Sanjaya, will surely fall into his power.

"He went into the inner rooms of the mighty King Jarasandha long ago, with
Vasudeva beside him, and struck him down. Wise Jarasandha, the mighty lord of
Magadha, had brought this whole goddess Earth under his power and scorched it.
The Kurus did not fall into his power only through Bhishma's might, and the
Andhakas and Vrishnis only through their policy, or else by fate. And the hero,
the son of Pandu, went to him, strong-armed, without a weapon, and killed him.
What is greater than that? Like a snake that has stored its poison a long time,
he will let loose his fury on my sons in battle, Sanjaya. As great Indra, best
of gods, killed the danavas with his thunderbolt, Bhimasena, mace in hand, will
kill my sons. I seem to see Vrikodara charging, unbearable, not to be held back,
fierce in his speed and courage, his eyes deep red. Even without mace or bow,
without chariot or armour, fighting with his arms, what man could stand before
him?

"Bhishma, Drona and the brahmin Kripa, son of Sharadvat, know as well as I do
the strength of that wise one. They know the vow of the noble. They do not want
to break from the battle, and those bulls among men will stand at the head of my
armies. Fate is stronger than anything, and for a man above all. I see their
victory, and still I do not restrain my sons. Those great archers have taken the
ancient road of Indra. They will give their lives in the tumult to guard the
glory of kings. As my sons are theirs, my son, so are the Pandavas: they are
Bhishma's grandsons and the pupils of Drona and Kripa. Whatever support, gift
and sacrifice they have had from us, Sanjaya, the three old men will repay out
of their nobility. For a brahmin who takes up weapons and follows the
kshatriya's dharma, death in battle is said to be the best.

"So I grieve for all those who want to fight the Pandavas. Vidura cried out in
the beginning, and this fear has come. I do not think knowledge destroys sorrow,
Sanjaya. When sorrow is very strong, even knowledge is struck down by it. Even
seers who are free, who look to the holding together of the world, are happy
with happiness and pained by pain. How much more I, who am bound in a thousand
ways, here and there, to my sons, my kingdom and my wives, my grandsons and my
kin? In this great doubt, what is best for me to do? When I think it over, I see
only the ruin of the Kurus.

"The great misfortune of the Kurus seems to begin with the dice. This evil was
done out of greed by a fool who wanted power. I think it is the turning law of
Time, which goes on to the very end. I am fixed to it like the rim to a wheel,
and I cannot escape. What should I do? How should I do it? Where can I go,
Sanjaya? These foolish Kurus are perishing, fallen into the power of Time.
Helpless, my son, I will hear the wailing of the women when my hundred sons are
killed. How can death touch me? As a fire kindled in the hot season, fanned by
the wind, burns up dry grass, so the Pandava, mace in hand, with Arjuna, will
kill my people."

---

"We have never heard of a false word from the man whose fighter is Dhananjaya,"
said Dhritarashtra. "The three worlds would be his. And I cannot see anyone who
could go against the Gandiva bowman with his chariot in battle, though I think
of it day and night. There is no one equal to the Gandiva bowman, who could face
him in battle as he shoots his barbed, reed and hollow arrows that pierce the
heart. If Drona and Karna, those heroes, bulls among men, go against him, there
is doubt in the world, because of their greatness. But there is no victory for
me.

"Karna is soft-hearted and careless. The teacher is old, a teacher. The Partha
is able and strong, firm of bow, never tired. There would be a terrible battle,
with no defeat on any side. They are all skilled in weapons and heroes, and all
have won great fame. They would give up the lordship of all the immortals rather
than victory. Surely there would be peace only with the death of those two, or
of Phalguna. But there is no one to conquer Arjuna, and no one to kill him. How
would his anger be calmed, now that it has risen against the fools?

"Others know weapons too. They are beaten, and they win. But we hear only of
victory for Phalguna. At Khandava he called the thirty-three gods to battle and
gave the fire its fill, and he beat all the gods. I know of no defeat for him.
Hrishikesha is his driver, his equal in character and conduct in battle. His
victory is certain, my son, as Indra's victory is certain. We have heard that
three powers have come together at once: the two Krishnas in one chariot, and
the Gandiva bow strung. We have no such bow, no such fighter and no such driver.
And the fools who follow Duryodhana do not know it.

"A blazing thunderbolt falling on a man's head might leave something, Sanjaya.
But the arrows the wearer of the diadem shoots, my son, would leave nothing.
Phalguna seems to be shooting already, and killing already, and pulling heads
from bodies with his showers of arrows. The blazing fire made of arrows, flaring
on every side, loosed from the Gandiva, seems to be burning my sons' army in
battle. That great army of the Bharatas seems to me, already, stricken with fear
at the roar of the Left-handed Archer's chariot, and scattering in terror. As a
fire, grown great and roaming on every side, with its great flames fanned by the
wind, burns up dry grass, so he will burn up my people. When the wearer of the
diadem stands in battle, raining hosts of sharp arrows, he will be as impossible
to hold back as Death, who takes everything, when the Creator lets him loose.
When I hear, again and again, of all the things that happen in the Kurus'
houses, and all around them at the head of the battle, then surely, they say,
destruction is coming to the Bharatas."

---

"All the Pandavas are strong and eager to win," said Dhritarashtra, "and so are
their allies, ready to give their lives, set on victory. You yourself told me of
my enemies, the strong Panchalas, the Kekayas, the Matsyas, the Magadhas and the
kings of the Vatsas. And Krishna, the best in the world, who could bring these
worlds with Indra under his power if he wished, is set on the Pandavas' victory.
Satyaki has quickly learned all Arjuna's knowledge. Shini's grandson will stand
in the battle and sow arrows like seed. Dhrishtadyumna, the Panchala, cruel in
his deeds, a great chariot fighter who knows the highest weapons, will do battle
among my armies. I am afraid, my son, of Yudhishthira's anger, of Arjuna's
courage, of the twins and of Bhimasena. The kings of men have spread a net
greater than human in between, and they will kill my army. That is why I cry
out, Sanjaya.

"The son of Pandu is handsome and proud, fortunate, with the splendour of
Brahman, wise, of formed understanding, and the soul of dharma. He is rich in
friends and ministers, and in those who yoke and those who are yoked. He has his
brothers, his fathers-in-law and his sons, great chariot fighters. That tiger
among men, the Pandava, is firm and discreet, kind, generous, modest and true in
his courage. He is learned, self-disciplined, a servant of the old, master of
his senses. Who is the fool who would fall like a moth into the fire of the
Pandava, full of every virtue, blazing like a kindled fire, burning, not to be
stopped, wanting to die, his mind deluded? The king is like a tall, slender
flame, shining like pure gold. He will make an end of my foolish sons in battle.

"I think it good not to fight them, Kurus. Understand this. In a war the whole
family will surely be destroyed. This is my highest peace, by which my mind is
calmed. If you want no war, we will strive for peace. Yudhishthira would not
ignore us if we tried. He shrinks from what is against dharma, and I am the
cause of it."

---

"It is as you say, great king," said Sanjaya. "In war the destruction of the
kshatriyas by the Gandiva is plain to see. But I do not understand how you, who
are always steady and know the Left-handed Archer's strength, have fallen into
your son's power.

"This is not the time, great king, for you who have always done wrong. You
yourself wronged the Parthas from the very start, bull of the Bharatas. A father
who is good, a friend, whose mind is well set, should stand for what is good.
One who betrays is not called an elder. When you heard at the dice, 'This is
won! That is gained!' of those who were beaten, great king, you smiled like a
child. You looked on long ago when harsh words were spoken to the Parthas. You
thought, 'They are winning the whole kingdom,' and did not see the fall.

"The kingdom of your fathers, great king, was only the Kurus and the Jangalas.
Then you received the whole earth, won by heroes. The land won by the strength
of the Parthas' arms was handed to you, and you think, best of kings, 'I did
this.' When your sons were seized by the king of the gandharvas and sinking in
the water without a raft, it was the Partha who brought them back, best of
kings. And you smiled like a child at the dice, when the Pandavas were cheated
and went again and again into the forest, king.

"When Arjuna rains down his hosts of sharp arrows, even oceans would dry up, let
alone creatures born of flesh. Phalguna is the best of archers. The Gandiva is
the best of bows. Keshava is the best of all beings, and Sudarshana the best of
discuses. The shining monkey is the best of banners. So, bearing all these, the
one with white horses, with his chariot, will destroy us in battle, king, like
the wheel of Time lifted up.

"Today the whole earth, bull of the Bharatas, belongs to him, best of kings, the
king whose fighters are Bhima and Arjuna. When the Kauravas, with Duryodhana at
their head, see your army sinking, mostly killed by Bhima, they will go to their
destruction. Your sons, great king, and the kings who follow them, will not win
victory, afraid as they are of Bhima. The Matsyas do not honour you today, nor
the Panchalas with the Kekayas. The Shalveyas and the Shurasenas all look down
on you. They have all gone over to the Partha, knowing the strength of that wise
man.

"Your son, great king, the wicked man, with his followers, who by his wrongdoing
wants to kill those who do not deserve it, men of dharma, must be held back by
every means. You should not grieve over it. At the time of the dice I said so,
and so did wise Vidura. All this lament of yours over the Pandavas, king of
kings, is as empty as the lament of a man who has no power."
