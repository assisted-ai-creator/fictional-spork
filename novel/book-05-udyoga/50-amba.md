# Amba

"Then, best of the Bharatas," said Bhishma, "I went to my mother, the mother of
heroes, the daughter of the fisherman, clasped her feet and said, 'I have beaten
the kings and won these daughters of the lord of Kashi for Vichitravirya, with
valour as their price.' Then Satyavati, glad, with tears in her eyes, kissed me
on the head, king, and said, 'By good fortune you have won, my son.'

"With Satyavati's consent the wedding was near, when the eldest daughter of the
lord of Kashi spoke, shyly. 'Bhishma, you know dharma and are skilled in all the
books. When you have heard my words, which are in keeping with dharma, you
should do what I ask. In my heart I chose the lord of the Shalvas as my husband
before, and he chose me before, in secret, without my father's knowledge. How
could you, who have studied the books, keep a woman who loves another in your
house, Bhishma, and you a Kaurava above all? Think this over and decide it in
your mind, bull among the Bharatas, and then do what is right, strong-armed one.
The king of the Shalvas is surely waiting for me, lord of the people. Have pity
on me, strong-armed one, best of those who keep dharma. For we have heard that
you are true to your vows on earth, hero.'

"Then I took leave of Kali Satyavati, of the ministers, the brahmins and the
priests, king, and let the eldest girl, Amba, go. With leave she went to the
city of the lord of the Shalvas, guarded by old brahmins and followed by her
nurse. When she had come to the end of that road, she came to the king.

"When she reached King Shalva she said, 'I have come for you, strong-armed,
splendid one.'

"The lord of the Shalvas all but smiled and said to her, lord of the people, 'I
do not want you for a wife, lovely one, when you have been another's. Go back
again, good lady, to the Bharata. I do not want you, whom Bhishma took by force.
You were gladly carried off by Bhishma when he beat you out of the kings, and
took hold of you in the great battle, and beat the lords of the earth. I do not
want for a wife, lovely one, a woman who has been another's. How could a king
like me, who knows what is known and teaches others their dharma, take in a
woman who has been another's? Go where you like, good lady. Do not let your time
go by.'

"Amba, tormented by the arrows of the bodiless god, said to him, king, 'Do not
say that, lord of the earth. It is not like that at all. I was not gladly
carried off by Bhishma, destroyer of enemies. I was taken by force, weeping,
when he had driven off the kings. Take me, lord of the Shalvas, for I love you,
and I am a girl who has done no wrong. To abandon those who love you is not
praised in the dharma. I took leave of Ganga's son, who never turns back in
battle, and with his leave I came to your house. The strong-armed Bhishma does
not want me, lord of the people. I have heard that Bhishma did it for his
brother. My two sisters who were carried off, Ambika and Ambalika, king, Ganga's
son has given to Vichitravirya, his younger brother. Lord of the Shalvas, tiger
among men, as I think of no other man but you, I swear it by my head. I have
come to you as no other's before, king of kings. I tell you the truth, Shalva. I
swear it by myself. Take me, large-eyed one, a girl who has come to you of
herself, no other's before, king of kings, and longing for your favour.'

"But as the daughter of the lord of Kashi spoke like this, Shalva let her go,
best of the Bharatas, as a snake lets go its old skin. Though she begged him
with many words like this, blameless one, the lord of the Shalvas did not
believe the girl, bull among the Bharatas. Then the eldest daughter of the lord
of Kashi was filled with anger, and with tears in her eyes and her voice broken
with weeping, she said, 'Abandoned by you, I shall go wherever I go, lord of the
people. Let the good be my refuge, as surely as I have spoken the truth.'

"While she spoke like this, the cruel king of the Shalvas cast her off as she
lamented pitifully, Kauravya. 'Go, go,' Shalva said to her again and again. 'I
fear Bhishma, lovely one, and you are Bhishma's.' When short-sighted Shalva said
this to her, she went out of the city, wretched, weeping like an osprey.

"As she went out of the city, Bharata, she thought, 'There is no young woman on
earth in a worse state than I am. I am cut off from my kin, and Shalva has
turned me away. I cannot go back to Varanasahvaya, for Bhishma let me go for
Shalva's sake. Shall I blame myself, or Bhishma, whom no one can face, or my
foolish father, who held my bridegroom choice? This is my own fault, that when
the terrible fight began I did not leap down from Bhishma's chariot then and
there and go to Shalva. This is the fruit of it, that I have come to this like a
fool. Shame on Bhishma! Shame on my dull, foolish father, who offered me with
valour as my price, as if I were a woman for sale! Shame on me! Shame on the
king of the Shalvas, and shame on the Maker, through whose bad dealing I have
come to this great disaster! A man always gets what is his lot. But the head of
this wrong done to me is Bhishma, son of Shantanu. So now I see that I must be
avenged on Bhishma, by austerity or by war, for I think he is the cause of my
sorrow. But what king would dare to beat Bhishma in battle?'

"When she had decided this, she went out of the city to an ashram of great
ascetics of holy conduct. There she spent the night with the ascetics around
her. The bright-smiling girl told them everything that had happened to her,
Bharata, at length and in full, strong-armed one: the carrying off, the release
and Shalva's rejection.

"There was a great brahmin there, strict in his vows, Shaikhavatya, old in
austerity, a teacher of the scriptures and of the forest texts. The sage
Shaikhavatya, of great austerity, spoke to the stricken girl, the good young
woman sighing and sunk in sorrow and grief. 'Since it has come to this, good
lady, what can be done by ascetics living in an ashram, fortunate as they are,
great and always practising austerity?'

"'Show me your favour,' she said to him, king. 'I want to become a wanderer
here. I shall practise hard austerity. I must have done these wicked deeds in my
former bodies, in my folly, and this is surely their fruit. I cannot bring
myself to go back to my own people, ascetics, refused and joyless, turned away
by Shalva. I want to be taught the ascetic's life here, you who are free from
stain and like gods. Have pity on me.'

"He comforted the girl with examples, scripture and reasons. He soothed her and
promised, with the brahmins, to act for her. Then all the ascetics set about the
matter, thinking of the girl and asking what should be done, righteous as they
were. Some ascetics said, 'Let her be taken to her father's house.' Some of the
best of brahmins set their minds on reproaching me. Some thought they should go
to the lord of the Shalvas and press him. 'No,' said others, 'for he has refused
her.'

"'Since it has come to this, good lady, what can the wise do?' all the ascetics,
strict in their vows, said again. 'Enough of becoming a wanderer here, good
lady. Hear what is good for you. Go from here to your father's house, good
fortune to you. The king your father will know what to do next. You will live
there happily, lovely one, with every good thing. No other refuge is so right
for you, good lady, as your father. A woman's refuge is her husband or her
father, lovely one. When things are even, the husband is her refuge; when they
are not, her father. The life of a wanderer is very hard, above all for someone
tender, a princess by nature and a girl, spirited one. There are many dangers,
good lady, in your living in an ashram, lovely one, that there would not be in
your father's house.' Then the brahmins said to the ascetic woman, 'When kings
see you alone here in the empty, deep forest, they will seek you. So do not set
your mind on this.'

"'I cannot go back to the city of Kashi, to my father's house,' said Amba. 'I
shall be despised by my kin, no doubt of it. I lived there differently as a
child, ascetics, in my father's house. I will not go, good fortune to you, where
my father is. I want to practise austerity, protected by the ascetics. So that
such great ruin and ill luck may not be mine in the other world too, best of
brahmins, I shall practise austerity.'

"While the brahmins were thinking this over in this way and that, the royal seer
Hotravahana, an ascetic, came to that forest. Then all the ascetics honoured the
king with welcomes and other honours, with a seat and water. When he had sat
down and rested, and was listening, the forest dwellers talked again about the
girl. When he heard Amba's story and the king of Kashi's, Bharata, he rose
trembling, for he was her mother's father. He took the girl into his lap and
comforted her, my lord. He asked her to tell the whole story of her trouble from
the beginning, and she told it to him at length, just as it had happened. Then
the royal seer was filled with sorrow and grief. The great ascetic decided in
his mind what to do.

"Trembling and deeply distressed, he said to the stricken girl, 'Do not go to
your father's house, good lady. I am your mother's father. I shall cut away your
sorrow. Stay with me, my child. Your heart is strong enough, daughter, since you
are wasting away like this. Go, at my word, to Rama Jamadagnya, the ascetic.
Rama will take away your great sorrow and grief. He will kill Bhishma in battle
if Bhishma does not do what he says. Go to that best of the Bhargavas, whose
splendour is like the fire at the end of time. The great ascetic will set you on
the right road.'

"Then, weeping aloud again and again, she said to Hotravahana, her mother's
father, 'I bow my head to you, and I shall go at your command. But will I see
that noble one today, who is famous in the world? And how will the Bhargava kill
my sharp sorrow? I want to hear this, and then I shall go there.'"

**Divergences.**

* **Book 1 and Book 5.** Book 1 tells briefly that Amba was let go to Shalva (1.96.47–52). Here Bhishma tells the whole story in the first person. The two tellings differ on one point. In Book 1 Amba says the match with Shalva was "my father's wish" (1.96.48, *eṣa kāmaś ca me pituḥ*). Here she says she and Shalva chose each other "in secret, without my father's knowledge" (5.171.6, *rahasy avidite pituḥ*). Each is kept as told.
* **Why Shalva refuses.** Shalva refuses Amba because she has been "another's" and because "I fear Bhishma" (5.172.4–7, 22). Amba insists she was taken by force and is no other man's (5.172.9–16).
* **Whom Amba blames.** Amba blames herself, her father, Shalva, the Maker and above all Bhishma, and resolves on revenge "by austerity or by war" (5.173.3–8). She does not ask Bhishma to marry her here. See ledger L-186.
