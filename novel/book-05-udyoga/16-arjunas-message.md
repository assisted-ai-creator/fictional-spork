# Arjuna's Message

So the night passed while the king talked with Sanatsujata and wise Vidura. When
it grew light, all the kings came gladly into the hall, eager to see the suta.
Wanting to hear the Parthas' words of dharma and good sense, they all went to
the lovely royal hall with Dhritarashtra at their head. It was white as plaster
and wide, with a courtyard of gold, bright as moonlight, very beautiful, and
sprinkled with the best of water. Fine seats were spread in it, of gold and of
wood, of stone and of ivory, well covered, with coverlets on them.

Bhishma, Drona, Kripa, Shalya, Kritavarma and Jayadratha went in, with
Ashvatthama, Vikarna, Somadatta and Bahlika. Wise Vidura and the great chariot
fighter Yuyutsu went in, and all the heroic kings together, bull of the
Bharatas, with Dhritarashtra before them. Duhshasana, Chitrasena and Shakuni,
son of Subala, Durmukha, Duhsaha, Karna, Uluka and Vivimshati went in with
Duryodhana, the impatient king of the Kurus, before them, king, like the gods
going into Shakra's hall. As those brave men with arms like iron bars came in,
the hall shone, king, like a mountain cave with lions. The great archers, the
ornaments of the assembly, bright as the sun, went in and took their costly
seats.

When all the kings were seated, Bharata, the doorkeeper announced that the
suta's son had come. "Here comes the chariot that went to the Pandavas. Our
envoy has come quickly, with good horses from Sindhu."

He came up quickly, jumped down from the chariot in his earrings, and went into
the hall, which was full of great kings.

"I have come back from the Pandavas," said Sanjaya. "Know this, Kauravas. The
Pandavas greet all the Kurus according to their age. The Parthas greet the old,
and greet those of their own age as equals, and they greet the young as is
fitting for their years. Hear, kings, what I said to the Pandavas, as
Dhritarashtra told me to before I went from here."

---

"I ask you, Sanjaya, in the middle of the kings," said Dhritarashtra, "what did
Dhananjaya say, my son? He is the great man of unbroken spirit, the leader of
battles, who cuts short the lives of the wicked."

"Let Duryodhana hear these words," said Sanjaya, "which Arjuna spoke, ready to
fight, with Yudhishthira's leave and with Keshava listening. The steady wearer
of the crown, unafraid, knowing the strength of his arms, spoke to me ready to
fight in Vasudeva's presence. 'Tell this to Dhritarashtra's son in the middle of
the Kurus,' he said, 'and let the kings who have been brought to fight the
Pandavas hear it. Repeat to that king and his counsellors every word I say.' The
Pandavas and the Srinjayas listened to the wearer of the crown's strong words,
as all the gods listen to the king of the gods with the thunderbolt in his hand.

"This is what Arjuna said, the Gandiva bowman, with his eyes like red lotuses,
ready to fight.

"'If Dhritarashtra's son will not give up the kingdom to King Yudhishthira,
descendant of Ajamidha, then surely some evil deed done by the sons of
Dhritarashtra in the past has not yet been paid for. They will have to fight
Bhimasena and Arjuna, the Ashvins' twin sons and Vasudeva. They will have to
fight Shini's grandson with his weapons raised, Dhrishtadyumna and Shikhandi.
And they will have to fight Yudhishthira, who is like Indra and could burn up
earth and heaven with a thought. If Dhritarashtra's son wants war with them,
then the Pandavas' whole aim is won. Do not make peace for the Pandavas' sake.
Come to war, if that is what you want.

"'The Pandava who keeps dharma lay on a bed of sorrow in the forest when he was
exiled. A bed more sorrowful still, a last bed, waits for the son of
Dhritarashtra, when his breath is gone. Wicked Dhritarashtra's son, living by
injustice, overpowered the Kurus and the Pandavas, who have shame, knowledge,
austerity, self-control, anger, the protection of dharma and wealth. The Pandava
used no tricks. He used care and straightness, austerity and self-control, the
protection of dharma, and strength. He spoke the truth, while the other used
falsehood in the guise of friendship, and he endured, suffering beyond all
measure. When the eldest Pandava, so disciplined, lets loose on the Kurus the
terrible anger he has held for so many years, his mind roused, then
Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. As a blazing fire burns dry brush in
the hot season, so Yudhishthira, ablaze with anger, will burn up the army of
Dhritarashtra's son just by looking at it.

"'When he sees Bhimasena in battle, mace in hand, spitting the poison of his
anger, the Pandava who cannot be withstood, terrible in his speed, then
Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. When terrible Bhima, the slayer, comes
mace in hand on the sons of Dhritarashtra, like a great lion going in among
cattle, then he will regret the war. He is fearless in great danger and skilled
in weapons, and he crushes the enemy's strength in battle. He will charge the
hosts of chariots with a single chariot, striking down the bands of foot
soldiers with his mace and crushing many troops with his speed. When the hero
scatters the army of Dhritarashtra's son like a man cutting down a forest with
an axe, then he will regret the war. Dhritarashtra's son will see his great host
scattered, like a village of straw burnt by fire, like ripe grain burnt by
lightning. Its heroes will be killed. It will be turned back, stricken with fear
and mostly fled, its fighters cowed, burnt by Bhimasena with the fire of his
weapons. Then he will regret the war.

"'When Nakula, the wonderful fighter, drawing arrows by the hundred with his
right hand from his quiver, the best of chariot fighters, brings down the
chariot fighters, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. Nakula was used
to comfort, and he lay long on a bed of sorrow in the forests. When he breathes
hard like an angry poisonous snake, then he will regret the war. When the son of
Dhritarashtra sees the heroes who have given up their lives to fight the kings,
sent by the Dharma King, charging his army in their bright chariots, then he
will regret it.

"'When the Kaurava sees the five heroes, young but skilled in weapons and not
childlike in their splendour, giving up their lives and charging the Kekayas,
then he will regret the war. Sahadeva, the killer, will mount his chariot with
its smooth yoke and silent axle, its golden stars and its well-trained horses.
He will scatter the heads of kings with floods of arrows. When Dhritarashtra's
son sees him, skilled in weapons, standing in his chariot, turning about in the
battle and rushing in every direction in that great terror, then he will regret
the war. Sahadeva holds back for shame. He is skilled and truthful, very strong,
rich in every dharma, quick to act and swift. He will go at the son of Gandhari
in the thick of battle and scatter men.

"'When he sees Draupadi's sons, the great archers, heroes skilled in weapons and
expert in chariot fighting, coming on like poisonous snakes with deadly venom,
then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. When Abhimanyu, the slayer of
enemy heroes, skilled in weapons like Krishna, plunges in and rains arrows on
the enemy like a cloud, then he will regret the war. When he sees Subhadra's
son, a boy but not a boy in courage, like Indra, skilled in weapons, falling on
the enemy army like death, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war.

"'When the young Prabhadrakas, very swift, skilled, as brave as lions, scatter
the sons of Dhritarashtra and their armies, then he will regret the war. When he
sees the two old great chariot fighters, Virata and Drupada, coming on with
their two armies against the sons of Dhritarashtra and their troops, then he
will regret the war. Drupada, skilled in weapons, standing in his chariot, will
pick out the heads of the young men in battle. When he cuts them off in his
anger with arrows from his bow, then he will regret the war. When Virata, the
slayer of enemy heroes, goes into the heart of the enemy army with the Matsyas,
who look so gentle, then he will regret the war. When he sees the eldest son of
the Matsyas, Virata's son, who looks so gentle, the chariot fighter, armed in
front for the Pandavas, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war.

"'When the best of the Kurus, the good son of Shantanu, is killed in battle by
Shikhandi, our enemies will not hold out against us at all, no doubt of it. I
tell you this truly. When Shikhandi, picking out the chariot fighters, drives at
Bhishma in his armoured chariot, crushing the hosts of chariots with his
heavenly horses, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war.

"'When he sees Dhrishtadyumna shining at the head of the Srinjaya army, the man
to whom wise Drona taught the secret weapon, then Dhritarashtra's son will
regret it. When that general of boundless strength, crushing the sons of
Dhritarashtra with his arrows, the bearer of enemies, attacks Drona in battle,
then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. Modest, wise, strong, proud and
fortunate is the chief of the Somakas. No other enemies could stand against
those whose leader is that lion of the Vrishnis.

"'And let him not say to the world, "Choose another." We choose Satyaki,
grandson of Shini, mighty, fearless and skilled in weapons, the companion
without equal in war, standing in his chariot. When the lord of the Shinis, whom
I have named, rains arrows on the enemy like a cloud and covers the fighters
with a net of arrows, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. When that
great man, long-armed, with his firm bow, makes up his mind to fight, the enemy
shrink from him like cattle that smell a lion, or as from fire. That long-armed
great man with the firm bow could split mountains and destroy all the worlds. He
is skilled in weapons, expert and quick of hand, and he shines like the sun in
the sky. The Yadava, the lion of the Vrishnis, has a wonderful, subtle,
well-made skill in weapons. Satyaki has every quality of skill that is praised.
When Suyodhana sees in battle the golden chariot of Madhava Satyaki, yoked with
four white horses, then that untrained fool will regret it.

"'When he sees in battle my fierce chariot, shining with gold and jewels, yoked
with white horses, with the monkey on its banner, driven by Keshava, then that
untrained fool will regret it. The slow-witted one will hear the fierce, great
sound of my bowstring slapping my palm, like the crash of the thunderbolt, as I
shake the Gandiva in the great battle. He will see his army blinded by the
darkness of the rain of arrows and broken like a herd of cows at the front of
the battle. Then the foolish son of Dhritarashtra, with his evil mind and his
bad friends, will regret the war.

"'When I pour out arrows that split bones and pierce vitals, killing thousands
in the meeting of enemies, like lightning flashing from a cloud, then
Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war. When he sees the hosts of arrows loosed
from the Gandiva's string, falling with their sharp points and carrying off
elephants, horses and armoured men, then he will regret the war. When the fool
sees the arrows the enemy shoots turned back by mine, and cut across by my
razor-headed arrows, then he will regret the war. When my broad-headed arrows,
loosed from my arms, cut off the heads of young men, like birds plucking fruit
from the tops of trees, then he will regret the war. When he sees warriors
falling from chariots, from great elephants and from horses, killed by my arrows
and thrown down on the field, then Dhritarashtra's son will regret the war.

"'Like Time with his mouth open, I shall drive away the enemy, the hosts of foot
soldiers and chariots on every side, with blazing rains of arrows. Then the
slow-witted one will regret it. When the slow-witted one sees his own army
bewildered, covered in dust by my chariot rushing in every direction, cut down
by the Gandiva, then he will regret it afterwards. Duryodhana will see his whole
army not knowing which way to turn, its bodies cut, senseless. Its horses,
heroes, best kings and elephants will be killed. It will be thirsty, its mounts
worn out, stricken with fear, crying out in pain, struck down and killed. It
will be a heap of scattered hair, bones and skulls, like the work of Prajapati
left half done. Then the slow-witted one will regret it.

"'Dhritarashtra's son will see them together in the battle: the Gandiva in my
chariot, Vasudeva, the heavenly conch Panchajanya, the horses, the two
inexhaustible quivers, the conch Devadatta and me. I will burn up the Kauravas
like fire, bringing down the gathered hosts of robbers, starting a new age at
the end of an age. Then Dhritarashtra will regret it with his sons. The foolish
son of Dhritarashtra will regret it afterwards, with his brothers, his sons and
his army, his power lost, in the grip of anger and of little mind. He will
tremble when the end of his pride has come.

"'One morning, when I had said my prayers, a brahmin said something pleasing to
me at the water's edge. "You have a hard task to do, Partha. You must fight your
enemies, Left-handed Archer. Either Indra, lord of the bay horses, with the
thunderbolt in his hand, will go before you in battle killing your enemies. Or
Krishna Vasudeva will guard you from behind in his chariot yoked with Sugriva."
And I chose Vasudeva over great Indra with the thunderbolt in his hand as my
helper in this war. I have won Krishna to kill the robbers. I think the gods
arranged this for me.

"'The man whose victory Krishna wishes for, even in his mind, without fighting,
will surely pass beyond all his enemies, even the gods with Indra. There is no
worry about men. The man who wants to beat Vasudeva in battle, Krishna, so full
of energy and so brave, wants to cross the great ocean with his arms, the sea
whose waters cannot be measured. The man who would split a mountain with his
palm, a huge white rock, would only have his hand and nails torn, and do nothing
to the mountain. The man who wants to beat Vasudeva in battle would put out a
blazing fire with his arms, and hold back the moon and the sun, and seize the
gods' amrita by force.

"'He carried off Rukmini of the Bhojas with a single chariot, crushing the
kingdom of the kings by force, and made her his wife, blazing with glory. Of her
great Pradyumna, Rukmini's son, was born. He crushed the Gandharas by force and
beat all the sons of Nagnajit. He freed by force King Sudarshana, who was held
prisoner and crying out, the jewel of the gods. He killed the Pandya at Kavata,
and crushed the Kalingas at Dantakura. Burnt by him, the city of Varanasi was
left without a lord for years. The king of the Nishadas named Ekalavya, whom
others thought unbeatable in war, lies dead, killed by Krishna, like Jambha who
dashed himself against a mountain. With Baladeva as his second he struck down
the wicked son of Ugrasena, who tormented the Vrishnis and Andhakas from among
them, and when he had killed him he gave the kingdom to Ugrasena.

"'He fought Shalva, king of Saubha, standing in the sky, terrifying with his
magic. He caught the shataghni at the gate of Saubha with his two arms. What
mortal could stand against him? There was a stronghold called Pragjyotisha, a
terrible city of the asuras that no one could take. There mighty Naraka, son of
the Earth, carried off Aditi's beautiful jewelled earrings. The gods with Shakra
could not stand against him when they came together, afraid, to take them back.
When they saw Keshava's courage, his strength and his weapon that cannot be
turned aside, they knew his nature, and they set Krishna to kill the robbers.
Vasudeva, rich in his powers, promised to do that hard task. At Nirmochana the
hero killed six thousand. He quickly cut the nooses with their razor edges,
killed Mura and the host of rakshasas, and went into Nirmochana. There the
mighty one fought with Vishnu, who is mightier. He lies dead, killed by Krishna,
like a karnikara tree uprooted by the wind. Wise Krishna brought back the
jewelled earrings, killed Naraka the son of Earth and Mura, and came home,
surrounded by fortune and fame, of matchless power.

"'There the gods gave him boons when they saw the terrible deed he had done in
battle. "You will not tire when you fight. You will be able to walk in the sky
and on the water. Weapons will not pierce your body." And Krishna had won his
aim. Vasudeva is beyond measure, mighty, and always rich in every quality.
Dhritarashtra's son hopes to beat this unbearable Vishnu of endless courage with
his strength. When the wicked one plots against him, he puts up with it, for our
sake.

"'He thinks he can stir up a quarrel and take what belongs to the Pandavas,
which has come round to me and Krishna. He will learn the truth of it when he
goes to the battle. I will bow to King Bhishma, son of Shantanu, to Drona and
his son, and to the matchless son of Sharadvat, and I will fight, wanting the
kingdom. I think the man who fights the Pandavas is sure to die by dharma. Cruel
men beat the sons of Pandu in a lying game, for twelve years. They met with long
hardship in the forest, and one year of living in hiding. The sons of
Dhritarashtra, in their places of power, will not put up with the Pandavas
living for no good reason. If they beat us in battle, with the gods led by Indra
as their allies, then adharma would be shown to weigh more than dharma, and
surely no good deed would exist. He may not think this man is bound by his
deeds, or think us the better. Even so, with Vasudeva as my second, I hope to
kill Duryodhana and all who are bound to him. If there is no such deed bound to
men, if a man's deed is not his own, then seeing this and that, the defeat of
Dhritarashtra's son is still right.

"'I tell you Kurus plainly: if the sons of Dhritarashtra fight, they will not
live. The Kurus could save themselves only by not fighting. If they fight, no
one will be left here. When I have killed the sons of Dhritarashtra and Karna, I
will win the whole kingdom of the Kurus. Do what you have to do, each of you.
Enjoy your dear wives and your children.

"'We too have old brahmins, very learned, of good character and good family,
astrologers versed in the stars, sure in their judgment of the conjunctions of
the stars. They read the high and the low that comes from fate, the secret
things, the heavenly questions, the circles of beasts and the hours. They
foretell a great destruction of the Kurus and Srinjayas, and victory for the
Pandavas. Ajatashatru thinks so too, his aim achieved, for holding down his
enemies. And Janardana, the lion of the Vrishnis, whose knowledge is direct,
sees no doubt about it.

"'And I myself know what form the future will take. I see it with my own
understanding, carefully. My old sight does not waver. If the sons of
Dhritarashtra fight, they will not live. My bow, the Gandiva, yawns though no
one touches it. My bowstring trembles though no one touches it. My arrows keep
wanting to leave the mouth of the quiver and fly. My bright sword comes out of
its sheath, like a snake leaving its worn-out skin. Fierce voices speak from my
banner: "When will your chariot be yoked, wearer of the crown?" Packs of jackals
howl at night. Rakshasas fly down from the sky. Deer and jackals, blue-throated
birds, crows, vultures, herons and hyenas, and flocks of eagles, come following
behind when they see my chariot yoked with white horses.

"'I alone, raining arrows, could send all the kings and their fighters to the
world of death. Taking up the ways of each weapon, like a fire kindled in a
thicket in the hot season, I will let loose the Sthunakarna, the terrible
Pashupata, the Brahma weapon and the one Shakra knows. Set on killing, letting
them fly with force, I will leave no one standing. Then I shall find peace. This
is my highest and firmest resolve. Tell them so, son of Gavalgana.

"'Look at his delusion. With the counsellors he always speaks of, he thinks he
can stir up a quarrel, even against the gods led by Indra as our allies. Old
Bhishma, son of Shantanu, Kripa, Drona and his son, and wise Vidura: let it be
as all of these say. Let all the Kurus live long.'"

**Divergences.**

* **The killing of Bhishma.** Arjuna's message already names Shikhandi as Bhishma's killer (5.47.35–36). This is the text's own foretelling and is kept.
* Rejected lines (\*303–\*306 and the following passages in 5.47) add detail and epithets. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 5.47.3 *upahvare*: "in Vasudeva's presence", in private.
* 5.47.11: the sentence contrasts Yudhishthira's means with Duryodhana's "falsehood in the guise of friendship".
* 5.47.23: "the five heroes … charging the Kekayas" are read as the five Kekaya brothers on the Pandava side charging. The verse is ambiguous.
* 5.47.30 *prabhadrakāḥ*: a band of Panchala warriors.
* 5.47.37 *astraṃ … guhyam*: "the secret weapon" Drona taught Dhrishtadyumna.
* 5.47.40 *mā pravṛṇīṣveti*: read as "let him not say, 'Choose another.'" The sense is uncertain.
* 5.47.57 *prajāpateḥ karma yathārdhaniṣṭhitam*: "like the work of Prajapati left half done".
* 5.47.62 *harivān*: Indra, "lord of the bay horses".
* 5.47.68–81: Krishna's deeds are kept as the text lists them, without filling in stories it does not tell. "Ugrasena's son" is Kamsa, who is not named here. "Sudarshana" (5.47.69) is a king freed by Krishna.
* 5.47.71: "like Jambha who dashed himself against a mountain" follows the plain words; the reference is obscure.
* 5.47.73 *śataghnī*: a weapon, "the hundred-killer". Kept as the word.
* 5.47.77 *nirmocane*: Nirmochana, a place in Naraka's land.
* 5.47.93 *mṛgacakrāḥ*: "the circles of beasts", a kind of omen reading.
* 5.47.100 *sthūṇākarṇam*: the Sthunakarna, a weapon.
