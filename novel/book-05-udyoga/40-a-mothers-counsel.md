# A Mother's Counsel

"'Your heart has been made of black iron, beaten hard, my mother,' said the
son," Kunti went on. "'You are pitiless, you think only of feuds, and you cannot
bear a wrong. Oh, the ways of the kshatriyas! You send me to war as if I were
someone else's son. How could a woman say such things to her only son? What good
would the whole earth be to you if you could not see me? What use would
ornaments be to you, or pleasures, or life?'

"'Everything the wise do, my son,' said his mother, 'they do for dharma and
gain. It was with those in view that I urged you on, Sanjaya. The time has come,
the chief time, that calls for action after thought. If you do not do what must
be done now that the time has come, you will be thought nothing of, and you will
do a very cruel thing. If I did not speak to you, Sanjaya, when shame had
touched you, they would call it a she-donkey's love, without strength and
without reason.

"'Leave the road that the good condemn and fools follow. There is a great
ignorance, and these people have taken shelter in it. If your conduct were good,
you would be dear to me for it, for conduct joined to dharma, gain and virtue,
the divine and the human, and practised by the good, and never for anything
else. A man who delights in a son or grandson who is ill-bred and will not rise
up has children for nothing. The lowest of men, who do not do what they should
and do what is condemned, find no happiness here or hereafter.

"'The kshatriya was made for war, Sanjaya, and for victory. He was made always
for hard work and the protection of his people. Winning or killed, he reaches
the world of Indra. There is no such happiness in Shakra's holy house in heaven
as a kshatriya enjoys when he has brought his enemies under his power. A proud
man, burning with anger, often wronged, wanting to beat his enemies, gives up
his own life or brings down his enemies. How else could he find peace? A wise
man here wants very little that is unwelcome. The man who has little that is
welcome in the world surely has little that is unwelcome too. With nothing
welcome a man wins nothing fine. He surely comes to nothing, as the Ganga goes
to the sea.'

"'You should not think this way, mother,' said the son, 'least of all towards
your son. Look at it with pity, like one who is dull and dumb.'

"'That gives me all the more joy, that you see it like this,' said his mother.
'You urge me on, who should be urged, and I urge you on all the more. I shall
honour you when you have killed all the men of Sindhu. I see your whole victory
coming.'

"'I have no treasury and no allies,' said the son. 'Where would my victory come
from? When I understood my terrible state for myself, my mind turned away from
the kingdom, as a wrongdoer's turns away from heaven. If you can see any way,
you who are ripe in wisdom, tell me it properly, for I ask you. I shall do all
that you command, just as you say.'

"'My son, the self should not be despised because of earlier failures,' said his
mother. 'Things come that were not, and things that were are lost. But very
foolish men should not start on things out of anger alone. The fruit of all
deeds is always uncertain, my son. Those who know it is uncertain sometimes win
and sometimes do not. But those who do nothing never win at all. When there is
no effort there is only one outcome, no fruit. When there is effort there are
two: the fruit may come, or it may not. A man who knows beforehand that all
things are uncertain, prince, will drive off both growth and wealth, and the
opposite will come.

"'One must get up, keep awake and set to the work of prospering, always sure in
one's mind that it will be, and untroubled, putting luck first, with brahmins
and with the powerful. A wise king soon prospers, my son. Fortune turns towards
him as the sun to the east. I see that you have been shown examples and means
and many ways to hearten yourself. Show your manhood. You should gather the aims
of a man that you want.

"'Find out, and take care to find out, those who are angry, greedy, worn out,
insulted and scorned, and any who are rivals. In this way you will split great
groups, as the mighty, rushing wind breaks up the clouds. Give them presents
first. Rise early and speak kindly to them. They will do what you want and
surely put you at their head. As soon as an enemy knows that his rival does not
care for his life, he is afraid of him, as of a snake in the house. If he knows
you are brave and still does not bring you under his power, he will settle with
you by agreements, and in the end that will happen. When you have won a footing
by agreement, your wealth will grow. For friends favour and follow a man with
money. But when a man's wealth fails, my son, even his kin leave him. They even
distrust such a man and despise him. The man who makes his enemy his ally and
trusts him may well win a kingdom from this.

"'A king should never give way to fear, whatever the danger. Even if he is
afraid, he should not act as if he were. When people see the king afraid,
everyone is afraid with him. The kingdom, the army and the ministers each go
their own way. Some go over to the enemy, and others desert. Those who were
insulted before try to strike. Only the truest friends stay by him, powerless,
wishing him well, like cows whose calves are tied up. They grieve with him in
his grief, as if he were a kinsman already dead. Even friends you honoured
before, even those you counted dear, turn their thoughts to the kingdom when the
king falls on hard times. Do not make your friends afraid. Do not let them leave
you in your fear.

"'I have been testing your power, your manhood and your mind. I spoke to cheer
you, as a strong man cheers a weak one. If you understand this, if I am right,
then steel yourself and rise for victory, Sanjaya. We have a great hoard of
treasure that you do not know about. I know of it, and no one else. I shall give
it to you. And you still have friends, many hundreds of them, Sanjaya, heroes
who will bear joy and sorrow with you and who do not turn back. Such are the
allies and counsellors, killers of enemies, of a man who wants to rise and is
ready to strike out a little.'

"'Who, even with a small mind, could hear such words, so rich in meaning and in
phrasing, and not have his darkness driven away?' said the son. 'I must carry
this load uphill, and run with it downhill, since you are my guide, you who see
what will be and what was. I wanted to hear more and more from you, and so I
said a little here and there, and kept quiet again and again. I could not have
enough of it, as of nectar, hard won from a kinswoman. Here I stand, ready to
hold my enemies down and to win.'

"Like a good horse whipped," said Kunti, "stung by the arrows of her words, he
did all that she told him. This rousing story is terrible. It makes the fire
grow. A counsellor should tell it to a king who is sinking under his enemies'
pressure. This story is called Victory, and a man who wants to conquer should
hear it. When he has heard it, he soon conquers the earth and crushes his
enemies. It brings the birth of a son and the birth of a hero. A pregnant woman
who hears it often will surely bear a hero. He will be a hero of learning, a
hero of austerity, a hero of self-control, an ascetic, blazing with the glory of
a brahmin, praised by the good. He will be bright and strong, fortunate, a great
chariot fighter, bold and not to be touched, a conqueror who is never beaten. He
will hold down the wicked and protect those who follow dharma. It is for this
that a kshatriya woman bears a hero, whose courage is true.

"Keshava, tell Arjuna this. When you were born, I was sitting in the lying-in
room in the ashram, surrounded by women. Then a voice came from the sky,
beautiful and divine. 'Kunti, this son of yours will be equal to the
thousand-eyed one. He will conquer all the Kurus who gather in battle, and with
Bhimasena beside him he will turn the world upside down. Your son will conquer
the earth, and his fame will touch heaven. When he has killed the Kurus in a
great battle, with Vasudeva as his ally, he will raise up again his father's
share that was lost. Glorious with his brothers, he will perform three great
sacrifices.'

"Achyuta, I know him, true to his word, Bibhatsu, Savyasachin, strong and hard
to face. So let it be as the voice said, Dasharha. If there is dharma,
Varshneya, it will come true. And you, Krishna, will bring all of it about. I do
not doubt what the voice said. Homage to great Dharma. Dharma holds up all
creatures.

"Tell this to Dhananjaya, and to Vrikodara, who is always ready: 'The time has
come for which a kshatriya woman bears a son. Bulls among men do not lose heart
when they have met with a feud.' You always know Bhima's mind. The destroyer of
enemies does not calm down until he has made an end of his enemies.

"Madhava, tell glorious Krishnaa, lovely daughter-in-law of great Pandu, who
knows every fine point of dharma, 'Fortunate, famous one, born of a good family,
it is fitting that you have behaved properly towards all my sons.' And tell the
sons of Madri, who always love the kshatriya's dharma, 'Choose pleasures won by
courage over life itself. Wealth won by courage always gladdens the heart of a
man who lives by the kshatriya's dharma, best of men.'

"Who could forgive the harsh words spoken to Panchali, who grows in every
dharma, while you looked on? The loss of the kingdom was not what grieved me,
nor the defeat at the dice, nor my sons' exile. That the tall, dark woman heard
harsh words in the hall as she wept, that I think sadder still. She was in her
period, the lovely woman who always loves the kshatriya's dharma, and Krishnaa
had husbands, but then she found no protector.

"Strong-armed one, tell him, Arjuna, tiger among men, the best of all who bear
weapons, 'Walk in Draupadi's footsteps.' You know very well that when Bhima and
Arjuna are angry they are like Yama and Death. They could send even the gods to
their last end. It was an insult to them that Krishnaa was brought into the
hall, and that Duhshasana spoke bitter words to Bhima while the Kuru heroes
watched. Remind them of that again. Ask after the Pandavas, with their sons and
Krishnaa. And tell them again that I am well, Janardana. Go on a safe road, and
protect my sons."

Strong-armed Krishna greeted her, walked round her keeping her on his right, and
went out with the playful stride of a lion. Then he sent away Bhishma and the
other bulls among the Kurus, put Karna in his chariot and drove off with
Satyaki. When the Dasharha had gone, the Kurus gathered together and talked of
the great and wonderful marvel of Keshava. "The whole earth is bewildered," they
said, "and bound in the noose of death, through Duryodhana's folly. This cannot
be."

Then the best of men went out of the city and drove on, and talked with Karna
for a long time. Then the joy of all the Yadavas sent Radheya away, and urged on
his horses at great speed. Driven by Daruka, the horses went with great speed,
swift as mind and wind, as if drinking up the sky. They went through that road
quickly, like swift hawks, and brought the bearer of the Sharnga bow to
Upaplavya while the sun was still high.

When they had heard Kunti's words, the great chariot fighters Bhishma and Drona
spoke to Duryodhana, who went beyond all commands.

"Tiger among men, you heard Kunti's words in Krishna's presence, full of sense,
calm, righteous and unsurpassed. The sons of Kunti will do what Vasudeva
approves. They will never calm down without the kingdom, Kaurava. You tormented
the sons of Pritha, bound as they were by the noose of dharma, and Draupadi too
in the hall, and they bore it from you. Now that he has Arjuna, skilled in
weapons, and Bhima, well trained, and the Gandiva, the quivers, the chariot and
the banner, and Vasudeva as his ally, Yudhishthira will not forgive it.

"You saw with your own eyes, strong-armed one, how wise Partha beat us all in
battle at Virata's city before. He fixed the fierce weapon on his bow and burned
the Nivatakavacha danavas in battle with the fire of his weapon, danavas of
terrible deeds. Karna and the rest, and you too, in your armour on your chariot,
were set free at the cattle tour. That is proof enough.

"Make peace with your brothers the Pandavas, best of the Bharatas. Protect this
whole earth, which has gone between the fangs of death. Your eldest brother is
righteous, loving, soft-spoken and pure. Go to that tiger among men, putting
your fault away. If the son of Pandu sees you with your bow laid aside and your
frown smoothed out, the glorious one will make peace for our family. Go to him
with your ministers, embrace the prince, and greet the king as before, tamer of
enemies. Let Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, Bhima's elder brother, take you in his
hands in friendship as you greet him. Let Bhima, the best of fighters, with
shoulders like a lion and great round arms, embrace you in his arms. Then let
lotus-eyed Gudakesha, son of Kunti, Dhananjaya with his lion's neck, greet you.
Let the sons of the Ashvins, tigers among men, unmatched on earth for their
looks, come to meet you with love and honour, as their elder. Let the kings led
by the Dasharha shed tears of joy. Put away your pride and join your brothers,
king. Then rule the whole earth with your brothers, and let the kings embrace
each other gladly and go home.

"Enough of war, king of kings. Hear your friends' reasons. In war the
destruction of the kshatriyas is surely seen. The stars are against us. Beasts
and birds are dreadful. Many kinds of portents are seen, hero, that mean death
for the kshatriyas. Above all, the signs here point to our destruction. Your
army is struck by blazing meteors. The animals are dejected and seem to weep,
lord of the people. Vultures sit all round your troops. The city is not as it
was, nor the royal palace. Jackals howl ill-omened cries and haunt the burning
quarter. Do what your father and mother say, and we who wish you well. Peace and
war both depend on you, strong-armed one. If you do not do what your friends
say, destroyer of enemies, you will grieve when you see your army crushed by
Partha's arrows. When you hear the great roar of mighty Bhima in battle, and the
twang of the Gandiva, you will remember my words, if what I say seems wrong to
you now."

When they said this, he was downcast. He looked away and down, knitted his
brows, and said nothing at all. When the two bulls among men saw him downcast,
they looked at each other and spoke again.

"What could be sadder than this," said Bhishma, "that we shall fight Partha, who
serves his elders, bears no malice, honours the brahmins and keeps his word?"

"My respect for Dhananjaya is greater, king, than for my son Ashvatthama," said
Drona, "and so is my regard for the ape-bannered one. If I fight Dhananjaya, who
is dearer to me than my son, keeping to the kshatriya's dharma, shame on the
kshatriya's way of life! There is no other bowman in the world like him. By my
grace Bibhatsu is better than other bowmen. A man who betrays his friends, whose
heart is bad, who denies the other world, who is crooked and deceitful, wins no
honour among the good, like a fool who comes to a sacrifice. Even when he is
held back from evil, the evil man wants evil. Even when he is urged on by evil,
the good man wants good. These faults, when they are treated falsely and yet
follow what they like, lead to harm, best of the Bharatas.

"You have been told by the eldest of the Kurus, by me and by Vidura, and by
Vasudeva too, and you do not take what is good for you. You think only, 'I have
strength,' and you want to cross rashly, like the Ganga's flood in the hot
season, full of crocodiles, sharks and makaras. You think you are wearing
Yudhishthira's fortune as if it were a cloth, or as if you had found a garland
someone threw away, out of greed. Who could outlive the son of Pandu, with
Draupadi, surrounded by his brothers with their weapons, whether he is in the
forest or in his kingdom? The Dharma King met Ailavila, whom all the kings serve
at his command, and he shone there. The Pandavas went to Kubera's house and got
jewels there, and now they want to attack your rich country and take the
kingdom.

"We have given, we have made offerings and we have studied. We have pleased the
brahmins with wealth. Our lives are nearly over, and know that we have done what
we had to do. But you will lose happiness, kingdom, friends and wealth, and fall
into great disaster if you make war on the Pandavas. How will you beat the
Pandava for whose victory truthful Draupadi prays, the goddess of terrible vows
and austerity? How will you beat the Pandava who has Janardana as his counsellor
and Dhananjaya, the best of all who bear weapons, as his brother? How will you
beat the Pandava, a hero of fierce austerity, whose allies are brahmins, steady
and masters of their senses?

"I shall say again what a friend who wants his friends' good should do when they
are sinking in a sea of disaster. Enough of war with those heroes. Make peace
for the good of the Kurus. Do not go down to defeat with your sons, your
ministers and your army."

**Divergences.**

* **Karna in Krishna's chariot.** Krishna takes Karna into his chariot and talks with him outside the city (5.135.24, 27–28). What they said is told in the next chapters (5.138–141). A rejected line (\*519) has Krishna also call Ashvatthama and talk with him. Not narrated.
* **The end of Vidura's story.** A rejected line (\*514) says that Sanjaya fought his enemies and won. The CE ends with Kunti's words on the story's power (5.134.15–21). Not narrated.
* **Drona and Arjuna.** Drona's words, that Arjuna is dearer to him than his own son and that he respects him more than Ashvatthama, are CE (5.137.4–5).
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*507–\*513, \*515–\*518, \*520.
