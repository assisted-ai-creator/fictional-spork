# The Swan and the Sadhyas

"On this they tell an old story," said Vidura, "the dialogue of Atreya and the
Sadhyas, as we have heard it.

"Long ago the Sadhya gods questioned a great seer, very wise, strict in his
vows, who was going about in the form of a swan. 'We are the Sadhya gods, great
seer,' they said. 'Looking at you, we cannot make you out. You seem to us steady
in learning and wise. You should tell us noble words of wisdom.'

"'This is what I have heard is to be done, immortals,' said the swan.
'Steadiness, calm, following truth and dharma. Untie every knot of the heart,
and bring the pleasant and the unpleasant under your own control. When abused,
do not abuse back. Anger that is borne burns up the abuser, and the patient man
takes his merit.

"'Do not abuse or insult another. Do not betray a friend, or serve the low. Do
not be too proud, or behave meanly. Avoid harsh and hurtful speech. Harsh words
burn up a man's vitals, his bones, his heart and his breath. So the man who
delights in dharma should always avoid hurtful, harsh speech. Know that the man
who wounds, who is harsh and rough of speech, who pricks people with the thorns
of his words, is the most unlucky of men. He carries Misfortune tied to his
mouth.

"'If another pierces a wise man hard with arrows, very sharp and burning like
fire or the sun, the wise man, though deeply hurt, should know that the other is
giving him merit. Whether a man serves the good or the bad, an ascetic or a
thief, he comes under their power, as a cloth takes the colour of the dye.

"'Take the man who does not quarrel or provoke a quarrel, who does not strike
back when struck or have another strike. He does not wish evil even to one who
means to kill him. That is the man the gods long for when he comes. They say
silence is better than speech. Speaking the truth is the second. Speaking kindly
is the third. Speaking according to dharma is the fourth. A man becomes like
those he argues with, like those he serves, and like what he wishes to be.
Whatever a man turns away from, from that he is freed. By turning away from
everything, he does not know even the smallest sorrow.

"'He is not beaten, and he does not try to beat others. He makes no enemies and
strikes no one back. He is the same in blame and praise. He neither grieves nor
rejoices. The best of men wants all to prosper and does not set his mind on
harm. He speaks the truth and is gentle and self-controlled. The middling man
does not comfort for nothing, gives when he has promised, and knows success and
failure.

"'These are the marks of the lowest man here. He is hard to rule. He wounds and
does not teach. He does not turn back from his anger, and he is ungrateful. He
is no one's friend, and he is wicked. The lowest man does not believe that
others mean well, because he doubts himself, and he pushes his friends away. A
man who wants good for himself should serve the best, and the middling when it
is needed, but should not serve the lowest. A man may get wealth by evil force,
by constant effort, by wit and by courage. But he does not win true praise, or
the conduct of great families.'"

---

"The gods long for great families," said Dhritarashtra, "and so do those great
in dharma and purpose, and the learned. I ask you this, Vidura. What are the
great families?"

"Austerity, self-control, knowledge of Brahman, sacrifices, pure marriages, and
always giving food," said Vidura. "Those families in which these seven qualities
are found, well practised, are the great families. Some families' conduct does
not falter, and their birth is not shamed. They practise dharma with the grace
of their conduct. They want special fame for the family, and they have given up
falsehood. Those are the great families.

"Families sink into no families by not sacrificing, by bad marriages, by
neglecting the Veda and by going against dharma. Families sink into no families
by destroying what belongs to the gods, by taking the property of brahmins, and
by going against brahmins. Families sink into no families by slighting and
slandering brahmins, Bharata, and by stealing what was left in trust. Families
rich in cattle, men and horses do not count as families if they fall short in
conduct. But families that do not fall short in conduct, even with little
wealth, count as families and win great fame.

"Let no one in our family make enemies. Let no king's minister take others'
property. Let there be no betrayer of friends, no cheat, no liar, no one who
eats before the ancestors, the gods and guests. Let no one who would kill a
brahmin, or hate brahmins, enter our assembly, nor anyone who would sow his
crops on our land. Straw, ground, water, and kind words as the fourth, these are
never lacking in the houses of the good. They are offered with the greatest
faith and honour, wise king, by those who keep dharma and do good deeds.

"Even a light chariot can carry a load, king, and other things born of the earth
cannot. In the same way men of great family can bear burdens, and other men
cannot.

"He is no friend whose anger one fears, or who has to be served with suspicion.
The friend in whom one trusts as in a father is the friend. The others are just
acquaintances. If even a man who is no kin acts as a friend, he is kin, he is
friend, he is refuge and support. A man whose mind is unsteady, who does not
serve the old, whose thought always wavers, gathers no lasting friends. Wealth
passes by the man whose mind is unsteady, who has no self, who follows his
senses, as swans pass by a dry lake. The wicked grow angry for no reason and are
pleased for no cause. That is their nature, like a wandering cloud. Not even the
flesh-eaters will eat the bodies of those ungrateful men who were honoured and
helped by their friends and then failed them. A man should ask things of his
friends, whether he has wealth or not. Without asking, he cannot know which
friends are true and which are hollow.

"Grief destroys beauty. Grief destroys strength. Grief destroys knowledge. Grief
brings sickness. What cannot be had is not had by grieving. The body suffers and
enemies rejoice. Do not set your mind on grief. Again and again a man dies and
is born. Again and again he falls and rises. Again and again he begs and is
begged. Again and again he grieves and is grieved for. Happiness and sorrow,
being and not being, gain and loss, death and life, all touch us here in turn.
So a steady man should neither rejoice nor grieve. These six senses are
restless. Wherever any of them goes, a man's wisdom leaks out through it, as
water always leaks from a cracked pot."

---

"The king is like a slender flame," said Dhritarashtra, "and I have dealt
falsely with him. He will make an end of my foolish sons in war. All this is
always in turmoil. My mind is always in turmoil. Tell me of the place that is
free of turmoil, wise one."

"I see no peace for you, faultless one," said Vidura, "except in learning and
austerity, in mastering the senses and in giving up greed. A man drives away
fear by understanding, wins greatness by austerity, knowledge by serving his
teacher and peace by giving up. Those who seek freedom go about here without
leaning on the merit of giving or the merit of the Veda, free of love and
hatred. At the end of good study, a good fight, a good deed and well-practised
austerity, happiness grows.

"Those who are divided never sleep, king, even on well-made beds. They take no
pleasure in women, nor in the praise of bards and heralds. Those who are divided
never practise dharma. Those who are divided never find happiness here. Those
who are divided do not honour what deserves honour. Those who are divided do not
like peace. Good advice does not taste good to them. They do not get or keep
what they have. Those who are divided have no refuge, king of men, but
destruction.

"Milk is to be expected in cows, austerity in a brahmin, fickleness in women and
danger from kinsmen. Threads, even fine ones, when many are stretched out
together, bear many strains because they are many. That is an image for the
good. Kinsmen apart smoke, and together they blaze, Dhritarashtra, bull of the
Bharatas, like firebrands. Those who are brave against brahmins, women, kinsmen
and cows fall, Dhritarashtra, like ripe fruit from the stalk.

"A great tree standing alone, however strong and firmly rooted, can be broken by
the wind, trunk and branches. But trees that stand together in a grove, firmly
rooted, bear the fiercest winds because they shelter each other. In the same way
enemies think a man alone, however good, can be beaten, as the wind thinks of a
tree that stands alone. Kinsmen grow by holding each other up and leaning on
each other, like lotuses in a lake.

"Brahmins, cows, women, children and kinsmen must not be killed, nor those whose
food one has eaten, nor those who come for refuge. There is no other good in a
man, even among the rich, than good health, bless you. The sick are as good as
dead. Anger is a bitter headache that does not come from sickness, bound up with
evil, harsh, sharp and fierce. The good drink it down, and the wicked do not.
Drink down your anger, great king, and be at peace. Those who are tormented by
sickness care nothing for fruits, and find no flavour in the things of the
senses. The sick are always in pain. They know neither the pleasure of wealth
nor happiness.

"Long ago, king, when you saw Draupadi won at the dice, I told you, 'Stop
Duryodhana at the game. The wise avoid gambling.' You did not do what I said.
That is not strength which goes against gentleness. Dharma mixed with gentleness
should be followed quickly. Fortune won by cruelty is soon destroyed. Fortune
grown with gentleness goes on to sons and grandsons.

"Let Dhritarashtra's sons protect the Pandavas, and let Pandu's sons protect
your sons. Let the Kurus have the same enemies and friends, and one counsel, and
live happily and prosper, king. Today you are the pillar of the Kauravas. The
Kuru family depends on you, descendant of Ajamidha. Protect the Parthas, who are
young and have suffered in their forest exile, and guard your own fame, my son.
Make peace between the Kauravas and the sons of Pandu. Do not let your enemies
seek out your weak spot. They all stand by the truth, god among men. Hold
Duryodhana back, king."

---

"Manu, son of the Self-born, king of kings," said Vidura, "spoke of these
seventeen men, son of Vichitravirya. They strike the sky with their fists. They
try to bend Indra's bow, which cannot be bent, and the sun's rays, which cannot
be bent. They are these. The man who teaches one who cannot be taught. The man
who is quick to anger. The man who serves an enemy too much. The man who does
not guard women, bless you. The man who begs from one who should not be begged
from. The man who boasts. The man of good birth who does what should not be
done. The weak man always at enmity with the strong. The man who talks to one
who has no faith. The man who wants what should not be wanted. The father-in-law
who jokes with his daughter-in-law. The man who lives on his daughter-in-law and
wants honour. The man who sows his seed in another's field. The man who speaks
too much ill of women. The man who, having received, says, 'I don't remember.'
The man who, having given, boasts of it when asked. And the man who tries to win
over the wicked with kind words. These men follow the wind with nooses in their
hands.

"As a man behaves towards another, so the other should behave towards him. That
is dharma. A deceiver should be met with deceit. Good conduct should be met with
good."

"All the Vedas say a man lives a hundred years," said Dhritarashtra. "Why then
does he not reach his full span?"

"Too much speech, too much pride, too little giving, anger, too much thirst for
pleasure and betraying friends," said Vidura. "Those are the six, king. These
are the sharp swords that cut short the lives of embodied beings. These kill
men, not death, bless you. Some are equal to the killers of brahmins: the man
who goes to the wife of one who trusts him, and the man who goes to his
teacher's bed. So are the twice-born man who is husband to a shudra woman, the
drinker, Bharata, and the man who kills one who has come for refuge. Penance
must be done for contact with them, so we are told.

"The learned householder goes to heaven if he is generous and his word is never
rejected. He eats what is left over. He does no harm and nothing useless. He has
given up quarrels, and he is grateful, true and gentle.

"Men who always speak pleasantly are easy to find, king. But a speaker and a
hearer of what is unpleasant but wholesome are hard to find. The man who holds
to dharma, sets aside what his master likes or dislikes, and says what is
unpleasant but wholesome, is the one who makes a king strong in allies.

"A man should give up one man for a family, a family for a village, a village
for a country, and the earth for the self. He should guard wealth against
misfortune, guard his wife even with his wealth, and always guard himself, even
with his wife and his wealth.

"At the time of the dicing too, king, I told you, 'This is not right, son of
Pratipa's son.' Like wholesome medicine to a sick man, it did not please you,
son of Vichitravirya. You have beaten the Pandavas, those peacocks with their
bright tails, with your crows, the sons of Dhritarashtra. You have given up
lions to keep jackals. When the time comes, you will grieve, king of men.

"Servants trust a master, my son, who is never angry with a devoted servant who
is set on his good, and they do not leave him in misfortune. A king should not
try to win new outsiders by cutting the living of his servants. Even loving
ministers leave a master when they are kept from what they are used to and lose
their pleasures. He should first count all his tasks, his income and spending,
and fix fitting wages, and then gather fitting helpers. Hard things are done
with helpers.

"A good servant knows his master's purpose and does all his work without
idleness. He speaks what is good for him, he is loyal and noble, and he knows
his master's strength. Such a servant should be cared for like oneself. But a
servant who does not respect what he is told, who answers back when he is given
orders, who is proud of his wisdom and argues, should be let go at once. They
say an envoy should have eight qualities: he should not be stubborn or weak or
slow; he should be kind and smooth, not easily bribed by others, sound in
health, and noble in speech.

"A thoughtful man should never go trustingly to another's house at the wrong
time. He should not stand hidden at a crossroads at night, nor seek a woman a
king desires. He should not contradict a man who sits in council with bad
company. He should not say, 'I don't trust you,' but give some reason. In
business dealings a man should avoid a merciful king, a loose woman and a king's
servant. He should avoid a son, a brother, a widow with a young son, a soldier
and a man who has lost his post.

"Ten qualities come to the man who bathes: strength, beauty, a clear voice and
complexion, a good touch and smell, purity, good fortune, delicacy and the best
of women. Six qualities come to the man who eats in measure: health, long life,
happiness and strength, and his children are sound, and no one mocks him as a
glutton.

"A man should not let these stay in his house. One is the man who does no work,
another the man who eats a great deal, another the man the world hates. Others
are the great deceiver, the cruel man, the man who does not know time and place,
and the man who dresses badly. However hard pressed, a man should never ask
anything of the miser, the abuser, the unlearned or the man of low birth. Nor
should he ask of the man who honours those who deserve no honour, the harsh man,
the man who has made enemies, or the ungrateful man. A man should not serve
these six lowest of men. They are the one whose deeds cause pain, the one who
says too much and the one who always lies. They are the one whose loyalty is not
firm, the one whose love has turned, and the one who thinks too much of himself.

"Wealth is bound up with helpers, and helpers with wealth. Each is bound to the
other, and neither succeeds without the other. A man should father sons, free
them of debt and arrange some living for them, marry all his daughters well, and
then live in the forest as a sage. A lord should do what is good for all beings
and brings happiness to himself. That is the root of success in dharma and
purpose. How can the man who has understanding, power, energy, courage, effort
and resolve fear for his living?

"Look at the evils of war with the Pandavas, where even the gods with Shakra
would suffer. It means enmity with your sons and a life always full of fear. It
means the ruin of your fame, and the joy of your enemies. If the anger of
Bhishma, of you who are like Indra, of Drona and of King Yudhishthira grew, it
would destroy this world, like a white comet falling slantwise across the sky.
Your hundred sons, Karna, and the five Pandavas could rule the whole earth with
the ocean as its garment. The sons of Dhritarashtra are a forest, king, and the
sons of Pandu are its tigers. Do not cut down the forest with its tigers. Do not
drive the tigers from the forest. There would be no forest without the tigers,
and no tigers without the forest. The forest is guarded by the tigers, and the
woods guard the tigers.

"Evil men do not want to know the virtues of others as much as they want to know
their faults. A man who wants the highest success should practise dharma from
the start. Purpose does not depart from dharma, as amrita does not depart from
heaven. The man whose self has turned from evil and is set on good has
understood all this, nature and its changes. The man who follows dharma, purpose
and desire, each in its time, wins the union of all three, here and in the next
world. The man who holds back the rising force of anger and delight, and is not
confused in trouble, is a vessel for good fortune, king.

"Hear from me the five kinds of strength men always have. The strength of the
arms is called the least. The second strength, bless you, is having counsellors.
Those who want to conquer say the third strength is getting wealth. The strength
a man is born with, king, from his father and grandfather, is called the
strength of birth, the fourth. But the strength by which all these are held
together, Bharata, the best of strengths, is called the strength of wisdom.

"When a man has the power to do another great harm, the other should not make an
enemy of him and then rest easy, thinking, 'I am far away.' What wise man would
trust women, kings, snakes, his own learning, those who serve his enemies,
pleasure or life? For a creature struck by the arrow of wisdom there are no
doctors, no medicines, no offerings or mantras, no lucky rites, no spells of the
Atharva Veda and no antidotes, however well made. A snake, a fire, a lion and a
son of good family should never be despised, Bharata. They are all full of
energy.

"Fire is a great energy in the world, hidden in wood. It does not use the wood
as long as others do not kindle it. But when it is rubbed out of the wood and
blazes up, its energy quickly burns that wood and the rest of the forest. In the
same way, those born in good families, with energy like fire, lie patient and
showing nothing, like fire in wood.

"You and your sons are like a creeper, and the sons of Pandu are like shala
trees. A creeper never grows without the support of a great tree. You and your
sons are a forest, son of Ambika. Know the Pandavas to be the lions in it, my
son. A forest without lions would perish, and lions would perish without the
forest."
