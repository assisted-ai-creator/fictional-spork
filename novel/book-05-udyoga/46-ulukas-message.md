# Uluka's Message

"When the armies were arrayed like that at Kurukshetra, best of brahmins," said
Janamejaya, "what did the Kurus do, driven on by time?"

"When the armies were arrayed like that and ready, bull among the Bharatas,"
said Vaishampayana, "Dhritarashtra said to Sanjaya, great king, 'Come, Sanjaya,
and tell me everything, leaving nothing out, that happened in the camps of the
Kuru and Pandava armies. I think fate is supreme, and human effort is useless.
For though I know the evils of war, which lead to destruction, I cannot restrain
my son, whose wisdom is trickery and who plays at crooked dice, nor do what is
good for myself. My mind sees the faults, suta, but when it meets Duryodhana it
turns again. Since it has come to this, what is to be will be, Sanjaya. They say
giving up the body in battle is honoured as the kshatriya's dharma.'

"'Your question is fitting, great king, as you deserve,' said Sanjaya. 'But you
should not lay this fault on Duryodhana. Listen, lord of the earth, while I tell
it all. A man who meets with evil through his own wrongdoing should not blame
fate or time for his sin, great king. Among men, the man who does everything
that is condemned deserves death from the whole world. The Pandavas and their
ministers bore insults, best of men, because they looked to you, when they were
cheated at the dice. Hear from me everything about the slaughter of horses and
elephants and kings of boundless power that happened in battle. Be firm, great
king, and when you have heard just how it was in the great war, the destruction
of the whole world, do not lose heart. A man is not the doer of good or evil
deeds. A man is not his own master. He is made to act like a wooden puppet. Some
are directed by the Lord, some by chance, and others by their earlier deeds. It
is pulled three ways.'

"When the great Pandavas had camped on the Hiranvati, great king," said Sanjaya,
"Duryodhana, with Karna, Saubala and Duhshasana, called Uluka aside and said to
him, 'Uluka, son of the gambler, go to the Pandavas and the Somakas. When you
get there, give them my message, while Vasudeva listens.

"'"Here it is, what you have thought of for many years, the war of the Pandavas
and the Kurus, which terrifies the world. The great boast that Sanjaya spoke in
the middle of the Kurus, son of Kunti: the time for it has come. Do all that you
promised. Remember your anger, the theft of the kingdom, the exile to the forest
and Draupadi's torment, son of Pandu, and be a man. The time has come for which
a kshatriya woman bears a child. Show your strength, your courage, your bravery,
your great speed with weapons, and your manhood in battle, and make good your
anger. Whose heart would not break, worn down and wretched, after living so long
thrown out of his power? Whose anger would not blaze, a brave man born of a good
family, greedy for others' wealth, when he attacks the kingdom that was cut from
him? Let the great words that were spoken be shown in deeds. Good men know a
coward by his boasting without deeds. A place under the enemy's power, or the
kingdom again: those are the two prizes of the man who fights. So show your
manhood. Beat us and rule this earth, or be killed by us and go to the world of
heroes. Remember your banishment from the kingdom, your suffering, the exile to
the forest and Krishnaa's torment, son of Pandu, and be a man. At the unwelcome
words spoken again and again as you went into exile, show your anger today, for
anger is manhood. Let your wrath, your strength and your courage, your knowledge
and your speed with weapons, be seen here in battle, son of Pritha. Be a man."

"'And say this from me, Uluka, again and again, to that fool Bhimasena, the
eunuch who eats so much and knows nothing: "What you swore in the hall,
Vrikodara, when you could do nothing, drink Duhshasana's blood if you can." And
say to Arjuna: "The smelting of iron is finished. Kurukshetra has no mud. Your
horses are fat and your fighters are fed. Fight tomorrow, with Keshava."'

"Gambler's son that he was, Uluka came to the Pandava camp. He met the Pandavas
and spoke to Yudhishthira. 'You know what an envoy's words are. I say only what
I was told to say. You should not be angry when you hear Duryodhana's message.'

"'Uluka, you have nothing to fear,' said Yudhishthira. 'Speak freely. Tell us
the mind of Dhritarashtra's greedy son, who does not look far ahead.'

"Then, in the middle of the shining, great-souled Pandavas, of all the Srinjayas
and of famous Krishna, before Drupada and his sons and Virata, and among all the
lords of the earth, he spoke," said Sanjaya. "'King Dhritarashtra's great-minded
son says this to you. Hear it, lord of men, while the Kuru heroes listen. You
were beaten at dice, and Krishnaa was brought into the hall. A man who thinks
himself a man can feel anger at that. You were banished from home into the
forest for twelve years, and lived a year in slavery to Virata. Remember your
anger, the theft of the kingdom, the exile in the forest and Draupadi's torment,
son of Pandu, and be a man. And what Bhimasena swore, son of Pandu, when he
could do nothing: let Duhshasana's blood be drunk, if it can be. The smelting of
iron is finished. Kurukshetra has no mud. The road is level, and the fighters
are fed. Fight tomorrow, with Keshava.

"'"Why do you boast before you have met Bhishma in battle? You are like a fool
who wants to climb Mount Gandhamadana. Son of Pritha, how can you want the
kingdom without beating in battle Drona, the best of fighters, equal in war to
Shachi's husband? You want to beat Drona in battle in your folly, but it is
false, son of Pritha. He is the teacher of the brahma weapon and of the bow, who
has mastered both Vedas, the burden-bearer in war, unshakeable, the unfailing
holder of the line. We have never heard of the wind uprooting Mount Meru. If
what you say to me were so, the wind might carry Meru, the sky might fall to the
earth, or the age might turn. What elephant, horse or man who wants to live
could meet the weapon of these two, the crusher of enemies, and go home safe?
How could anyone they aim at, or strike with a dreadful blow, get free alive
from the battle, with his feet on the ground? Are you like a frog lying in a
well, that you do not understand this gathered army of kings, hard to attack,
like the army of the gods, guarded by kings as heaven is by the thirty gods? It
has men of the east, the west and the south, of the north, the Kambojas, the
Shakas and the Khashas, the Shalvas and the Matsyas, the Kurus and the people of
the middle country, the mlecchas, the Pulindas, the Dravidas, the Andhras and
the Kanchyas. It is a flood of many peoples, swollen for battle, like the
Ganga's current that nothing can hold back. And I stand in the middle of the
elephant host. Do you want to fight me, fool, with your little mind?"'

"When he had said this to King Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, Uluka turned
again to Jishnu and spoke. 'Fight without boasting. Why do you boast so much,
Arjuna? Success comes from action in its turn. It does not come from boasting.
If deeds in the world succeeded by boasting, Dhananjaya, everyone would get what
he wanted, and the wretched would boast a great deal. I know you have Vasudeva
as your ally. I know your Gandiva is the length of a palm tree. I know there is
no fighter like you. And knowing this, I am taking your kingdom. A man does not
win great success by the dharma of turns. The Maker brings beings under his
power by his mind alone. For thirteen years I have enjoyed the kingdom while you
wept, and I shall rule it again when I have killed you and your kin.

"'Where was your Gandiva then, when you were won as a slave at the stake? Where
was Bhimasena's strength then, Phalguna? You had no way out then, from Bhimasena
with his mace or from Partha with his Gandiva, except blameless Krishnaa. The
proud woman freed you from slavery, when you had come down to something less
than human and were doing a slave's work. What I said of you, that you were
sesame seeds without the seed, was true. For Partha wore a braid at Virata's
city then. And Bhimasena was worn out doing the cook's work in Virata's kitchen,
son of Kunti. That was my manhood too. So kshatriyas have always punished a
kshatriya who runs away in battle, with the braid, the girdle and the row of
women.

"'I shall not give back the kingdom for fear of Vasudeva, nor of you, Phalguna.
Fight, with Keshava. No magic, no conjuring and no frightening trick can bear up
against me when I have my weapon in my hand in battle. A thousand Vasudevas or
hundreds of Phalgunas will run to the ten quarters when they meet me, whose
arrows never fail. Go into battle with Bhishma, split a mountain with your head,
or cross with your arms this great, deep ocean of men. Its great fish is the son
of Sharadvat, and it is full of the fish Vivimshati. Brihadbala is its tide, and
the son of Somadatta its whale. Duhshasana is its flood, and Shala and Shalya
its fish. Sushena and Chitrayudha are its snakes and crocodiles. Jayadratha is
its rock and Purumitra its depth. Durmarshana is its water and Shakuni its
falls. It is an endless flood of weapons, swelling beyond measure. When you
plunge into it and your mind is lost with weariness, and all your kin are
killed, then your mind will grieve. Then let your mind turn away from ruling the
earth, son of Pritha, as heaven turns from the unclean. For ruling the kingdom
is as hard for you to win as heaven for a man with no austerity.'"

"Uluka spoke again to Arjuna the words he had been told," said Sanjaya,
"stinging him with the spikes of his words, as one stings an angry poisonous
snake. When they heard it, the Pandavas were deeply angered. They had been very
angry already, and now the gambler's son had insulted them. They could not stay
on their seats. They flung out their arms, and like angry poisonous snakes they
looked at each other. Bhimasena, with his head bowed, looked at Keshava with
eyes red at the corners, hissing like a poisonous snake.

"When the Dasharha saw the son of the Wind stricken and deeply hurt by anger, he
all but smiled and answered the gambler's son. 'Go quickly, son of the gambler,
and tell Suyodhana this: "Your words have been heard and their meaning taken.
Let it be as you think." And tell Suyodhana again what I say: "Now you will be
seen tomorrow. Be a man, fool. You think, fool, that Janardana will not fight,
since he has been chosen by the sons of Pritha as a charioteer, and so you are
not afraid. At the very last, if it came to it, I could burn up all the kings in
my anger, as fire burns up grass. But at Yudhishthira's bidding I shall be the
charioteer of great Phalguna, who knows himself, as he fights. If you fly up
over the three worlds, or go down into the earth, you will see Arjuna's chariot
in front of you there at dawn. And you think that Bhimasena's roaring is empty.
Take it that Duhshasana's blood has been drunk. Neither Partha nor King
Yudhishthira, nor Bhimasena nor the twins, will even look at you when you speak
against them.'""

**Divergences.**

* **The cat who kept a vow.** A long appendix passage (App. I No. 9, at 5.157.5) gives Duryodhana's message a fable: a cat who pretends to practise austerity on the bank of the Ganga, wins the trust of the mice and eats them, until an old mouse sees through him. Duryodhana calls Yudhishthira such a cat. The fable is not in the CE constituted text, and is not narrated. See ledger L-183.
* **Messages to each of the Pandavas.** Appendix passages (App. I Nos. 10 and 11) add taunts to Bhima, Nakula, Sahadeva, Virata, Drupada, Dhrishtadyumna and Shikhandi, and repeat the taunts to Arjuna. Not narrated. So is a rejected line on Bhima as Ballava the cook (\*559).
* **The Pandavas' replies.** An appendix passage (App. I No. 12, at 5.159.6) gives long angry replies by Bhima, Sahadeva and Arjuna. In the CE the Pandavas are silent and angry, and Krishna answers (5.159.2–13). Not narrated.
* **Duryodhana's briefing and Uluka's delivery.** The CE gives the message twice: Duryodhana's instructions (5.157) and Uluka's speech in the Pandava camp (5.158). The second is fuller, and adds the taunts to Arjuna. Both are kept.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*555–\*558, \*560–\*562.

**Choices.**

* 5.156.15 *traidham etad vikṛṣyate*: "It is pulled three ways."
* 5.157.3 *kaitavya*: "son of the gambler", Uluka, Shakuni's son.
* 5.157.16 *tūbarakam*: "eunuch", an insult; *bahvāśinam*, "who eats so much".
* 5.157.18 *lohābhihāro nirvṛttaḥ*: "The smelting of iron is finished", read as the forging of weapons, or the lustration of arms before battle. The sense is uncertain.
* 5.158.25 *gāṇḍivaṃ tālamātram*: "the length of a palm tree".
* 5.158.26 *paryāyadharmeṇa*: "by the dharma of turns", taken as waiting for one's turn by right.
* 5.158.31 *ṣaṇḍhatilān*: "sesame seeds without the seed", an insult from the dicing. Duryodhana claims it as his own; at the time it was Duhshasana who said it (2.68.8, 13–14). *veṇī*: "a braid", Arjuna as Brihannada.
* 5.158.33 *śreṇyāṃ kakṣyāṃ ca veṇyāṃ ca*: "with the braid, the girdle and the row of women", read as punishments of dress for a coward. The sense is uncertain.
* 5.158.38–39: the ocean of men is built from the Kaurava warriors' names. Sharadvat's son is Kripa.
* 5.158.41 *svarga ivātapasvinā*: "as heaven for a man with no austerity".
* 5.159.8 *sārathyena vṛtaḥ*: "chosen … as a charioteer".
* 5.159.9 *jaghanyakālam*: "At the very last".
