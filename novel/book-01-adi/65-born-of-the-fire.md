# Born of the Fire

"When those tigers among men had killed the rakshasa Baka," asked Janamejaya,
"what did the Pandavas do next, brahmin?"

They went on living there, king, after they had killed the rakshasa Baka,
studying the highest Veda in the brahmin's house. Then, a few days later, a
brahmin of strict vows came to the brahmin's house looking for lodging. The
learned and excellent brahmin, who had vowed always to welcome every guest,
honoured him properly and gave him a place to stay. Then all the Pandavas, the
bulls among men, with Kunti, sat with the brahmin as he told his stories. He
told of countries and of holy places of many kinds, of the doings of kings and
of many cities. When his stories were done, Janamejaya, the brahmin told them
there of the wonderful bridegroom choice of Yajnasena's daughter among the
Panchalas: of the birth of Dhrishtadyumna and the birth of Shikhandi, and of how
Krishna was born, not from a womb, at Drupada's great sacrifice.

When the bulls among men heard of that great man's deeds, the most wonderful in
the world, they asked him to tell the whole story. "How was Dhrishtadyumna,
Drupada's son, born from the fire? How did Krishna's wonderful birth from the
middle of the altar come about? How did he learn all the weapons from Drona, the
great archer? And how did those dear friends fall out, and through whose doing?"

When the bulls among men urged him like this, king, the brahmin told them the
whole story of Draupadi's birth.

---

"There was a great seer at Gangadvara," said the brahmin, "Bharadvaja, very
wise, always strict in his vows. He went to bathe in the Ganga, and there he saw
the apsaras Ghritachi, who had come before him and was bathing. By the river
bank the wind blew her garment away. When the seer saw her with her garment
pulled away, he desired her. He had been celibate since boyhood, and as his
heart went out to her, his seed fell, and the seer put it in a pot, a *drona*.
From it the wise man's son Drona was born, and he studied the Vedas and all
their limbs.

"Bharadvaja had a friend, a king named Prishata, and at that time a son named
Drupada was born to him. Every day Prishata's son, that bull of the kshatriyas,
went to the hermitage, and played and studied with Drona. When Prishata died,
Drupada became king. Drona heard that Rama was giving away all his wealth. When
Rama had set out for the forest, the son of Bharadvaja said to him, 'Know that I
am Drona, best of brahmins, and I have come for wealth.'

"'I have only my body left,' said Rama. 'Choose one of the two, brahmin, my
weapons or my body.'

"'You should give me all your weapons,' said Drona, 'and the art of calling them
back, and of using them all.'

"'So be it,' said the joy of the Bhrigus, and gave them to him. When he had
received them, Drona had what he wanted. He was overjoyed, and when he had won
from Rama the Brahma weapon, most highly prized, he was above all men.

"Then the mighty son of Bharadvaja went to Drupada, and the tiger among men
said, 'Know that I am your friend.'

"'A man who does not know the Veda is no friend of one who does,' said Drupada,
'nor a man without a chariot of a chariot fighter. A man who is not a king is no
friend of a king. Why do you want a friendship that is past?'

"The wise man made up his mind about the Panchala, and went to the city of the
Kuru chiefs, named after the elephant. When he came, Bhishma took his grandsons
and wealth of many kinds and gave them to wise Drona as his pupils. Then Drona,
the learned, called all his pupils together and said to them, meaning trouble
for Drupada, 'There is a fee for your teacher turning over in my heart. When you
have mastered your weapons you must give it. Tell me truly, sinless ones.'

"And when all the Pandavas had mastered their weapons, and worked hard at them,
Drona spoke again about his fee. 'Prishata's son Drupada is king in Chhatravati.
Take his kingdom from him and give it to me quickly.' Then the five sons of
Pandu beat Drupada in battle, bound him with his ministers, and showed him to
Drona.

"'I ask again for your friendship, lord of men,' said Drona. 'A man who is not a
king, it seems, cannot be a king's friend. So I have made an effort for your
kingdom, Yajnasena. You are king on the south bank of the Bhagirathi, and I on
the north.'

"That great insult never left the king's heart for a moment. He was sick at
heart, and he grew thin.

---

"King Drupada could not bear it. He wandered from one brahmin settlement to
another, looking for brahmins of great power who could bring about what he
wanted. Longing for the birth of a son, his mind struck down with grief, he
thought all the time, *I have no child good enough.* In his despair he said of
the sons he had, 'Shame on such kin!' He sighed constantly, wanting to pay Drona
back. He thought of Drona's power, his discipline, his training and his deeds.
The best of kings tried, but he could find no way to repay him with a
kshatriya's strength, Bharata.

"Wandering about near Kalmashi on the bank of the Ganga, the king came to a holy
settlement of brahmins. There was no brahmin there who had not finished his
studies, none without a vow, and none who was not a great man. There Prishata's
son saw two brahmin seers of strict vows, Yaja and Upayaja, living in calm,
devoted to the study of the Veda, both of the Kashyapa line. Those two best of
seers were fit to carry him across. He invited them tirelessly, offering them
all they could desire.

"He weighed their strength and their wisdom, and went in private to the younger,
Upayaja, of firm vows, tempting him with gifts. He served at his feet, spoke
kindly and gave him all he could want. When he had honoured Upayaja properly, he
said to him, 'By what rite, brahmin, could I have a son who will be Drona's
death? If you do it, Upayaja, I will give you a hundred million cows, or
anything else your heart desires, best of brahmins. I will give it all, without
doubt.'

"'No,' said the seer. But Drupada went on serving him, trying to win him over.
Then at the end of a year Upayaja, best of brahmins, spoke to Drupada in a sweet
voice, king, when the time had come.

"'My elder brother, wandering by a forest stream, picked up a fruit that had
fallen on ground whose purity he did not know. I was following my brother, and I
saw that it was not fitting. He would not stop to think about taking what was
unclean. He saw the fruit, and did not see the faults that go with it. A man who
does not tell clean from unclean in one thing, how will he in others? When he
was studying the Veda and living in his teacher's house, he always ate the alms
that others had left, and praised the taste of the food again and again, without
disgust. I reason from this that my brother wants gain. Go to him, king. He will
perform the sacrifice for you.'

"When he heard Upayaja's words, the king, who knew every dharma, felt disgust
and thought it over in his mind. But he honoured the seer Yaja, who deserved
honour, and said to him, 'I will give you eighty thousand cows. Perform the
sacrifice for me, lord. I burn with my enmity against Drona, and you should
bring me relief. He is the best of those who know Brahman, and unmatched in the
Brahma weapon. That is why Drona defeated me in our quarrel as friends. There is
no kshatriya on earth to equal Bharadvaja's wise son, the chief teacher of the
Kauravas. Drona's nets of arrows take the lives of living things. His bow, six
cubits long, looks huge and without equal. The great-spirited archer,
Bharadvaja's son, beats back a kshatriya's force with a brahmin's force, without
doubt. He stands there like Jamadagni's son, made to cut down the kshatriyas.
The terrible power of his weapons cannot be withstood by men on earth. Speaking
out his brahmin power, like a fire fed with offerings, he meets the kshatriyas
in battle and burns them up, with Brahman going before him. Of brahmin and
kshatriya, brahmin power is the greater. I am weaker in kshatriya strength, and
I have turned to brahmin power, finding you, better than Drona, the best of
those who know Brahman. Let me have a son who will be Drona's end, unconquerable
in battle. Perform that rite for me, Yaja, and I will give you a hundred million
cows.'

"'So be it,' said Yaja, and he began to prepare the sacrifice. And though
Upayaja wanted none of it, he urged him on, saying, 'It is for your elder.' Yaja
promised to bring about Drona's destruction. Then great Upayaja told the king
the rite of the sacred fires for the fruit of a son. 'The son will be of great
courage, great splendour and great strength, just as you want him, king.'

"Fixing his mind on a son who would kill Bharadvaja's son, the king gathered
everything for the rite to succeed. At the end of the offering, Yaja called the
queen. 'Come to me, Queen Prishati. A pair of children has come to you.'

"'My mouth is not yet cleansed, brahmin,' said the queen, 'and I am wearing holy
scents. I am held back, though it is for a son. Wait, Yaja, for my sake.'

"'Yaja has cooked the offering and Upayaja has spoken the mantras over it,' said
Yaja. 'How could it fail to fulfil the wish? Come, or stay.'

"When Yaja had said this, and the prepared offering had been poured in, a boy
like a god rose out of the fire. He was the colour of flame, terrible to look
at, with a crown on his head and the finest armour, with a sword, arrows and a
bow, roaring again and again. He climbed into a fine chariot and drove off in
it. Then the Panchalas cried out in joy, 'Well done! Well done!' And a great
unseen being moving in the sky said, 'This prince, who takes away the Panchalas'
fear and brings them fame, who takes away the king's grief, is born to kill
Drona.'

"And a girl too, the Panchali, rose from the middle of the altar, fortunate,
lovely in every limb, slim-waisted, charming. She was dark, with eyes like lotus
petals and dark curling hair, a goddess who had taken a human body. The scent of
blue lotuses came from her a mile away. She had the greatest beauty, and there
was none like her on earth. When the girl of the lovely hips was born, a voice
without a body said, 'This dark girl, the best of all women, will lead the
kshatriyas to destruction. In time this slender-waisted one will do the work of
the gods. Because of her a great terror will come upon the kshatriyas.'

"When they heard this, all the Panchalas roared like a pride of lions, and the
earth could not bear them in their joy. When she saw the two, Prishati went to
Yaja, wanting children. 'Let these two know no other mother but me,' she said.
'So be it,' said Yaja, to please the king. And the brahmins, their hearts full,
gave the two their names. 'Because he is bold, *dhrishta*, and very daring, and
because he was born from dharma and with splendour, *dyumna*, let Drupada's son
be Dhrishtadyumna.' And they called the dark girl Krishna, for she was dark in
colour. So those twins were born at Drupada's great sacrifice.

"Then mighty Bharadvaja brought Dhrishtadyumna the Panchala to his own house and
taught him weapons. The very wise Drona thought that fate cannot be escaped, and
that what will be must be, and so, to guard his own fame, he did it."

---

When they heard this, the sons of Kunti felt as if they had been pierced by a
dart, and the great warriors were all uneasy at heart. When Kunti saw her sons
bewildered and lost, she spoke to Yudhishthira, she who always spoke the truth.

"We have lived a long time here in the brahmin's house, Yudhishthira, enjoying
this lovely town and getting alms. All the lovely forests and groves here we
have seen, again and again, tamer of enemies. Seen again, they do not please us
as they did. And alms are not so easy to get now, hero, joy of the Kurus. Let us
go to the Panchalas, if you think fit. It will be pleasant to see something new,
son. The Panchalas are said to have plenty of food, tamer of enemies, and we
have heard that King Yajnasena is good to brahmins. I do not think it is right
to live in one place too long. Let us go there, if you think fit, son."

"What you think best is best for us," said Yudhishthira. "But I do not know
whether my younger brothers want to go or not."

Then Kunti spoke to Bhimasena, Arjuna and the twins about going, and they said,
"So be it." Then Kunti and her sons took leave of the brahmin, king, and set out
for the lovely city of great Drupada.

**Divergences.**

* **No wish for Arjuna.** In the popular story Drupada holds the sacrifice to get a son
  to kill Drona *and* a daughter to marry Arjuna. The CE's Drupada asks only for a son
  "who will be Drona's death" (1.155.11, 29). The daughter comes unasked. The lines
  about Arjuna are in the vulgate (\*1689, \*1691, \*1699, \*1700). See ledger L-92.

**The CE's own doublets.**

* The brahmin's retelling of Drona's story differs in places from the narrator's
  (1.121–122, 1.128). Here Rama offers Drona "my weapons or my body" (1.154.10), Drona
  gets the Brahma weapon (1.154.13), Drupada's capital is Chhatravati (1.154.21), and
  "the five sons of Pandu" defeat Drupada (1.154.22). Each version is kept where it
  stands.
* Shikhandi's birth is announced among the brahmin's stories (1.153.8) but not told
  here. It is told in Book 5.

**Choices.**

* 1.155.3: Drupada's "shame on such kin" is said of the sons he already has.
* 1.155.15–19: Upayaja's story of the fallen fruit is his reason for thinking his
  brother would take the fee.
* 1.155.24 *ṣaḍaratni*: a bow "six cubits" long.
* 1.155.34–36: the exchange between Yaja and the queen is given as it stands.
* 1.155.41 *vedimadhyāt*: "from the middle of the altar".
* 1.155.49: the etymology of Dhrishtadyumna is the brahmins' own. The CE reads
  *dharmād*, "from dharma"; a reading *varmāt*, "with armour", would suit his birth
  in armour better, but the novel follows the CE.
* 1.155.52: Drona teaches his future killer "thinking fate cannot be escaped" and "to
  guard his fame".
