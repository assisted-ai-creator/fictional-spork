# Tapati

"Tell me the reason, gandharva," said Arjuna, "why you attacked us, tamer of
enemies, when we are good men who know the Veda, travelling by night."

"You have no fire with you," said the gandharva, "no offerings, and no brahmin
going before you. That is why I attacked you, joy of the Pandavas. Yakshas,
rakshasas, gandharvas, pishachas, snakes and men all tell at length of the
glorious line of the Kurus. I have heard divine seers, Narada and the others,
telling the virtues of your wise forefathers, hero. I have seen for myself the
greatness of your family as I roamed this whole earth, clothed by the ocean. I
know your teacher in the Veda and in archery, Arjuna, famous Bharadvaja, known
in the three worlds. I know Dharma, the Wind, Shakra, the two Ashvins and Pandu,
tiger of the Kurus: these six who made your family grow, your fathers, Partha,
the best of gods and men. All of you brothers are heroes of divine spirit,
great-hearted, the best of all who bear weapons, and your vows are well kept.

"Even knowing that your minds and understanding are the highest, Partha, I
attacked you here. When a man is near women, Kauravya, and sees himself
attacked, he should not bear it, relying on the strength of his arms. And our
strength grows greater at night. That is why anger came over me, Kaunteya, with
my wife beside me.

"So I have been beaten by you in battle, Tapatya, you who make the Tapati line
grow. Hear from me how it came about. Celibacy is the highest dharma, and it is
fixed in you. That is why I was beaten by you in this fight, Partha. But any
kshatriya who lives by desire and fights at night would never come out alive.
Yet even a king who lives by desire, Tapatya, may beat all the night-roamers in
battle, if a family priest goes before him. So, Tapatya, whatever good men want
in this world, they should set self-controlled family priests to that work. The
family priests of kings should be devoted to the Veda and its six limbs, pure,
truthful, righteous and self-disciplined. A king who has a priest who knows
dharma, speaks well, and is good and pure, is sure of victory, and heaven will
follow. To win what he has not won and guard what he has, a king should appoint
a priest with good qualities. A king who wants to win the whole earth, crowned
with Meru and clothed by the ocean, should keep to his priest's counsel. For no
king who has no brahmin will ever win the earth by courage alone, Tapatya, or by
birth. So know this, you who make the Kuru line grow: a kingdom with brahmins at
its head can be kept for a long time."

---

"You called me Tapatya," said Arjuna. "I want to know what that word means. Who
is this Tapati, because of whom we are Tapatyas? For we are sons of Kunti, good
friend. I want to know the truth."

When Arjuna said this, the gandharva told Kunti's son Dhananjaya the story
famous in the three worlds.

"I will tell you this whole lovely story, Partha, just as it happened, a story
of dharma, best of those who uphold dharma. Listen closely, and I will tell you
why I called you Tapatya.

"The one who fills the heavens with his splendour from his place in the sky, the
Sun, had a daughter named Tapati, who had no equal. She was the daughter of
Vivasvat, Kaunteya, the younger sister of Savitri, famous in the three worlds,
Tapati, full of heat and austerity. No goddess, asura woman, yaksha woman,
rakshasa woman, apsaras or gandharva woman was as beautiful. Her limbs were well
formed and flawless, her eyes dark and long, her ways good, her life virtuous
and her dress fine. There was no one like her in the three worlds, Bharata.
Savitri, the Sun, looked for a husband for her in beauty, character, family and
learning. When he saw his daughter grown up and ready to be given in marriage,
he found no peace, thinking of whom to give her to.

"Then, Kaunteya, Riksha's son, the mighty bull of the Kurus, King Samvarana,
worshipped the Sun all the time. The king was always disciplined, with water
offerings, garlands and gifts, with rules and fasts and austerities of many
kinds. Devoted, pure, eager to serve and without pride, the joy of the Pauravas
worshipped the Sun as it rose. So the Sun thought Samvarana, who was grateful,
knew dharma and had no equal on earth in beauty, a fit husband for Tapati. He
wanted to give the girl to Samvarana, the best of kings, whose family was
famous. As the Sun lights up the sky with his splendour, so King Samvarana shone
on earth. As those who speak of Brahman worship the rising sun, so the people
below the brahmins worshipped Samvarana, Partha. The glorious king outshone the
moon in loveliness and the sun in splendour, for his friends and his enemies
alike. The Heater himself set his mind on giving Tapati to a king of such
virtues and such conduct.

"Once, so they say, the glorious king, whose fame was wide on earth, went
hunting in the mountain woods, Partha. As he hunted, worn out with hunger,
thirst and weariness, the king's matchless horse died on the mountain, Kaunteya.
With his horse dead, the king went on foot on the mountain, and he saw a girl
with long eyes who had no equal in the world. Alone, the tamer of enemies came
upon the lone girl, and the tiger among kings stood looking at her, his eyes
fixed. From her beauty the king thought she was Shri. Then he thought she was
the light of the sun, fallen. On the mountain slope where the dark-eyed girl
stood, the place seemed golden, with its trees, shrubs and creepers. When he saw
her, the king thought little of the beauty of every other creature, and he
thought his eyes had found their reward. Of all that the king had seen since his
birth, he could think of nothing as beautiful as she. His heart and eyes were
bound to her by the cords of her virtues, and he did not move from the place,
and was aware of nothing. *Surely the Maker churned the world of gods, asuras
and men and brought out this beauty for this wide-eyed girl*, he thought. So
King Samvarana judged the girl, who had no equal in the world for the wealth of
her beauty.

"As soon as he saw the lovely girl, the king of noble family fell to brooding,
pierced by the arrows of love. Burning with the fierce fire of love, bold as he
was, he spoke to the shy and famous girl. 'Who are you, and whose are you, you
whose thighs are like plantain stems? Why are you standing here? How do you come
to be wandering alone in this empty forest, you of the bright smile? Every limb
of yours is flawless, and you are decked with every ornament. You seem to be the
ornament that ornaments long for. I do not think you a goddess, an asura woman,
a yaksha woman, a rakshasa woman, a snake woman, a gandharva woman or a human
woman. Of all the fine women I have seen or heard of, I think none your equal,
proud beauty.'

"So the king spoke to her. But she said nothing at all to him, sick with love in
the empty forest. As the king went on pleading, the long-eyed girl vanished
there and then, like lightning in the clouds. The king wandered all round the
forest looking for the girl with eyes like lotus petals, roaming like a madman.
When he could not see her, the best of the Kauravas lamented at length, and then
stood a while without moving.

---

"When she had vanished, the king, the feller of hosts of enemies, bewildered by
love, fell to the ground. When he had fallen, the girl with the lovely smile and
the full, wide hips showed herself to the king again. And the lovely girl spoke
in a sweet voice to the king, the founder of the Kuru line, whose heart was
struck by love. 'Get up, get up, bless you. You should not fall into a faint and
lie on the ground, tamer of enemies, tiger among kings.'

"When she spoke to him in that sweet voice, the king saw the girl of the wide
hips standing before him. Then the lord of men, his whole being on fire with
love, spoke to the dark-eyed girl in a faltering voice. 'Dark-eyed one, proud
beauty, love me, for I love you and am sick with love. My life is leaving me.
For your sake, wide-eyed one, this god of love keeps piercing me with sharp
arrows and will not stop, you who are like the heart of a lotus. I am swallowed
by the great snake of love, good lady, with no one to help me. Take me, you of
the wide, full hips, lovely-faced one. My life depends on you, you whose voice
is like the song of the kinnaras, lovely in every flawless limb, with a face
like the lotus and the moon. Without you, timid one, I cannot live. So have pity
on me, wide-eyed girl. I am devoted to you, dark-eyed one. You should not
abandon me. Save me with your love, lady. Come to me, timid, lovely one, by the
gandharva rite of marriage. Of all marriages, you with thighs like plantain
stems, the gandharva rite is said to be the best.'

"'I am not my own mistress, king,' said Tapati. 'I am a girl with a father. If
you love me, ask my father. As I have taken your life, lord of men, so you have
taken mine even more, by the mere sight of you. I am not mistress of my body,
best of kings, so I will not come near you. Women are not free. What girl in all
the worlds would not want for her lord a king of famous family, a husband who
loves those devoted to him? So, as things are, ask my father, the Sun, with
bowing, with austerity and with discipline. If he wishes to give me to you,
tamer of enemies, I will always be yours to command, king. I am named Tapati,
the younger sister of Savitri, the daughter of this lamp of the world, Savitri,
the Sun, bull of the kshatriyas.'

---

"When she had said this, the blameless girl quickly rose into the sky, and the
king fell to the ground again, right there. His minister, with his followers,
found him in the great forest, fallen on the ground, like Indra's banner raised
high and then fallen. When his counsellor saw the great archer fallen on the
ground without his horse, he was as if set on fire. He hurried to him, in alarm
and in love, and lifted up the king, bewildered by love, from the ground, as a
father lifts a fallen son. Old in wisdom and years, in fame and self-control,
the minister lifted him up, and his own fear left him. He spoke to the king, now
risen, in a kind, sweet voice. 'Do not be afraid, tiger among men. Bless you,
sinless one.' He thought the king, the feller of enemies in battle, had fallen
to the ground worn out by hunger and thirst. He sprinkled the king's head with
cool water scented with lotuses, without touching his crown.

"When he came to himself, the mighty king sent away the whole army, except that
one counsellor. At the king's command the great army left, and the king sat down
again on that mountain slope. There, on that fine mountain, purified, with
joined hands, he stood on the ground with his arms raised, wishing to win over
the Sun. And King Samvarana, killer of enemies, thought in his mind of
Vasishtha, the best of seers, his family priest.

"The lord of men stayed in one place day and night, and on the twelfth day the
brahmin seer came. The great seer, whose soul was perfected, knew at once by
divine means that the king's heart had been stolen by Tapati. The righteous man
spoke to the best of kings, who was so disciplined, wishing to do what he
wanted. Then, while the lord of men watched, the blessed seer, bright as the
sun, went up to see the Sun. The brahmin came before the thousand-rayed one with
joined hands and announced himself gladly: 'I am Vasishtha.'

"The splendid Vivasvat said to the best of sages, 'Welcome, great seer. Tell me
what you wish.'

"'Your daughter named Tapati, the younger sister of Savitri,' said Vasishtha, 'I
ask you for her for Samvarana, Vibhavasu. That king is of great fame, knows
dharma and wealth, and is generous. Samvarana is a fitting husband for your
daughter, sky-goer.'

"When he said this, Savitri the Day-maker, who had already decided, *I will give
her*, welcomed the brahmin and answered, 'Samvarana is the best of kings, and
you are the best of seers, sage, and Tapati is the best of women. What is there
but to give her?'

"Then the Heater himself gave Tapati, flawless in every limb, to great Vasishtha
for Samvarana, and the great seer received the girl Tapati. Then Vasishtha,
given leave, came back to where the famous bull of the Kurus was. The king,
possessed by love, his whole heart fixed on her, saw the divine girl Tapati,
with her lovely smile, coming with Vasishtha, and he shone with joy. When the
king's hard twelve nights were over, the blessed seer Vasishtha, pure of soul,
came. So Samvarana won his wife by winning over by austerity the god who gives
boons, the lord of light, and by Vasishtha's power alone.

"Then on that finest of mountains, the haunt of gods and gandharvas, the bull
among men took Tapati's hand in marriage as the rites require. With Vasishtha's
leave the royal seer wanted to enjoy himself there on that mountain with his
wife. So the king put that same minister in charge of his city and kingdom, his
carriages and his armies. When Vasishtha had taken leave of the king he went
away, and the king enjoyed himself on the mountain like an immortal.

"For twelve years the king delighted there on that mountain, in forests and
waters, with that wife alone. And for twelve years the thousand-eyed god did not
rain at all in that king's city or anywhere in his kingdom. The city, with its
people starving, joyless and like corpses, was like the city of the king of the
dead, crowded with ghosts. When the righteous blessed seer Vasishtha saw it like
that, he went to the best of kings, and brought that tiger among kings back to
the city with Tapati, after he had lived there twelve years. Then, when the
tiger among kings had entered the city again, the killer of the gods' enemies
rained there as before. And the city and the kingdom rejoiced with the greatest
joy, cared for by that chief of kings, whose soul was perfected. Then the lord
of men offered sacrifices again for twelve years with his wife Tapati, like
Shakra, lord of the Maruts.

"This was the fortunate Tapati, your ancestress, Partha, the daughter of
Vivasvat, through whom I think of you as Tapatya. On her, on Tapati, King
Samvarana fathered Kuru. So you, best of the heaters, are Tapatya, Arjuna."
