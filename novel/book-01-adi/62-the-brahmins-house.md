# The Brahmin's House

"When the sons of Kunti, those great warriors, had come to Ekachakra," asked
Janamejaya, "what did the Pandavas do next, best of brahmins?"

When the sons of Kunti, the great warriors, had come to Ekachakra, they lived
for a while in a brahmin's house. They went about seeing lovely forests of many
kinds, and royal lands, rivers and lakes. They all went out begging for alms,
lord of the people, and the townspeople grew fond of them for their good
qualities. Every night they brought their alms to Kunti, and they each ate the
portion she divided out for them. The heroes ate half with their mother, the
tamers of enemies, and mighty Bhima ate the other half of all the alms.

So the great men lived there, king, and a long time went by, bull of the
Bharatas. Then one day, when the bulls of the Bharatas had gone begging,
Bhimasena happened to stay there with Pritha. Then Kunti heard a great, terrible
cry of pain rise up in the brahmin's house, Bharata. As she heard them all
weeping and lamenting, the queen could not bear it, king, for she was kind and
good. With her heart churned up by grief, gentle Pritha spoke to Bhima full of
pity.

"We live very happily in this brahmin's house, son, unknown to Dhritarashtra's
sons, honoured and free from care. I am always thinking, son, what I could do
for this brahmin that would please him, as people do when they have lived
happily in someone's house. A man is worth as much as the good done to him is
not lost on him. However much another does for him, he should do more in return.
Surely some grief has come upon this brahmin. If we could help him in it, it
would be a good deed."

"Find out what his grief is," said Bhima, "and where it comes from. When I know,
I will set about it, however hard it may be."

As they were talking, lord of the people, they heard again the cry of pain from
the brahmin and his wife. Then Kunti hurried into the inner rooms of that good
brahmin, like a cow whose calf is tied up. There she saw the brahmin with his
wife, his son and his daughter, his face twisted with grief.

---

"Shame on this life in the world," said the brahmin. "It has no pith and no
point. Its root is sorrow. It belongs to others, and it is full of what we hate.
Life is the greatest sorrow. Life is the greatest fever. For anyone living, the
pairs of opposites are sure to come. A single man cannot pursue dharma, wealth
and desire together, and to be parted from them is thought the greatest sorrow.
Some say that freedom is the highest thing, but it is not to be had at all. When
wealth is won, the whole of hell comes with it. Longing for wealth is great
sorrow. Having it is greater. And when a man has grown fond of his wealth,
losing it is greater still.

"I see no way to escape this danger, or to flee with my son and wife to safety.
I tried before, as you know, brahmin wife, to go where it was safe. But you
would not listen to me. 'I was born here and grew up here, and my father lived
here,' you said, you foolish woman, when I begged you again and again. Your old
father went to heaven long ago, and your mother too, and the kin you had are
gone. What joy was there in living here? You wanted your kin, and would not
listen to me, and now the loss of our kin has come, which grieves me terribly.
Or rather, it is my own death, for I could never give up any of my kin and live
on myself, like a cruel man.

"You are my partner in dharma, self-controlled, always like a mother to me. The
gods made you my friend and my highest refuge. My mother and father chose you,
and you share my householder's life. I chose you rightly and married you with
mantras. You are of good family and good character, the mother of my children, a
good woman who has never harmed me, always devoted to me. I could never give you
up, my wife, to save my life.

"How then could I give up my daughter myself, a child not yet grown, whose body
has not yet shown the signs of womanhood? The great Ordainer placed her in my
keeping for her husband. Through her I hope for the worlds that come through a
daughter's sons, with my ancestors. I fathered her myself. How could I give up
this child? Some men think a father loves his son more, but not his daughter. To
me they are equal. In her the worlds, our line and our happiness rest for ever.
How could I give up this innocent child?

"If I gave up my own life and went into the power of the dead, I would still
grieve. For if I leave these, they will surely not be able to live here. To give
up any one of them would be cruel, and the wise condemn it. And if I give up
myself, they will die without me. I have fallen into great trouble and cannot
cross this danger. Alas! Where shall I turn today with my family? It would be
better to die with them all. I cannot bear to live."

---

"You must not grieve at all," said the brahmin's wife, "like a common man. This
is no time for grief, for a learned man like you. All men must die in this
world. Where something must be, there is no place for grief. A wife, a son, a
daughter: all are wanted for one's own sake. Put away your distress with your
good sense. I will go there myself.

"This is a woman's highest duty in the world, the eternal one: to give up even
her life and do what is good for her husband. What I do there will bring you
happiness here, and it will be undying for me in the other world, and bring me
fame in this one. And this is the weighty dharma I will tell you. There is more
gain for you in it, and more dharma. The purpose for which a man wants a wife
you have gained through me: a daughter and a son. You have freed me of my debt.
You can feed and protect the children. I could not feed and protect them as you
can.

"Without you, every hope I have would turn to disaster. How would our two little
children live, and how would I? How could I, a widow with no protector and small
children, keep the two of them alive without you, and stay on the path the good
walk? How could I guard this daughter of yours, when proud, arrogant men who are
not fit to be your kin come asking for her? As birds go after a piece of meat
thrown on the ground, so all men go after a woman who has lost her husband.
Harassed and pestered by wicked men, I could not keep to the path that good
people love, best of brahmins.

"How could I set this one girl of your family, still unmarried, on the path of
her fathers and grandfathers? How could I give this boy the qualities we want,
with no protector and everything lost, as you would, who see dharma so clearly?
Unworthy men will despise me, having no protector, and ask for your daughter
too, as shudras ask to hear the Veda. If I did not want to give her, when she
has your qualities, they would seize her by force, like crows snatching the
offering from a sacrifice. Seeing your son unlike yourself, and your daughter in
the power of unworthy men, despised by the world, not knowing myself, I would
surely die at the hands of proud men, brahmin. And without me and without you,
my two children would surely perish, like fish when the water dries up. Without
you, all three of us will surely perish. So you should let me go.

"For women, the highest blessing is to go before their husband, brahmin, not to
live on under their sons. I have given up this son, and this daughter, and my
kin, and my life, for you. For a woman, always standing by what is dear and good
to her husband is better than sacrifices, austerities, vows and gifts of every
kind. What I want to do is in keeping with dharma and highly thought of, dear
and good for you and for the family.

"Good people hold that children, wealth, dear friends and a wife too are there
to free a man from danger. The whole family on one side and a man himself on the
other are not equal. That is the settled view of the wise. So do your work
through me. Save yourself by me. Give me leave, noble one, and look after my
children.

"Those who know dharma say that women must not be killed, when they settle
questions of dharma. They say the rakshasas know dharma. Perhaps he will not
kill me either. For men, death is certain. For women it is uncertain. So you
should send me, you who know dharma. I have enjoyed life. I have had what I
loved. I have practised dharma. I have had your children, whom I love. Death
will not grieve me. I have borne children, I am old, and I have always wanted to
please you. I have thought all this through, and so I have made up my mind. When
you have given me up, noble one, you will find another wife. Then your dharma
will be established again. It is no wrong for a man to have many wives, good
man. But for a woman it is a great wrong to pass over her first husband. Think
of all this, and of how giving up your own life is condemned. Save yourself, the
family and these two children, through me."

When his wife said this to him, Bharata, her husband embraced her. And he and
his wife wept quietly, in deep distress.

**Divergences.**

* **No potter's bowl.** The vulgate has a potter give Bhima a huge bowl for his alms
  (\*1593–\*1594). Not in the CE.
* **The rakshasa is not yet named.** The brahmin and his wife speak of "going there"
  and of "the rakshasa" (1.146.29) without naming Baka. The name comes in the next
  adhyaya. The vulgate names him earlier (\*1599).
* The vulgate adds a passage on women's dependence (\*1606) and one on a widow's
  duty to die with her husband (\*1610–\*1611). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.145.14: Kunti's maxim on returning a kindness is given literally.
* 1.145.18 *baddhavatseva saurabhī*: "like a cow whose calf is tied".
* 1.145.23 *arthaprāptau … narakaḥ*: "in gaining wealth, hell".
* 1.145.35 *dauhitrajān lokān*: "the worlds won through a daughter's sons".
* 1.146.16: the simile of shudras "asking for the Veda" is the wife's.
* 1.146.29: "they say the rakshasas know dharma" is the wife's hope, not the text's
  claim.
* 1.146.34: the wife's statement that it is no wrong for a man to have many wives,
  but a great wrong for a woman to pass over her first husband, is hers.
