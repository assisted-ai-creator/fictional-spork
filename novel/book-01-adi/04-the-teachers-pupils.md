# The Teacher's Pupils

The bard began the first story.

King Janamejaya, the son of Parikshit, was holding a long sacrifice at
Kurukshetra with his brothers. He had three brothers: Shrutasena, Ugrasena
and Bhimasena.

While they sat at the sacrifice, a dog came there, a puppy, the son of
Sarama, the dog of the gods. Janamejaya's brothers beat him, and he ran
howling back to his mother.

His mother saw him howling. "Why are you crying?" she asked. "Who beat you?"

"Janamejaya's brothers beat me," he said.

"You must have done something wrong there," said his mother, "or they would
not have beaten you."

"I did nothing wrong," he said. "I did not look at the offerings. I did not
lick them."

When his mother Sarama heard this, she was stricken with grief for her son.
She went to the sacrifice where Janamejaya and his brothers were sitting,
and she spoke to him in anger.

"My son did nothing wrong. Why was he beaten? He did you no harm, and you
beat him. So a danger will come upon you, a danger you cannot see."

When Sarama, the dog of the gods, spoke to him like that, Janamejaya was badly
shaken, and his heart sank.

When the sacrifice was over, he went back to Hastinapura. There he tried
very hard to find a family priest who would be right for him. *He must be a
priest who can quiet this evil that hangs over me*, he thought.

One day Janamejaya, son of Parikshit, went out hunting, and in a part of his
own country he saw a hermitage. A seer named Shrutashravas lived there, and
with him lived his son Somashravas, whom he loved. Janamejaya went to the son
and asked for him as his family priest. He bowed to the seer.

"Holy one," he said, "let this son of yours be my priest."

The seer answered him. "Janamejaya, this son of mine was born of a
snake-woman. She drank my seed, and he grew in her womb, and so he was made
out of my power as an ascetic. He is a great ascetic himself, and learned in
the Veda. He can quiet every evil that threatens you, except any that comes
from Mahadeva, the great god. But he has one secret vow. If a brahmin asks
him for anything, he will give it to him. If you can accept that, take him."

"Holy one," said Janamejaya, "it shall be so."

He took the priest with him and went back to his brothers. "I have chosen
this man as our teacher," he told them. "Whatever he tells you to do, do it,
and do not question it."

His brothers did as he said. Having given them these orders, Janamejaya set
out for Takshashila, and he brought that country under his rule.

---

At this time there was a seer named Dhaumya, of the Ayoda family, and he had
three pupils: Upamanyu, Aruni and Veda.

He sent one of them, Aruni of Panchala, on an errand. "Go," he said. "There is
a breach in the bank of a field. Close it."

Aruni of Panchala went there as his teacher had told him, but he could not
close the breach. He struggled, and then he saw a way. *So be it*, he
thought. *I will do this*. He lay down in the breach, and while he lay there,
the water stopped.

Some time later the teacher, Dhaumya of the Ayodas, asked his pupils, "Where
has Aruni of Panchala gone?"

"You sent him yourself, holy one," they answered. "You said, 'Go and close
the breach in the bank of the field.'"

"Then let us all go where he is," said Dhaumya.

When he got there, he called out to him. "Aruni! Aruni of Panchala! Where are
you? Come here, child!"

Aruni heard his teacher's voice. He jumped up out of the breach at once and
came and stood before his teacher.

"Here I am," he said. "I was lying in the breach, to hold back the water
that was flowing out and could not be stopped. I heard your voice, holy
one, and I broke through the bank at once and came to you. I greet you,
holy one. Tell me what to do."

"You broke open the bank and rose up out of it," said the teacher. "So your
name will be Uddalaka, the one who breaks open."

Then the teacher blessed him. "Because you did what I told you, you will win
what is good. All the Vedas will shine out in you, and all the teachings on
dharma."

When his teacher had said this, Aruni went where he wished.

---

Dhaumya of the Ayodas had another pupil, named Upamanyu.

The teacher sent him out. "Upamanyu, my child," he said, "look after the
cows."

So, as his teacher had told him, Upamanyu looked after the cows. He guarded
them all day, and at the end of the day he came home and stood in front of
his teacher and bowed.

The teacher saw that he was plump. "Upamanyu, my child," he said, "how do you
keep yourself? You are very plump."

"I live on alms, teacher," said Upamanyu. "I beg for my food."

"You must not eat what you beg without giving it to me first," said the
teacher.

"Yes," said Upamanyu, and he went on guarding the cows. When he had guarded
them, he came home in the same way, and stood in front of his teacher and
bowed.

Even so, the teacher saw that he was still plump. "Upamanyu, my child," he
said, "I take every scrap of your alms. How do you keep yourself now?"

"First I give you what I beg, holy one," said Upamanyu. "Then I go begging a
second time, and I live on that."

"That is not the right way to serve a teacher," said the teacher. "Living
like that, you take food away from others who live on alms. You are greedy."

"Yes," said Upamanyu, and he guarded the cows. When he had guarded them, he
came back to his teacher's house again, and stood in front of him and bowed.

The teacher saw that he was still plump. "I take all your alms," he said,
"and you do not go begging a second time. Yet you are plump. How do you keep
yourself?"

"Sir," said Upamanyu, "I live on the milk of these cows."

"It is not right for you to drink their milk," said the teacher. "I have not
allowed it."

Upamanyu promised he would not, and guarded the cows, and came back to his
teacher's house, and stood in front of his teacher and bowed.

The teacher saw that he was still plump. "You do not eat what you beg," he
said. "You do not go begging again. You do not drink the milk. Yet you are
plump. How do you keep yourself?"

"Sir," said Upamanyu, "I drink the froth that the calves bring up when they
suck from their mothers."

"These good calves pity you," said the teacher, "and so they bring up more
froth than they should. Living like that, you are taking food from the
calves. You must not drink the froth either."

Upamanyu promised, and he guarded the cows with nothing to eat. He was
forbidden everything. He did not eat what he begged, he did not go begging
again, he did not drink the milk, and he did not take the froth.

One day, in the forest, tormented by hunger, he ate the leaves of the arka
plant. Those leaves are salty and sharp and hot, and they are harsh when they
are digested. They struck his eyes, and he went blind. Even blind, he kept
wandering about, and he fell into a well.

When he did not come back, the teacher spoke to his pupils. "I have
forbidden Upamanyu everything. He is surely angry. That is why he has been
gone so long and has not come back."

So he went to the forest and called for him. "Upamanyu! Where are you? Come
here, child!"

Upamanyu heard his teacher calling and answered loudly. "Here I am, teacher!
I have fallen into a well."

"How did you fall into a well?" asked the teacher.

"I ate arka leaves and went blind," said Upamanyu. "That is how I fell into
the well."

"Praise the Ashvins," said the teacher. "They are the physicians of the
gods. They will give you back your sight."

So, as his teacher told him, Upamanyu began to praise the twin gods, the
Ashvins, with words from the verses of the Rig Veda:

> *You go first, you were born first; your light is many-coloured.
> I praise you with my voice, you who blaze and have no end:
> two birds of heaven, spotless, beyond all measure,
> dwelling above all the worlds.
> Golden birds, guides to the world beyond,
> Nasatya and Dasra, fair of face and always winning,
> you weave swiftly on your fine loom, weaving the white and the dark.
> When the quail was caught in the eagle's grip,
> you set her free, Ashvins, for her good fortune.
> Three hundred and sixty cows give birth to one calf,
> and they milk it; they stand in many stalls,
> yet they give a single milking. You milk the hot offering from them.
> Seven hundred spokes rest in one hub,
> and twenty more are set in the rims;
> a wheel with no rim turns and never grows old.
> One wheel turns, with twelve spokes, six hubs and one axle,
> and it holds the nectar of immortality,
> and all the gods cling to it. Ashvins, set it free, and do not fail.
> You split the mountain and drove the cows out.
> At the beginning you made the ten directions,
> and the seers follow where you go; gods and men walk the earth.
> You make every colour of every form, and they dwell in all the worlds.
> Nasatyas, Ashvins, we come to you, and to your garland of lotuses.
> Immortal ones, who make the truth grow:
> set the cows free, so that we may live.*

When he had praised them like this, the Ashvins came. "We are pleased," they
said. "Here is a cake for you. Eat it."

"You have never said anything untrue," said Upamanyu. "But I cannot eat this
cake without first offering it to my teacher."

"Your teacher once praised us in just this way," said the Ashvins, "and we
were pleased and gave him a cake. He ate it without offering it to his own
teacher. You do as your teacher did."

"I beg your pardon, Ashvins," said Upamanyu. "I cannot eat it without offering
it to my teacher."

"We are pleased with you," said the Ashvins, "for the way you serve your
teacher. Your teacher's teeth are made of black iron. Yours will be made of
gold. You will see again, and you will win what is good."

When the Ashvins had said this, Upamanyu had his sight back. He went to his
teacher, greeted him, and told him everything. His teacher was pleased with
him.

"You will win what is good," he said, "as the Ashvins told you. And all the
Vedas will shine out in you."

That was Upamanyu's test.

---

Dhaumya of the Ayodas had a third pupil, named Veda.

The teacher gave him his orders. "Veda, my child, stay here. You must live in
my house for a while and serve me. It will be good for you."

"Yes," said Veda, and he lived for a long time in his teacher's house, giving
himself to his teacher's service. Like an ox, he was always yoked to his
teacher's loads. He bore cold and heat, hunger and thirst and pain, and he
never went against his teacher in anything.

After a long time, his teacher was satisfied with him. Because his teacher
was satisfied, Veda won what is good, and came to know everything.

That was Veda's test.

Then, with his teacher's leave, Veda finished his studies and left his
teacher's house, and became a householder. While he lived in his own home,
he had three pupils of his own. He never told them, "Do this work," or
"Serve your teacher." He knew how hard it is to live in a teacher's house,
and he did not want to lay those hardships on his pupils.

**Choices.**

* **The Ashvin hymn (1.3.60–70)** is a Vedic riddle-poem, and several of its
  lines are obscure even in Sanskrit. It is given here by its images, in
  order and compressed. Lines whose meaning is uncertain (parts of 1.3.62,
  1.3.66 and 1.3.69–70) are shortened to their clear images and not
  explained. Ganguli's version is an expanded interpretation from the
  vulgate and was not followed.
* 1.3.15 *antareṇa mahādevakṛtyām*: "except any [harm] that comes from
  Mahadeva". *Kṛtyā* can mean a spell or a harm sent against someone.
* 1.3.29 *Uddālaka*: from *ud-√dal*, "to break open". The name's meaning is
  given because the text makes the pun.
* The "thoughts" in italics (1.3.10, 1.3.22) render the text's *iti*
  clauses, which report what the person thought.
