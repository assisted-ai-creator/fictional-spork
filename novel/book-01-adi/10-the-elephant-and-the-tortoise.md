# The Elephant and the Tortoise

"When the snakes had said this," the bard went on, "Garuda said to his mother,
'I am going to bring the nectar. I want to know what I may eat.'

"'In a lonely place in the ocean's belly,' said Vinata, 'there is a fine home
of the Nishadas, many thousands of them. Eat them, and bring the nectar. But
you must never think of killing a brahmin, not in any way. A brahmin must not
be killed by any creature. He is like fire. When he is angry, a brahmin
becomes fire, and the sun, and poison, and the sword. The brahmin eats first
of all beings. He is the best of the classes, a father and a teacher.'

"'Mother,' said Garuda, 'tell me the good signs by which I will know a
brahmin. I ask you for good reason.'

"'If one goes down your throat like a swallowed fishhook,' said Vinata, 'and
burns you like a live coal, my son, know that he is a bull among brahmins.'

"Vinata knew her son's strength had no equal, and still, out of love for him,
she said these words and gave him her blessing.

"'May the Wind guard your wings, my son, and the Moon your back. May Fire guard
your head, and the Sun guard all of you. And I, my son, will always be given
to your peace and your welfare. Go safely on your way, my child, and do what
you have to do.'

"When he heard his mother's words, he spread his wings and flew up into the
sky. Then, mighty and hungry, he went to the Nishadas, like Time, the great
ender, come to make an end. As he fell upon the Nishadas, he raised a great
cloud of dust that touched the sky. He dried up the water in the ocean's belly,
and he shook the mountains near by.

"Then the king of the birds made his mouth huge, and blocked the Nishadas'
path. The Nishadas ran in haste straight towards the mouth of the eater of
snakes. They went into that wide, gaping mouth by the thousand, like birds
flying in terror into the sky, when the wind and the dust bewilder them and a
great storm shakes the trees of the forest. Then the bird, the scorcher of
enemies, restless and mighty, closed his mouth. Hungry, the lord of the
sky-travellers destroyed those fish-eaters of many kinds.

---

"A brahmin went down his throat, together with his wife, and burned like a
live coal. The sky-traveller said to him, 'Best of brahmins, come out quickly
from my mouth. I have opened it for you. I must never kill a brahmin, even one
who is always given to wrongdoing.'

"The brahmin answered Garuda as he said this. 'This Nishada woman is my wife.
Let her come out with me.'

"'Take the Nishada woman too, and fly out quickly,' said Garuda. 'Save
yourself fast, while my heat has not yet digested you.'

"So the brahmin came out, with the Nishada woman, and he blessed Garuda and
went on to the place he wished. When the brahmin and his wife had come out,
the king of the birds spread his wings and flew up into the sky, swift as
thought.

"Then he saw his father. When his father asked him, he told him everything.
'The snakes have sent me to bring the soma, and I am ready. I will bring it
today, to free my mother from slavery. My mother told me to eat the Nishadas.
But I have eaten them by the thousand and I am still not satisfied. So show
me something else to eat, blessed father, so that when I have eaten it I will
be able to bring the nectar, my lord.'

"'There was a great seer named Vibhavasu,' said Kashyapa, 'who was very quick
to anger. He had a younger brother, Supratika, a great ascetic. The great sage
Supratika did not want to keep his wealth together with his brother's. He
always spoke of dividing it.

"'Then his brother Vibhavasu said to Supratika, "Many people are always
wanting to divide what they own, out of folly. Once they have divided it,
their wealth bewitches them and they no longer respect each other. Enemies
who look like friends see that they are fools, each out for himself, each
cut off with his own wealth, and they split them further apart. Others see
that they are divided, and they fall on them through the gaps. Ruin beyond
measure comes quickly to those who are divided. That is why the wise do not
praise division, among those who should be bound by the rule of their elders
and who suspect each other. You will not be held back, Supratika. You want
your wealth divided. So you will become an elephant."

"'Cursed like this, Supratika said to Vibhavasu, "And you will become a
tortoise, living under the water."

"'So Supratika and Vibhavasu cursed each other, and for the sake of wealth, in
their folly, they became an elephant and a tortoise. Their anger was at fault.
Even though they have been born as animals, they delight in hating each other,
and they are proud of their size and strength. The two huge creatures are in
this lake, and they keep up their old feud.

"'Here comes one of them now, the glorious great elephant. At the sound of his
trumpeting, the huge tortoise who lies under the water rises too, and churns
the whole lake. When he sees him, the mighty elephant curls up his trunk and
falls upon the water. He churns the lake full of fish with the force of his
tusks and the tip of his trunk, his tail and his feet, and the mighty tortoise
raises his head and comes to fight. The elephant stands six yojanas high and
twice that in length. The tortoise is three yojanas high and ten yojanas
round. They are mad for battle, and each of them wants to beat the other. Eat
them quickly, and do what you want to do.'

---

"When he heard his father's words, the sky-traveller of terrible speed seized
the elephant in one claw and the tortoise in the other. He flew high up into
the sky, and came to the Alamba ford, and went to the trees of the gods.

"Struck by the wind from his wings, the heavenly trees with their golden
branches trembled with fear. *May he not break us!* they thought. He saw that
their limbs were shaking, those trees whose fruits and shoots are the wishes
of the heart, and the sky-traveller went on to others, of matchless beauty:
great trees shining with fruits of gold and silver and branches of beryl, with
the waters of the ocean all around them.

"There a very great rohina tree, grown enormously tall, spoke to the best of
birds as he flew towards it, swift as thought. 'This great branch of mine
stretches a hundred yojanas. Sit on this branch and eat the elephant and the
tortoise.'

"Then the best of birds, whose body was like a mountain, came down fast on
that tree, the home of thousands of birds, and shook it. And he broke that
branch, thick with leaves.

"At the mere touch of mighty Garuda's feet, the branch of the tree broke, and
he caught the broken branch. He looked in surprise at the great broken branch,
and he saw the Valakhilya seers hanging from it head down. Afraid that he
would kill them, the lord of birds swooped after it and took the branch in his
beak, watching over them. The bird flew slowly, knocking down mountains as he
went. So he flew over many lands, with the elephant and the tortoise, out of
pity for the Valakhilyas, and he could find no place to come down.

"He went to Gandhamadana, the best of mountains, that never passes away, and
there he saw his father, Kashyapa, deep in austerity.

"His father saw him too: the bird of heavenly form, full of splendour, courage
and strength, as swift as mind and wind, like a mountain peak, raised like the
staff of Brahma. He was beyond thought, beyond knowing, terrifying to every
creature. He had the power of illusion, and he was like blazing fire, rising.
Gods, danavas and rakshasas could not assail him or defeat him. He split
mountain peaks and dried up the waters of rivers. He was terrible, a stirrer
of worlds, and to look at he was like Death.

"When the blessed Kashyapa saw him come, he knew what he meant to do, and he
said, 'My son, do nothing rash, or you will soon be in pain. Do not let the
Valakhilyas, who drink the rays of the sun, burn you in their anger.'

"For his son's sake, Kashyapa made peace with the Valakhilyas, whose austerity
had made them perfect, and told them the reason. 'Garuda has set out to do
something for the good of all creatures, you ascetics. He means to do a great
deed. Please give him leave.'

"When the blessed one said this, the sages let go of the branch and went to
the holy Himalaya to practise austerity.

"When they had gone, Vinata's son, his mouth full of the branch, asked his
father, Kashyapa, 'Blessed one, where shall I let go of this branch of the
tree? Tell me of a place where there are no brahmins.'

"Kashyapa told him of a mountain where no people lived, its caves shut in by
snow, a place no one else could reach even in thought. Tarkshya, the bird, set
his mind on the great hollow of that mountain and flew
there fast, with the branch, the elephant and the tortoise. A thong made from
a hundred hides could not have gone round the great branch the bird carried as
he flew. Garuda, the best of flying things, covered a hundred thousand
yojanas, and it did not take him long.

"At his father's word, he reached that mountain in a moment, and there the
sky-traveller let go of the great branch, and it fell with a crash. The king
of mountains shook, struck by the wind of his wings, and its trees broke and
fell and let loose a shower of flowers. All around the mountain, its peaks,
bright with jewels and gold, the pride of the great mountain, broke and
crumbled. Many trees that the branch struck down shone with their golden
flowers like clouds lit with lightning. Blossoming in gold, mixed with the
coloured ores of the mountain and reddened by the rays of the sun, the trees
glowed there.

"Then the best of birds perched on a peak of that mountain, and Garuda ate
both of them, the elephant and the tortoise. And he flew up from the
mountaintop, swift as thought.

---

"Then omens rose among the gods, warning of danger. Indra's beloved
thunderbolt blazed up, as if in pain. A meteor, smoking and flaming, fell from
the sky in the daytime. The weapons of the Vasus, the Rudras, the Adityas, the
Sadhyas, the Maruts and all the other hosts of gods turned on one another.
This had never happened before, not even in the war of the gods and the
asuras. Winds blew, with thunder, and meteors fell on every side. The
cloudless sky thundered with a great roar, and even the god of the gods rained
blood. The gods' garlands faded, and their splendour dimmed. Fierce clouds of
evil omen rained blood in torrents, and dust rose up and fouled their crowns.

"Then Indra of the hundred sacrifices, shaken with fear, with the gods around
him, saw these terrible omens and said to Brihaspati, 'Why have these great
and terrible omens appeared, blessed one? I see no enemy who could attack us
in battle.'

"'This has come of your fault, lord of the gods, and your carelessness, Indra
of the hundred sacrifices,' said Brihaspati. 'By the austerity of the
Valakhilyas a wonderful being has arisen. The son of the sage Kashyapa and of
Vinata, a sky-traveller, has come to take the soma. He is mighty, and he can
take whatever form he wants. That bird, the best of the strong, is able to
take the soma. I believe anything of him. He could do even what cannot be
done.'

"When Shakra heard this, he said to the guardians of the nectar, 'A bird of
great courage and strength has set out to take the soma from here. I am
warning you, so that he does not take it by force. Brihaspati has told me his
strength has no equal.'

"When the gods heard this, they were amazed, and they took great care. They
stood round the nectar, and Indra of the hundred sacrifices stood with them,
holding the thunderbolt. They wore costly armour, golden and bright, set with
beryl, those proud gods. They raised terrible weapons of every kind by the
thousand, sharp and keen-edged, sparking, flaming and smoking on every side:
discuses and iron clubs, tridents and axes, sharp spears of many kinds,
spotless swords and fearsome maces, each suited to its bearer's body. With
those shining weapons, and adorned with heavenly ornaments, the hosts of the
gods stood shining and free of stain.

"The gods, whose strength, courage and splendour had no equal, who break open
the cities of the asuras, set their minds on guarding the nectar, and their
bodies shone like blazing fire. So the gods held that great field of battle,
crowded with hundreds of thousands of clubs, and it shone in the rays of the
sun like a piece of the sky that had fallen."

**Choices.**

* **Two meetings with Kashyapa.** Garuda "saw his father" at 1.25.7, without a
  place named, and then finds him again on Gandhamadana at 1.26.5. Both are
  kept.
* 1.25.15 *guruśāstre nibaddhānām*: "those bound by the teacher's (or elder's)
  rule". Rendered "those who should be bound by the rule of their elders".
* 1.25.20 *sarasy asmin*: "in this lake". The CE does not name the lake. Garuda
  then goes to the Alamba ford (*alambatīrtha*, 1.25.27).
* 1.25.29 *manorathaphalāṅkurān*: trees "whose fruits and shoots are the heart's
  wishes". Kept as an image.
* 1.26.2 *smayan*: "smiling" or "in surprise". Rendered "in surprise".
* 1.26.18 *tārkṣyaḥ*: Garuda is called Tarkshya here. The name is kept.
* 1.26.19 *vadhraḥ … śatacarmā*: a thong of a hundred hides could not go
  round the branch.
* 1.26.31 *devānām api yo devaḥ*: "even he who is the god of the gods". The CE
  does not say which god. Left as it is.
* **The Valakhilyas.** Their size, "the size of a thumb joint", is given at
  1.27.8, in the next chapter. Here the novel names them only.
* Rohina (*rohiṇa*): a kind of tree, kept as its name.
