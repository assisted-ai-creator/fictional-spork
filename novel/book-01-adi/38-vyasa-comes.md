# Vyasa Comes

"Mother," said Bhishma, "I will tell you again the sure way to make the line of
Bharata live on. Hear me as I tell it. Let some brahmin of good qualities be
invited, with wealth, to father children in Vichitravirya's fields."

Then Satyavati spoke to Bhishma. Her voice faltered, and she was half laughing
and ashamed.

"What you say is true, strong-armed Bharata. Because I trust you, I will tell
you something, for the sake of our line. I cannot keep it from you, when we are
in such trouble. You are the dharma of our family. You are its truth, and its
last refuge. So listen to what I say, and then do what comes next.

"My father was a righteous man, and he kept a ferry boat. One day, when I was in
my first youth, I went to the boat. Then the great seer Parashara, the wise, the
best of those who uphold dharma, came to the boat to cross the Yamuna. As I was
taking him across the river, the best of sages came up to me. He was tormented
by desire, and he spoke to me gently and sweetly, at length. I was afraid of a
curse, and I was afraid of my father. He offered me boons that are hard to win.
I did not dare refuse him, Bharata. I was only a girl, and he overpowered me
with his power and brought me under his control. He covered the world in
darkness, and it happened there in the boat.

"Before that I had a strong smell of fish, which people found disgusting. The
sage took it away and gave me this fine scent instead. Then he said to me, 'When
you have given birth to my child on an island in this river, you will still be a
virgin.'

"So Parashara's son was born to me when I was a girl, long ago. He became a
great seer and a great yogi. He is remembered as Dvaipayana. The blessed seer
divided the four Vedas by his austerity, and so in the world he came to be
Vyasa, the Divider. And because he is dark, he is Krishna. He speaks the truth,
and he is devoted to calm. He is an ascetic whose sins are burned away. If you
and I appoint him, you of boundless splendour, he will surely father good
children in his brother's fields. He said to me then, 'Think of me when there is
something to be done.' I will think of him, strong-armed Bhishma, if you wish
it. With your consent, Bhishma, that great ascetic will surely father sons in
Vichitravirya's fields."

When the great seer's name was spoken, Bhishma joined his hands.

"A wise man looks at the three, dharma, wealth and desire," he said. "He sees
how wealth leads to more wealth, dharma to more dharma, and desire to more
desire, and how each can also lead the other way, each on its own. He thinks it
all through carefully with his mind, and then he makes up his mind. What you
have said is in keeping with dharma, and it is good for our family. It is the
best way, and it pleases me very much."

---

When Bhishma had agreed, joy of the Kurus, Kali called to mind the sage Krishna
Dvaipayana. The wise one was reciting the Vedas, and he knew his mother's
thought. In an instant, unseen by anyone, he was there, joy of the Kurus.

His mother honoured her son with the proper rites. She put her arms round him,
and her breasts flowed with milk over him. The fisherman's daughter wept to see
her son after so long. The great seer sprinkled his grieving mother with water
and bowed to her. Then Vyasa, her first-born son, spoke to her.

"I have come to do what you want. You know the truth of dharma. Tell me what to
do, and I will do what pleases you."

Then the family priest honoured the great seer, and he accepted it, with
mantras, as the rules require. When he was seated, his mother asked after his
health. Then Satyavati looked at him and spoke.

"Sons are born to a mother and a father alike, poet. The father is their master,
and so, without doubt, is the mother. By the Ordainer's will you are my first
son, brahmin seer, and Vichitravirya was my youngest. As Bhishma is his brother
on his father's side, so you are his brother on your mother's side. Or so I hope
you see it, son. This son of Shantanu keeps to the truth, and his courage is
truth. He will not set his mind on having children, nor on ruling the kingdom.

"So, out of regard for your brother, for the sake of the line, at Bhishma's word
and at my bidding, sinless one, out of pity for all beings and to protect them,
and out of kindness, hear what I say and do it. Your younger brother's wives are
like daughters of the gods. They are young and beautiful, and they long for sons
by dharma. Father children on them, son, for you can. Give them children worthy
of this family, to carry on its line."

"Satyavati," said Vyasa, "you know dharma, both the higher and the lower. Your
mind, you who know dharma, is set on dharma. So at your bidding, and for the
sake of dharma, I will do what you wish. This has been seen from of old. I will
give my brother sons like Mitra and Varuna. But the two queens must keep the vow
I lay down for them, for a year, as is right. Then they will be pure. No woman
may come to me who has not kept the vow."

"Let the queen conceive at once," said Satyavati. "Do what you must to make it
so. When a kingdom has no king, there is no rain and there are no gods. How can
a kingdom without a king be held together, lord? So give her a child. Bhishma
will bring him up."

"If I must give a son at once, out of season," said Vyasa, "then let her bear my
ugliness. That will be her highest vow. If Kausalya can bear my smell, my looks,
my clothes and my body, let her conceive a fine child this very day."

When he had said this, the sage vanished, to wait for the meeting.

Then the queen went to her daughter-in-law and found her alone. She spoke to her
words of dharma, sensible and kind.

"Kausalya, listen to the dharma I tell you. It is plain that the Bharatas are
dying out, because my luck has run out. Bhishma saw my distress, and the trouble
of his father's line, and gave me an idea, to make dharma grow. But that idea
depends on you, daughter. I know it. Raise up the lost line of Bharata again.
Bear a son as bright as the king of the gods, you of the lovely hips. He will
carry the heavy burden of our family's kingdom."

So, somehow, by dharma, she won over her daughter-in-law, who kept to dharma.
Then she fed brahmins, divine seers and guests.

---

Then, in time, when her daughter-in-law had bathed after her season, Satyavati
settled her on the bed. And she said to her softly, "Kausalya, you have a
husband's brother. He will come to you tonight. Be watchful and wait for him. He
will come at midnight."

When she heard her mother-in-law's words, as she lay on the fine bed, she
thought of Bhishma and of the other bulls of the Kurus.

Then the truthful seer, appointed first to Ambika, came into her bed while the
lamps were burning. When the queen saw how dark he was, with his tawny matted
hair, his blazing eyes and his brown beard, she shut her eyes. He lay with her
that night, to please his mother. But the princess of Kashi was so afraid that
she could not look at him.

When he came out, his mother met her son and said, "Will she have a prince with
good qualities, son?"

Vyasa, who knew what lies beyond the senses, and whom fate was driving, heard
his mother's words. He said, "He will have the strength of ten thousand
elephants. He will be learned and the best of royal seers, very fortunate, very
brave and very wise. He will have a hundred mighty sons. But because of his
mother's fault, he will be blind."

When she heard this, his mother said to her son, "A blind man is not a fit king
for the Kurus, rich in austerity. You should give the Kuru line a second king,
to protect his kin and make his fathers' line grow."

He promised, "So be it," and the great ascetic went out. And in time Kausalya
gave birth to that son, and he was blind.

Again the blameless queen Satya spoke to her daughter-in-law, and she called the
seer as before. The great seer went to Ambalika in the same way. When she saw
the seer, she too was dismayed, Bharata, and she turned pale.

When Vyasa, Satyavati's son, saw her frightened, pale and dismayed, he said to
her, "Because you turned pale when you saw how ugly I am, this son of yours will
be pale. He will be Pandu, the Pale One. And that will be his name here, lovely
one." With that the blessed one, the best of seers, went out.

When he came out, Satya saw her son and spoke to him. And he told his mother
again that the boy would be pale. His mother asked him for one more son, and the
great seer said to her, "So be it."

Then the queen, in time, gave birth to a boy, Pandu, who had every lucky mark
and seemed to blaze with glory. His sons were the five Pandavas, great archers.

Then, when her season came, Satyavati appointed her elder daughter-in-law to
Vyasa again. But the girl, who was like a daughter of the gods, thought of the
great seer's looks and his smell, and she was afraid. So she did not do as the
queen told her. The princess of Kashi dressed a slave woman, as lovely as an
apsaras, in her own ornaments, and sent her to Krishna.

When the seer came, the slave woman rose to meet him and bowed to him. When he
gave her leave, she lay down, and she served him with respect. The seer was
pleased with her in the joy of love, and he spent the night with her, and she
was glad. As he rose, he said to her, "You will be a slave no longer. A glorious
child has come into your womb, lovely one. He will be righteous in the world,
the best of all the wise."

He was born, and his name was Vidura, the son of Krishna Dvaipayana, the brother
of Dhritarashtra and of Pandu, of boundless understanding. He was Dharma, in the
form of Vidura, because of the curse of the great Mandavya. He knew the truth of
things, and he was free of desire and anger.

Then Vyasa, free of his debt to dharma, met his mother again. He told her of the
child that had been conceived, and he vanished there and then.

So in Vichitravirya's field, from Dvaipayana, were born those sons like children
of the gods, who made the Kuru line grow.

**Divergences and the CE's own doublets.**

* **Satyavati's story told twice.** Chapter 19 (1.57.56–71) tells Vyasa's birth in
  the narrator's voice, with Parashara's mist, the island and the boon of scent.
  Here Satyavati tells it herself, more briefly, with darkness instead of mist
  (1.99.10) and no mention of her father's being King Vasu. The vulgate adds the
  mist and island here too (\*1049, \*1051). The novel keeps each telling as its
  own place gives it.
* **Who proposes Vyasa.** Bhishma proposes "some brahmin" (1.99.2). Satyavati names
  Vyasa (1.99.15–17). Later she tells Kausalya that "Bhishma gave me the idea"
  (1.99.46). All kept.
* **Why the children were marked.** Vyasa asks for a year's vow first (1.99.38–39). Satyavati wants a child at once (1.99.40–41), so he makes bearing his ugliness the vow instead (1.99.42–43). He names each child's flaw only afterwards, as the mother's fault (1.100.10, 17). See ledger L-72.

**Choices.**

* **Kausalya.** Ambika is called *Kausalyā* (1.99.43, 45; 1.100.2, 13), "the Kosala
  woman", presumably after her mother. The novel keeps the name in speech and where
  the text uses it, and says Ambika elsewhere.
* **Satyavati's other names.** *Kālī* (1.99.21), *dāśeyī*, "the fisherman's daughter"
  (1.99.23), and *Satyā* (1.100.14, 19). The novel keeps "Kali" once and "Satya"
  where the text has it.
* 1.99.10 *abhibhūya sa māṃ bālāṃ tejasā vaśam ānayat*: "overpowering me, a girl,
  with his power, he brought me under his control". Rendered plainly.
* 1.99.14 *vyasya vedān*: "having divided the Vedas", the source of the name Vyasa;
  *kārṣṇyāt kṛṣṇatvam*: "Krishna from his darkness".
* 1.99.18–19: Bhishma's maxim on dharma, wealth and desire and their
  *anubandha*, "consequences", including the "opposite" (*viparīta*) outcomes of
  each. Paraphrased closely.
* 1.99.23 *prasnavair abhiṣicya*: she "bathed him with the flow of her breasts".
* 1.99.42 *akālikam*: "out of season", i.e. without the year's vow.
* 1.100.10 *mātuḥ … vaiguṇyāt*: "because of the mother's fault".
* 1.100.21: the five Pandavas are named as Pandu's sons at his birth, in the
  narrator's voice. Later chapters tell how they were fathered.
* 1.100.22: the text says Satyavati sent "the eldest daughter-in-law" (*jyeṣṭhām*)
  again, that is, Ambika.
* 1.100.28: Dharma born as Vidura through Mandavya's curse. The story is told in the
  next two adhyayas (1.101–102).
