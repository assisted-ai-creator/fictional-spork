# Ganga

There was a king born in the line of Ikshvaku, a lord of the earth named
Mahabhisha, true in his word and true in his courage. He pleased the king of the
gods with a thousand horse sacrifices and a hundred Vajapeya rites, and so the
lord won heaven.

Once the gods were waiting upon Brahma, and the royal seers were there, and King
Mahabhisha among them. Then Ganga, the best of rivers, came to the Grandfather.
The wind lifted her garment, bright as the moon, and at once the hosts of the
gods lowered their faces. But the royal seer Mahabhisha looked at the river
without fear. The blessed Brahma thought ill of Mahabhisha, and said to him,
"You will be born among mortals, and then you will win back these worlds."

The king thought about all the kings who were rich in austerity, and he chose
Pratipa, of great splendour, to be his father.

The river, the best of rivers, saw King Mahabhisha lose his composure, and she
went away thinking of him in her heart. On her way she saw the Vasus, the gods
who live in heaven, their forms ruined, their power struck down by faintness.
When the best of rivers saw them like that, she asked them, "Why have you lost
your forms? Is all well with the dwellers of heaven?"

"We have been cursed, great river," said the Vasu gods, "by the great Vasishtha,
in his anger, for a small offence. Long ago, in our folly, we all passed by the
best of seers, Vasishtha, as he sat hidden at his twilight prayers. In his anger
he cursed us: 'Be born from a womb.' What a man who speaks the sacred word has
said cannot be made otherwise. So you must become a woman and bear us, the
Vasus, as your sons on earth. We do not want to enter the unclean belly of a
human woman."

When they said this, Ganga said to the Vasus, "So be it," and she asked them,
"Who among mortals, the best of men, will be your father?"

"The son of Pratipa," said the Vasus, "a righteous king named Shantanu, will be
born in the world of men. He will be our father."

"I think so too, sinless gods, as you say," said Ganga. "I will do what pleases
him, and what you want."

"You must throw your sons into the water as soon as they are born," said the
Vasus, "so that our release, you who go through the three worlds, comes before
long."

"I will do this," said Ganga. "But let one son be kept for him, so that his
union with me for the sake of a son is not in vain."

"Each of us will give a quarter of half his strength," said the Vasus. "With
that strength your son will be the son he wants. But he will not have children
of his own among mortals. So your son will be mighty, and have no son."

When they had made this agreement with Ganga, the Vasus went away gladly, as
they had planned.

---

Then King Pratipa, who delighted in the good of all creatures, sat for many
years on the bank of the Ganga, praying. Ganga rose from the water, full of
beauty and grace, like Shri in bodily form, most desirable to see. The
lovely-faced woman of heavenly form and high spirit came and sat on the right
thigh of the royal seer as he prayed, a thigh like the trunk of a sal tree.

"What can I do for you, lovely one?" said Pratipa, lord of the earth, to the
proud woman. "What do you desire?"

"I desire you, king, best of the Kurus," said the woman. "Love me. The good
condemn turning away women who desire them."

"I will not go to another man's wife out of desire, fair one," said Pratipa,
"nor to a woman not of my class. Know that this is my vow, and it is lawful."

"I am not unlucky," said the woman, "nor forbidden to you, nor one to be spoken
ill of, ever. Love me, king, for I love you. I am a maiden, and a fine woman."

"I have set aside what you urge me to," said Pratipa, "dear as it would be. If I
did otherwise, the wreck of dharma would destroy me. You came and sat on my
right thigh, lovely woman. Know, timid one, that this is the seat of children
and daughters-in-law. The left side is the place of a lover, and you avoided it.
So I will not love you with desire, fine woman. Be my daughter-in-law, lovely
one. I choose you for my son, for you came and sat on the side of the
daughter-in-law, you of the lovely thighs."

"So be it, you who know dharma," said the woman. "Let me be joined with your
son. Out of devotion to you I will serve the famous Bharata line. You are the
refuge of all the kings on earth. I could not tell your virtues in a hundred
years. The goodness of those who have gone before in your line is beyond
compare. But your son must not know my family, my lord, and whatever I do, your
son must never question it. Living with your son like this, I will make his joy
grow. By holy and dear sons your son will reach heaven."

"So be it," she said, king, and she vanished there and then. And the king kept
it in mind, waiting for the birth of a son.

At this very time, Pratipa, the bull among kshatriyas, practised austerity with
his wife for the sake of a son, joy of the Kurus. To them, when they were old,
that Mahabhisha was born as their son. He was born the child of one who was
calm, *shanta*, and so he was Shantanu. Remembering the worlds that never pass
away, which he had won by his own deeds, Shantanu did only holy deeds, best of
the Kurus.

When Shantanu was a young man, Pratipa instructed him. "Long ago a woman came to
me, Shantanu, for your good. If that lovely woman comes to you in private, my
son, full of desire, beautiful, a heavenly woman wanting a son, you must not
question her. Do not ask her, 'Who are you, lovely one, and whose?' Whatever she
does, you must not ask her about it, sinless one. At my command, love her, for
she loves you." So he told him.

When Pratipa had instructed his son Shantanu like this, the king consecrated him
in his own kingdom and went into the forest.

---

King Shantanu was wise, and famous on earth as a bowman. He loved to hunt, and
he was always in the forest. The best of kings killed deer and buffalo, and he
roamed alone along the Ganga, where the perfected ones and the heavenly singers
go.

One day, great king, he saw a wonderful woman, blazing with beauty, like Shri
herself, the lotus goddess. She was flawless in every part, with lovely teeth,
adorned with heavenly ornaments, wearing fine clothes, alone, and she shone like
the heart of a lotus. When he saw her, the hair on his body stood up. He was
amazed at her beauty, and though he drank her in with his eyes, the lord of men
could not have enough. And when she saw the king of great splendour wandering
there, her heart went out to him in love, and the graceful woman could not have
enough of him either.

Then the king spoke to her gently, in a soft voice. "Whether you are a goddess
or a danava woman, a gandharva woman or an apsaras, a yaksha woman or a snake
woman, or a human woman, you of the slender waist, whoever you are, you who
shine like the child of a god, be my wife, lovely one."

When she heard the king's gentle, sweet words, spoken with a smile, the
faultless woman remembered the Vasus' agreement, and she came to him. She spoke,
delighting the king's heart with her words. "I will be your queen, lord of the
earth, and I will obey you. But whatever I do, king, good or bad, you must not
stop me, nor say anything unkind to me. As long as you behave like this, I will
live with you, king. If I am stopped, or spoken to unkindly, I will surely leave
you."

"So be it," the king said to her, best of the Bharatas, and she was filled with
joy beyond compare to have won that best of kings.

Shantanu, master of himself, won her and enjoyed her as he wished. Thinking,
*She must not be questioned*, he said nothing to her at all. The lord of the
earth was delighted with her character and her conduct, her beauty and
generosity and her virtues, and the care she gave him in private. For she was
the goddess Ganga, the river that flows in three worlds, who had taken on a
lovely human body, of heavenly beauty, the fair one. She had come as a wife to
Shantanu, the lion among kings, as bright as the king of the gods, whose desire
had been granted by his good fortune. With her skill in love and tenderness,
with her charming ways and her dancing, she delighted the king, as he delighted
in her. The king was so lost in love, so captured by the virtues of that finest
of women, that he did not notice the many years, seasons and months go by.

Taking his pleasure with her as he wished, the lord of men fathered eight sons
on her, as fair as the immortals. And as each son was born, Bharata, she threw
him into the water. "I am doing you good," she would say, and she would drown
him in the stream of the Ganga.

King Shantanu did not like this. But the lord of the earth said nothing to her,
afraid she would leave him.

Then, when the eighth son was born, and she seemed to be smiling, the king, in
pain, wanting to save his son, said to her, "Do not kill him! Who are you, and
whose? Why do you kill your sons? Killer of your sons, do not bring this great
sin on yourself. Stop, you whom all will condemn."

"You want a son," said the woman. "I will not kill your son, best of those who
have sons. But my time with you is worn out, as we agreed. I am Ganga, the
daughter of Jahnu, served by the hosts of great seers. I have lived with you to
bring about a purpose of the gods. These eight are the Vasus, gods of great
fortune and great power, who became men through the fault of Vasishtha's curse.
There was no one on earth but you to father them, and no human woman like me to
bear them. So I became a woman to be their mother. By fathering the eight Vasus
you have won worlds that never pass away. This was the gods' agreement with the
Vasus, which I promised: 'I will set each one free from his human birth as soon
as he is born.' So they are freed from the curse of the great Apava. Good
fortune to you. I will go. Protect this son of great vows. This was my time of
dwelling with you, made for the sake of the Vasus. Know that this son was born
of me, given by Ganga."

---

"Who is this Apava?" said Shantanu. "What wrong did the Vasus do, that by his
curse they all came into human bodies? And what has this boy, given by Ganga,
done, that because of it he will live among men? How did the Vasus, the lords of
the whole world, come to be born among men? Tell me, daughter of Jahnu."

When he asked her this, the goddess Ganga, the daughter of Jahnu, said to her
husband Shantanu, the bull among men,

"The son whom Varuna had long ago, best of the Bharatas, is the sage named
Vasishtha, also known as Apava. His holy hermitage, full of deer and birds, is
on the side of Meru, the king of mountains, and flowers of every season cover
it. There the son of Varuna practised austerity, best of the Bharatas, in a
forest with sweet roots, fruit and water, the best of those who do holy deeds.

"Surabhi, the daughter of Daksha, very proud, bore a cow to Kashyapa, bull of
the Bharatas. For the good of the world, the righteous son of Varuna won that
fine cow, who gave everything desired, as the cow for his fire offerings. She
lived in that ascetics' forest, where sages came, and wandered there without
fear, in that lovely and holy place.

"Then one day, bull of the Bharatas, the Vasus, Prithu and all the others, came
to that forest, where the gods and the divine seers came. They wandered all over
that forest with their wives, and played among the lovely mountains and woods.
There the wife of one of the Vasus, you with the courage of Indra, as she
wandered in that forest, saw the cow, the sage Vasishtha's finest cow, who gave
everything desired. The slender woman was amazed at the cow's wealth of good
qualities, and she showed her to Dyaus, you whose eyes are like a bull's:
full-uddered, a good milker, with a fine tail and a fine face, lovely, with
every virtue and a matchless nature. Long ago, joy of the Pauravas, the Vasu's
wife showed the Vasu that cow with all those virtues.

"As soon as Dyaus saw the cow, king, you with the courage of the king of
elephants, he told the goddess about her beauty and her virtues. 'This fine cow,
goddess, dark-eyed one, belongs to the son of Varuna, the seer whose forest this
is, lovely one. A mortal who drinks her sweet milk, slender one, will live ten
thousand years in lasting youth.'

"When the slender, faultless goddess heard this, best of kings, she said to her
husband, who blazed with splendour, 'I have a friend in the world of men, the
daughter of a king, named Jinavati, rich in beauty and youth, the daughter of
Ushinara, the wise royal seer, true to his word, famous in the world of men for
her beauty. For her sake, fortunate one, best of immortals, bring me that cow
and her calf, which I want. Be quick, you who make merit grow. When my friend
has drunk her milk, you who give honour, let her alone among men be free from
old age and sickness. You should do this for me, fortunate and faultless one.
There is nothing dearer to me, nothing at all.'

"When he heard the goddess's words, Dyaus, wanting to please her, stole that
cow, with his brothers, Prithu and the others. Sent by his lotus-eyed wife,
king, Dyaus could not see the seer's fierce austerity. He stole the cow, and did
not think of the fall.

"Then the son of Varuna came back to his hermitage with the fruit he had
gathered, and he did not see the cow and her calf in that fine forest. The
ascetic searched for her in the forest, and the sage of great mind, searching,
did not find the cow. With his heavenly sight he knew that the Vasus had taken
her, and at once he fell into a rage and cursed the Vasus. 'Since the Vasus have
taken my cow, my good milker with her fine tail, they will all be born among
men. There is no doubt of it.'

"So the blessed Apava, the best of sages, cursed the Vasus, bull of the
Bharatas, in the power of his anger. And when he had cursed them, the fortunate
one set his mind on austerity again. So, king, the ascetic, the brahmin seer of
great power, cursed the eight Vasu gods, in anger.

"Then, knowing that they had been cursed, the Vasus came again to the great
one's hermitage and approached the seer. They tried to win the seer's favour,
bull among kings, but they did not win it from Apava, the best of seers, tiger
among men, who knew all dharma. But the righteous one said, 'You seven, Dhara
and the rest, will be free of the curse within a year. But this one, for whose
deed I cursed you, Dyaus, will live a long time in the world of men, through his
own deed. I will not make false what I said in anger. And this great-minded one
will not have children among men. He will be righteous and skilled in all the
sciences. He will be devoted to what is good and dear to his father, and he will
give up the pleasures of women.' When he had said this to all the Vasus, the
blessed seer went away.

"Then the Vasus all came to me together, king, and asked me for a boon, and I
granted it: 'As soon as we are born, Ganga, throw us into the water yourself.'
So, best of kings, I have done rightly what was needed to free them from the
world of men, cursed as they were. By that seer's curse, best of kings, this one
alone, Dyaus, will live long in the world of men, Bharata."

When she had told him this, the goddess vanished there and then, and she took
the boy with her and went where she wished. He was named Devavrata, and also
Gangeya: the son of Shantanu had two names, and in virtue he surpassed Shantanu.

And Shantanu, stricken with grief, went back to his city. Now I will tell you
the boundless virtues of Shantanu, and the great fortune of that famous Bharata
king, whose bright history is called the Mahabharata.

**Divergences.**

* **Which Vasu, and which cow.** In the CE, the Vasu who steals the cow is **Dyaus**
  (1.93.17, 26, 37, 42), led on by his wife, with "Prithu and the others" helping
  (1.93.11, 26). The cow is not named here. Ganguli's vulgate text calls her Nandini (G 1.99). The CE does call Vasishtha's cow Nandini in a different story, Vishvamitra's attempt to take her (1.165.15–22). The popular name "Prabhasa" for the thief is in neither. See ledger
  **L-69**.

**Choices.**

* 1.91.6 *apadhyātaḥ*: Brahma "thought ill of" Mahabhisha, a curse by thought.
  Kept.
* 1.91.12 *atyabhisṛtāḥ*: the Vasus "passed by" (or slighted) Vasishtha at his
  prayers. This version of their offence differs from the story of the cow told in
  1.93. The CE has both.
* 1.91.20 *turīyārdham*: "a quarter of half", an eighth, of each Vasu's strength.
* 1.92.18: Shantanu is explained as "the child of one who was calm (*śānta*)".
  1.90.48 gave a different explanation (his youth-restoring touch). Both are kept.
* 1.92.44 *prīṇāmi tvā*: "I am pleasing (doing good to) you", said to each child.
* 1.92.54 *putraṃ pāhi*: "protect (this) son". Yet at 1.93.43 she takes him with
  her. Both are kept.
* 1.93.9 *homadhenu*: the cow whose milk is used for the fire offerings.
* 1.93.36 *dharādayaḥ*: "Dhara and the rest". 1.93.11 had "Prithu and the rest".
  The CE names the Vasus differently in the two places.
