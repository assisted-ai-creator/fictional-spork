# Agni's Hunger

Living in Indraprastha, at the command of King Dhritarashtra and of Shantanu's
son, the Pandavas defeated other kings. The whole world lived happily, relying
on the Dharma King, as an embodied soul lives in a body marked by holy deeds.
The bull of the Bharatas served dharma, pleasure and wealth equally, as a man
with kin honours three kinsmen as dear to him as himself. Those three, dharma,
wealth and pleasure, were evenly shared, as if they had bodies on earth, and the
king shone among them like a fourth. The Vedas found in him their finest
student, the great sacrifices their performer, and the classes of society a good
protector. Through that king, Lakshmi had a firm seat, wisdom its highest
refuge, and all dharma a kinsman. The king, with his four brothers, shone the
more, like a great sacrifice spread wide by the Vedas used in it. Dhaumya and
the other brahmins, the first among them equal to Brihaspati, attended him, as
the immortals attend Prajapati.

The eyes and hearts of the people delighted equally in the Dharma King, who was
as spotless as the full moon. The people did not delight in him only because
fate had made him king, but because of the kind of man he was. Whatever pleased
their hearts he did in deed. Nothing improper, nothing untrue, nothing false and
nothing unkind was ever spoken by the wise and sweet-spoken Partha. The splendid
best of the Bharatas was happy, wanting the good of the whole world, and his
own. So all the Pandavas lived gladly, free of care, and made the kings of the
earth afraid by their power.

---

Some days later Bibhatsu said to Krishna, "It is hot, Krishna. Let us go to the
Yamuna. We will enjoy ourselves there with our friends, Madhusudana, and come
back in the evening, if you like the idea, Janardana."

"Son of Kunti," said Vasudeva, "I like it too, that we should enjoy ourselves in
the water with our friends, Partha, as we please."

So, taking leave of the Dharma King, Bharata, Partha and Govinda went off with
their friends. They came to a pleasure ground, the finest, full of trees of many
kinds, with houses high and low, like the house of Purandara. It was stocked,
for Varshneya and Partha, with food and drink of every kind, rich and full of
flavour, and with garlands of many kinds. They went into it, and it was full of
fine jewels of every sort. And all the people enjoyed themselves as they liked,
Bharata.

The women of Krishna and Partha, lovely women, enjoyed themselves as the place
allowed and as they pleased, some in the woods, some in the water, some in the
houses. Draupadi and Subhadra gave costly clothes and ornaments to the women,
who were merry with drink. Some danced for joy, others shouted, and others
laughed. Some drank the finest wine, and others wept. Some struck at one
another, and others whispered secrets. The rich forest was filled with the sound
of flutes, lutes and drums, all lovely.

While this went on, the two joys of the Kurus and the Dasharhas went to a lovely
spot close by. When they reached it, king, the two great Krishnas, the
conquerors of enemy cities, sat down on costly seats. There Partha and Madhava
talked of many deeds of courage and love from the past, and enjoyed themselves.

As Vasudeva and Dhananjaya sat there happily, like the two Ashvins on the vault
of heaven, a brahmin came up to them. He was like a great sal tree, with the
glow of molten gold. He was tawny and green-gold, with a tawny beard, as broad
as he was tall. He was like the morning sun, dressed in black, with matted hair,
a face like lotus petals, and a tawny colour, and he seemed to blaze with power.
When that shining best of brahmins came near, Arjuna and Vasudeva sprang up at
once.

---

He spoke to Arjuna and to Vasudeva the Satvata, those heroes of the world, as
they stood near the Khandava forest.

"I am a brahmin who eats a great deal. I always eat without measure. I beg you,
Varshneya and Partha: give me one meal that satisfies me."

When he said this, Krishna and the Pandava answered him, "Tell us what food
would satisfy you, sir, and we will try to get it."

When the two heroes asked him what food they should prepare, the blessed one
said to them, "I do not want your food. Know that I am Agni. Give me the food
that suits me. Indra always protects this Khandava forest, and while that great
one guards it, I cannot burn it. His friend, the snake Takshaka, lives in it
always with his people, and for his sake the wielder of the thunderbolt guards
the forest. Many other creatures are protected there because of him. I want to
burn it, but I cannot, because of Shakra's power. When he sees me blazing, he
rains on me with the water of the clouds, and so I cannot burn the forest I long
to burn. Now I have found the two of you, who know weapons, to help me, and I
could burn the Khandava forest. This is the food I choose. For you two, who know
the finest weapons, will hold back those streams of water, and the creatures on
every side."

When he said this, Bibhatsu answered Agni, Jatavedas, who wanted to burn the
Khandava forest against the will of Shatakratu. "I have many heavenly weapons of
the finest kind, with which I could fight even many wielders of the thunderbolt.
But I have no bow, blessed one, to match the strength of my arms, one that could
bear my force when I strain in battle. And I need a great many arrows, arrows
that never run out, when I am shooting fast. No chariot could carry as many
arrows as I would want. I would want heavenly horses too, white and as swift as
the wind, and a chariot that roars like a cloud, as bright as the sun. So too
there is no weapon to match Krishna's strength, with which Madhava could kill
snakes and demons in battle. Blessed one, tell us the means by which the work
can be done, by which I could hold back Indra as he rains on the great forest.
Whatever is to be done by human effort, Agni, we two will do. But you must give
us, blessed one, the instruments equal to it."

---

When he was told this, the blessed Agni, with smoke for his banner, the eater of
offerings, thought of Varuna, wanting to see him: the guardian of the world, the
son of Aditi, the god who lives in the waters, the lord of the waters. Knowing
that he had been thought of, Varuna showed himself to Agni. Then Agni, with
smoke for his banner, honoured the lord of the waters, the fourth of the world's
guardians, the protector, the great lord, and said to him, "Give me quickly the
bow and the two quivers that King Soma gave you, and the chariot with the ape on
its banner. Partha will do a very great deed with Gandiva, and Vasudeva with the
discus. Give them to me for my sake."

"I will give them," Varuna answered.

Then he gave that jewel of a bow, wonderful and mighty, which made glory and
fame grow. No weapon could harm it, and it could destroy every weapon. It was
the greatest of all arms and a crusher of enemy armies. It was equal by itself
to a hundred thousand bows, and it made kingdoms grow. It was bright with
colours of every kind, smooth and without a flaw, and worshipped for endless
years by gods, danavas and gandharvas. And he gave the two great quivers that
never run out.

He gave the chariot too, yoked with heavenly horses, with the finest of apes on
its banner. It was drawn by silver horses of the gandharvas, wearing golden
garlands, like white clouds, as swift as thought or the wind. It had every piece
of equipment, and neither gods nor danavas could defeat it. It shone, it roared
aloud, and it delighted the hearts of all beings. Prajapati, the lord of the
world, had made it by his own austerity, and its form was as hard to describe as
the sun's. The lord Soma had mounted it and conquered the danavas. It looked
like a mountain or a cloud, and it seemed to blaze with glory. On that finest of
chariots stood a fine flagstaff of gold, very lovely, beautiful as Indra's bow.
On it was a heavenly monkey, with the marks of a lion and a tiger. Perched on
the top, it seemed to roar, and it looked splendid. On the banner were creatures
of many kinds, and huge, at whose roar the enemy's troops lose their wits.

Partha walked round that fine chariot, bright with many flags, keeping it on his
right, and bowed to the gods. Then, armed, in his armour, with his sword, his
leather guard and finger guards bound on, he mounted the chariot, as a man of
good deeds mounts a flying palace of the gods. When he took hold of Gandiva,
that heavenly best of bows, made by Brahma long ago, Arjuna was overjoyed. He
bowed to Agni, and then the mighty man took up the bow, gathered his strength,
and strung it. As the strong Pandava fitted the string, those who heard its
twang were shaken at heart. Having gained the chariot and the bow, and the two
great quivers that never run out, Kaunteya was glad, and ready to help.

Then Agni gave Krishna a discus with a hub like a thunderbolt, a weapon of fire,
dear to him, and he too was ready. "With this, Madhusudana," Agni said to him,
"you will defeat even beings who are not human in battle, without a doubt. With
this you will always be greater than men in battle, and than gods, rakshasas,
pishachas, daityas and snakes, without any doubt, in destroying the best of your
enemies. Every time you throw it at enemies in battle, Madhava, it will kill
them, and come back to your hand again, unchecked."

And Varuna gave him a mace that roared like thunder, a terrible weapon that
brings the daityas' end, named Kaumodaki, for Hari.

Then Krishna and the Pandava, delighted, said to Agni, "Now we are armed, we
have weapons, and chariots and banners. We are ready, blessed one, to fight even
all the gods and asuras, let alone one wielder of the thunderbolt who wants to
fight for a snake."

"When the Varshneya, the mighty one, lets fly the discus and this weapon in
battle," said Arjuna, "there is nothing in the three worlds that Janardana could
not conquer. And I too, Agni, with the bow Gandiva and the two great quivers
that never run out, am ready to conquer the worlds in battle. Surround this
forest on every side with a great blaze, lord, and set it all alight at once, as
you wish. We two are ready to help."

When the Dasharha and Arjuna said this to him, the blessed one took on his form
of fire and began to burn the forest. Surrounding it on every side, the fire of
seven flames burned the Khandava in anger, as if showing the end of an age. He
seized that forest and entered it, bull of the Bharatas, roaring like thunder,
burning up all living things. As it burned, the forest's form shone like golden
Meru, the king of mountains, with its great splendour, Bharata.

**Divergences.**

* **Why the forest burned.** In the CE, Agni wants to eat the forest, and Indra keeps him off
  for Takshaka's sake (1.215.5–10). It is not burned to clear land for the city, which was
  already built (1.199.27–36). See ledger L-107.
* **King Shvetaki.** The vulgate explains Agni's hunger with the story of King Shvetaki, whose
  endless sacrifices gave Agni a surfeit of ghee that only the forest could cure (App. I
  No. 118). The CE has no such story. See ledger L-107.
* **The banner monkey is not named.** The CE says "a heavenly monkey, with the marks of a lion
  and a tiger" (1.216.13). The vulgate names it Hanuman, son of the wind (\*2114, \*2115). The CE
  does bring Hanuman to Arjuna's banner, by his promise to Bhima in Book 3 (3.150.15). See
  ledger L-106.
* **The discus is not named.** The CE calls it "a discus with a hub like a thunderbolt". The name
  Sudarshana is only in \*2117.
