# The Wager

"So I have told you everything," said the bard, "how the nectar was churned, and
where that glorious horse of matchless speed was born.

"When Kadru saw the horse, she said to Vinata, 'What colour is Uchchaihshravas?
Tell me quickly, my dear.'

"'This king of horses is white,' said Vinata. 'What do you think, lovely one?
Tell me his colour too, and we will make a bet on it.'

"'I think this horse has a black tail, you of the bright smile,' said Kadru.
'Come, play me for it, proud lady, and let the loser be the other's slave.'

"So they made their agreement, that the loser would be a slave, and they went
home, saying, 'We will look tomorrow.'

"Then Kadru, meaning to cheat, gave an order to her thousand sons. 'Turn
yourselves into hairs as black as kohl, and go into the horse's tail quickly, so
that I do not become a slave.'

"They would not agree to what she said. So she cursed the snakes. 'When the
snake sacrifice of the wise royal seer Janamejaya, of Pandu's line, is under
way, the fire will burn you.'

"The Grandfather himself heard this curse, which Kadru had spoken, a curse far
too cruel. It was the work of fate. With all the hosts of the gods he approved
her words, for he saw how many snakes there were, and he wished the creatures of
the world well. These biters were very strong, and their poison was fierce and
deadly. Because their poison was so sharp, and for the good of creatures, he
gave the great Kashyapa the knowledge that takes poison away.

---

"When the night had passed and the sun had risen, the two sisters, Kadru and
Vinata, were angry and fierce with each other, for they had wagered slavery.
They went to look closely at the horse Uchchaihshravas.

"There they saw the ocean, the store of all the waters. It teemed with whales,
with fish and with the great beasts that swallow whales. Crocodiles filled it,
and many thousands of creatures of every shape. Fierce creatures lived there, so
that no one could ever safely enter it, and it swarmed with tortoises and
sharks.

"It was the mine of every jewel, Varuna's home, and the lovely home of the
nagas. It was the lord of rivers, the dwelling of the fire below the world and
the prison of the asuras, terrible to living things, the store of the waters. It
was bright and divine, the great source of the immortals' nectar. No one could
measure it or even imagine it, and its waters were holy and wonderful.

"It was dreadful, fierce with the cries of the creatures in it, and its roar was
frightening. It was full of deep whirlpools, and it terrified every creature.
The wind at the shore rocked it back and forth, and it rose up in restless
turmoil, and its waves moved everywhere like hands, so that it seemed to be
dancing. As the moon waxed and waned, its waves swelled high and no one could
come near it. It had given birth to the conch Panchajanya, and it was the finest
of all the mines of jewels.

"Its waters had been churned and muddied to their depths when the blessed
Govinda, whose power has no measure, took the form of a boar and went looking
for the earth. The brahmin seer Atri practised austerity for a hundred years and
could not find its bottom. Its floor is the world below, and it never wastes
away. It is the bed where Vishnu, whose splendour has no measure, whose navel
bears a lotus, lies in the sleep of yoga at the beginning of each age. It is the
blessed giver of water, as an offering, to the blazing fire of the Mare's Mouth.
It is the lord of rivers, bottomless and boundless, wide beyond measure.

"They saw the great ocean, and many great rivers by the thousand hurrying into
it without rest, as if racing each other. They saw it deep and full of whales
and fierce sea monsters, roaring with the dreadful cries of the creatures in the
water, vast and bright as the sky, bottomless, the endless store of the waters.
Looking on that ocean, full of fish and sea monsters and waves, deep and
spreading, bright as the sky and lit by the flames of the fire below the world,
the two women flew on quickly.

---

"Kadru crossed that ocean with Vinata, and flew fast, and soon came down close
to the horse. She saw many black hairs clinging to its tail. And she put Vinata,
whose face had fallen, into slavery. So Vinata lost the wager. She lived as a
slave, burning with sorrow.

"Meanwhile, when his time came, Garuda was born. He split the egg without his
mother there, and he blazed with power. He shone like a mass of fire, kindled
and terrifying. The bird grew huge all at once, with a vast body, and rose into
the sky.

"When all creatures saw him, they went to Agni, the fire, for refuge. They bowed
to him where he sat, in his form that holds all forms, and said to him, 'Agni,
do not grow any more! Do you mean to burn us up? For that great mass of yours,
all ablaze, is spreading.'

"'It is not what you think, you crushers of the asuras,' said Agni. 'This is
Garuda, the mighty. He is my equal in splendour.'

"When he had told them this, the gods and the hosts of seers went close to
Garuda and praised him.

"'You are a seer, greatly blessed. You are a god, the lord of birds. You are the
master, bright as the sun. You are our highest protection. You are a sea of
strength. You are good and undaunted, rich in power, and hard to withstand. You
whose fame never fails, you are all austerity and all sacred learning, all that
is to come and all that has come.

"'You are the highest. You light up this whole world, moving and unmoving, with
your rays, like the sun. Again and again you outshine the light of the sun
itself. You are the end of all this world, of what lasts and what passes. As the
angry sun would burn all creatures, so you burn them, bright as the fire. You
are terrible, like the fire that rises at the end of the world, destroying,
bringing all to an end when the ages turn.

"'We have come to you for refuge, lord of birds, mighty one, you who are free of
darkness and move among the clouds. We have come to mighty Garuda, who flies in
the sky, the highest and the lowest, the giver of boons, whose courage no one
can overcome.'

"When the gods and the hosts of seers had praised him like this, Suparna, the
bird of the beautiful wings, drew his splendour back into himself.

---

"Then the bird, great in courage and strength, who could go wherever he wished,
went to his mother on the far shore of the great ocean. There Vinata, beaten in
the wager, had become a slave, and she was bitterly unhappy.

"Then one day, at the proper time, Kadru called Vinata, who was bowing low where
her son was near, and said this. 'My good woman, there is a lovely home of the
nagas, a charming place, in a lonely spot in the ocean's belly. Carry me there,
Vinata.'

"So Suparna's mother carried the mother of the snakes. And Garuda, urged by his
mother, carried the snakes. The son of Vinata, the bird, flew up towards the
sun, and the snakes, caught in the sun's rays, fainted.

"When Kadru saw her sons in that state, she praised Indra.

"'Homage to you, lord of the gods of gods! Homage to you, slayer of Bala! Slayer
of Namuchi, homage to you, thousand-eyed, husband of Shachi! Be a raft of water
for the snakes the sun is burning. You are our highest protection, best of the
immortals. You have the power to pour out water in plenty, Purandara, breaker of
cities. You are the cloud, you are the wind, you are the fire of the lightning
in the sky. You scatter the massed clouds, and they say you are the massed cloud
itself. You are the matchless, terrible thunderbolt. You are the roaring storm
cloud.

"'You make the worlds, and you destroy them, and no one conquers you. You are
the light of all beings. You are the sun, the bright one. You are the great
wonder among beings. You are the king, you are the best of the gods. You are
Vishnu, you are the thousand-eyed, you are the god, you are the last refuge. You
are all the nectar, god. You are the soma, honoured above all. You are the hour,
the day of the moon, the moment and the instant. You are the bright half of the
month and the dark half, and the smallest measures of time. You are the year and
the seasons, the months, the nights and the days.

"'You are the fair earth, with her mountains and forests, and the sky, with its
sun, free of darkness. You are the great ocean with its whales and the beasts
that swallow whales, its great waves and its many sea monsters, the home of the
fish. You are great glory. Wise, great seers always worship you with glad
hearts. Praised at the sacrifice, you drink the soma, and the offerings made
with the cry of *Vashat*, for the good of the world. Brahmins worship you here
always, to win the fruits of it, and you are sung in the limbs of the Veda, you
flood of matchless strength. It is for your sake that the best of the brahmins,
given to sacrifice, learn the limbs of the Veda along with all the Vedas.'

---

"When Kadru had praised him like this, the blessed Indra, whose horses are bay,
covered the whole sky with masses of dark clouds. The clouds poured down water
in floods, bright with lightning, thundering all the time in the sky as if at
one another. The sky seemed packed with vast and wonderful clouds, pouring out
water beyond measure without a pause, with a great roar. The sky seemed to dance
with the waves of the downpour, and it filled with the rolling of the thunder.
As Indra rained, the joy of the nagas was very great, and the earth filled with
water on every side.

"Carried by Suparna, the snakes soon came to that place. It was ringed by the
waters of the ocean and loud with flocks of birds. Lines of forest covered it,
with fruits and flowers of every colour, and lovely houses, and lotus pools. It
was bright with lakes of clear water, and pure breezes fanned it, carrying
heavenly scents. Sandalwood trees that seemed to reach up to the sky made it
beautiful. When the wind shook them, they let fall showers of flowers, as if
they were raining flowers on the nagas there. It was a holy place that made the
heart glad, dear to the gandharvas and the apsarases, lovely with the songs of
many birds, and it delighted Kadru's sons.

"The snakes came to that forest and played there happily. Then they said to
Suparna, the mighty, the best of birds, 'Carry us to another island, a lovely
one with plenty of water. You fly, sky-traveller, and you see many lovely
lands.'

"The bird thought it over, and then he said to his mother, Vinata, 'Mother, why
must I do what the snakes tell me?'

"'Best of birds,' said Vinata, 'I have become the slave of my sister, who is not
noble. The snakes played a trick, and made the wager false.'

"When his mother had told him the reason, the sky-traveller was pained by her
pain, and he spoke to the snakes. 'What must I bring, or what must I learn, or
what brave deed must I do here, to be freed from slavery to you? Tell me the
truth, you flickering tongues.'

"When they heard him, the snakes said, 'Bring the nectar of immortality by your
own strength. Then you will be freed from slavery, sky-traveller.'"

**Divergences.**

* **Who made the black hairs?** In the CE the snakes refuse Kadru's order
  (1.18.7), yet the sisters then see "many black hairs" on the tail (1.20.2),
  and Vinata later blames "the snakes' trick" (1.23.9). The CE never explains
  the gap. The popular telling, in which Karkotaka becomes the hair, is a
  rejected passage (\*286). See ledger **L-65**. The novel narrates what the
  CE says, in its order, and adds nothing.
* **Brahma's words to Kashyapa** about the snakes (\*285) are rejected, and
  are not used.

**Choices.**

* **Garuda's birth told again.** 1.20.4 narrates the birth a second time
  (see chapter 8, 1.14.22). The novel keeps it.
* 1.18.9 *daivād atīva*: the curse was spoken "greatly through fate". Rendered
  "It was the work of fate."
* 1.18.11 *kāśyapāya*: the CE says only "Kashyapa". It does not say whether
  this is the snakes' father or the brahmin healer Kashyapa of the Parikshit
  story (1.38–39). Left as it is.
* 1.19.6 *pātālajvalana*: the fire of the world below; 1.19.14
  *vaḍavāmukha*: the fire of the Mare's Mouth. These are kept as the text's
  images.
* 1.19.11 *gāṃ vindatā … varāharūpiṇā*: Govinda in the form of a boar,
  "finding the earth". The CE names the boar form here, and the novel says
  no more than that.
* 1.20.6–8: "all creatures" go to Agni, but Agni addresses them as "crushers of
  the asuras", and 1.20.9 says the gods and seers then praise Garuda. The
  novel keeps the text's wording.
* 1.21.3 *pravaṇāṃ putrasaṃnidhau*: Vinata "bowing low, near her son". The
  exact force of *pravaṇā* (humble, submissive) is kept as "bowing low".
* 1.21.14 *kalā kāṣṭhā truṭiḥ*: small units of time. Rendered "the smallest
  measures of time".
* 1.23.11 *lelihāḥ*: "flickers" (tongues), a name for snakes. Rendered "you
  flickering tongues".
* Kohl: *añjana*, the black eye-paint (1.18.6).
