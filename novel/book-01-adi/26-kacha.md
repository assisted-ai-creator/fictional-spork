# Kacha

Now, sinless one, I will tell you the line of Daksha the lord of creatures, and
of Manu the son of Vivasvat, of Bharata, Kuru, Puru and Ajamidha: the line of
the Yadavas and of the Pauravas, all of it, and of the Bharatas. It is holy and
brings great blessing, and it brings wealth, fame and long life.

The ten sons of Prachetas, all shining with splendour, as bright as great seers,
are remembered as the good men of old. Long ago those mighty ones were burned by
a fire born of the clouds. From them was born Daksha, son of the Prachetases,
and from Daksha these creatures came, tiger among men, for he is the grandfather
of the world. The sage Daksha lay with Virini and fathered a thousand sons like
himself, of strict vows. Narada taught Daksha's thousand sons together the lore
of release, the matchless knowledge of Samkhya. Then Daksha, the lord of
creatures, wanting to make creatures, Janamejaya, appointed fifty daughters to
bear sons for him. He gave ten to Dharma, thirteen to Kashyapa, and
twenty-seven, who are set to measure time, to the Moon.

The finest of Kashyapa's thirteen wives was a daughter of Daksha, and on her
Kashyapa, the son of Marichi, fathered the Adityas, Indra first among them, full
of courage, and Vivasvat. Vivasvat's son was the lord Yama, son of Vivasvat. And
Martanda was born as Yama's son, king. Martanda's son was the wise lord Manu,
and from him came the line of Manu's people, the human race. From that Manu were
born men, brahmins, kshatriyas and the rest. Then, king, the brahmin joined with
the kshatriya, and the brahmins among Manu's people kept the Veda with its
limbs.

They name Manu's mighty sons, devoted to the dharma of kshatriyas: Vena,
Dhrishnu, Narishyanta, Nabhaga and Ikshvaku, Karusha and Sharyati, and Ila the
eighth, Prishadhra the ninth, and Nabhagarishta the tenth. Manu had fifty other
sons on earth as well. They all perished by quarrelling among themselves, so we
have heard.

Then wise Pururavas was born of Ila. She was both his mother and his father, so
we have heard. Pururavas enjoyed thirteen islands of the ocean, a man of great
fame surrounded by beings who were not human. Drunk with his own courage,
Pururavas quarrelled with the brahmins, and he carried off their jewels even as
they cried out. Sanatkumara came from the world of Brahma, king, and gave him
counsel, but he would not take it. Then the angry great seers cursed him, and he
perished at once, a lord of men full of greed, his mind lost in the pride of his
strength. It was he who, in the world of the gandharvas, with Urvashi, the lord,
brought down the three fires set out for the rites.

Six sons were born to Aila by Urvashi: Ayus, Dhimat, Amavasu, Dridhayus, Vanayus
and Shrutayus. Nahusha, Vriddhasharman, Raji, Rambha and Anenas: these they call
the sons of Ayus by Svarbhanavi. Ayus's son Nahusha was wise and truly brave.
That lord of the earth ruled a very great kingdom by dharma. Nahusha protected
the forefathers, the gods, the seers and the brahmins, the gandharvas, snakes
and rakshasas, the brahmins, kshatriyas and vaishyas. He killed the hordes of
robbers and made the seers pay him tribute, and the mighty king made them carry
him on their backs like beasts. He overcame the dwellers of heaven by his
splendour, his austerity, his courage and his strength, and he made himself
Indra.

Nahusha fathered six sons on Priyavasas: Yati, Yayati, Samyati, Ayati, Pancha
and Uddhava. Yayati, the son of Nahusha, was an emperor of true courage. He
protected the earth and offered many kinds of sacrifice. Always devout, he
honoured the forefathers and the gods with all his strength, and unconquered
Yayati was kind to all his people. His sons, great archers with every virtue,
were born to Devayani and Sharmishtha, great king. Yadu and Turvasu were born to
Devayani, and Druhyu, Anu and Puru to Sharmishtha.

Yayati protected his people by dharma for years without end, king. Then the son
of Nahusha came to old age, most terrible, the destroyer of beauty. Overcome by
old age, the king spoke to his sons, Yadu, Puru, Turvasu, Druhyu and Anu,
Bharata. "I want to be young, and take my pleasure with young women, enjoying
life in my youth. Help me, my sons."

Yadu, his eldest son, the son of Devayani, said to him, "What must we do? What
do you want with our youth?"

"Take my old age," Yayati said to him, "and with your youth I will enjoy the
world. I was offering long sacrifices, and by the curse of the sage Ushanas I
lost the enjoyment of my desires. It pains me, my sons. Let one of you rule the
kingdom with my body, and let me be young, and have my desires, with a new
body."

Yadu and the others would not take his old age. Then Puru, the youngest, true in
his courage, said to him, "King, go about in a new body, as a young man. I will
take your old age and stay in the kingdom at your command."

When he said this, the royal seer, by the power of his austerity, passed his old
age to his great-souled son. With Puru's youth, the king became young again, and
Puru ruled the kingdom with Yayati's age. After a thousand years, unconquered
Yayati, still not satisfied in his desires, said to his son Puru, "Through you I
have an heir. You are the son who will carry on my line. It will be famous in
the world as the Paurava line, after you."

Then that tiger among kings consecrated Puru as king, and a long time afterwards
he went the way of Time.

---

"Yayati was our forefather," said Janamejaya, "the tenth from the lord of
creatures. How did he win Shukra's daughter, who was so very hard to win? I want
to hear this in full, best of brahmins. And tell me one by one, in order, those
who carried on Puru's line."

Yayati was a royal seer, as bright as the king of the gods. Shukra and
Vrishaparvan both chose him, as happened long ago. I will tell you, Janamejaya,
since you ask, how Yayati, the son of Nahusha, came to be joined with Devayani.

There was a struggle between the gods and the asuras for power over the three
worlds, with all that moves and all that does not. To win, the gods chose the
sage Angirasa, Brihaspati, as their priest, to perform their sacrifices, and the
others chose Kavya Ushanas, Shukra. Those two brahmins were always fierce
rivals.

The danavas that the gods killed when they met them in battle, Kavya brought
back to life by the power of his knowledge, and they rose again and fought the
gods. But the gods that the asuras killed at the front of the battle,
Brihaspati, for all his great mind, did not bring back to life. For he did not
know the knowledge that mighty Kavya knew, the knowledge of bringing back to
life. The gods fell into deep despair.

Frightened of Kavya Ushanas, the gods went to Kacha, the eldest son of
Brihaspati, and said to him, "We are devoted to you, so be devoted to us, and do
us the greatest help. Fetch quickly the knowledge that lives in the brahmin
Shukra, whose splendour has no measure, and you will have a share with us. You
can see that brahmin near Vrishaparvan. There he protects the danavas. He does
not protect those who are not danavas. You are young, and you can win over the
seer, and his dear daughter, Devayani. You can win them over, and no one else,
with your good character, your courtesy and sweetness, your conduct and your
self-control. When Devayani is pleased, you will surely get that knowledge."

"So be it," said Kacha, the son of Brihaspati, and, honoured by the gods, he set
out for Vrishaparvan's court. Sent by the gods, king, Kacha went quickly, and in
the city of the king of the asuras he saw Shukra, and said, "I am the grandson
of the seer Angiras and the son of Brihaspati himself. My name is Kacha. Take me
as your pupil. I will live as a celibate student with you, my highest teacher.
Allow me this, brahmin, for a thousand years."

"Kacha, you are most welcome," said Shukra. "I accept your words. I will honour
you, for you deserve honour, and so Brihaspati will be honoured."

Kacha said, "So be it," and he took the vow that Shukra Ushanas, the son of
Kavi, had set him. He took on the vow for the time it had to last, as it was
said, and he served his teacher and Devayani, Bharata. Always meaning to please
her, the young man in his youth sang and danced and played music, and he made
Devayani glad. He waited on Devayani, a girl now grown to her youth, with
flowers and fruit and errands, and he made her happy, Bharata. And Devayani,
singing along with him, lovely girl, waited on that brahmin, who kept his rules
and his vows, in private.

So five hundred years went by as Kacha kept his vow. Then the danavas found out
about Kacha. They saw him alone, in a lonely place in the forest, guarding the
cows, and in their anger they killed him, out of hatred for Brihaspati and to
keep the knowledge safe. When they had killed him, they cut him up as small as
sesame seeds and gave him to the jackals.

Then the cows came home without their herdsman. When Devayani saw the cows come
back from the forest without Kacha, she spoke to her father, Bharata.

"Your evening fire offering has not been made, and the sun has set, my lord. The
cows have come back without their herdsman. Kacha is nowhere to be seen, father.
He must have been killed, father, or died. I cannot live without Kacha. I tell
you truly."

"I will call out, 'Come here,'" said Shukra, "and bring the dead man back to
life."

Then he used the knowledge of bringing back to life and called Kacha, and at the
call Kacha appeared, whole, by the power of the knowledge. When the brahmin's
daughter asked him, he told her, "I was killed."

Again, at Devayani's word, the brahmin went into the forest, as it happened, to
gather flowers, and the danavas saw him. Then they killed him a second time,
burned him and ground him to powder, and the asuras gave him to the brahmin
himself, in wine.

Then Devayani spoke to her father again. "Kacha went to gather flowers for me,
on an errand, father. He is nowhere to be seen."

"Daughter," said Shukra, "Brihaspati's son Kacha has gone the way of the dead.
Even when he is brought back to life by my knowledge, he is killed like this.
What can I do? Do not grieve so, Devayani. Do not cry. A woman like you should
not mourn for a mortal. The gods, all of them, and the whole world bow to the
change that comes."

"His grandfather is Angiras, the most ancient," said Devayani, "and his father
is Brihaspati, rich in austerity. He is the son and grandson of seers. How can I
not grieve for him? How can I not cry? He was a celibate student, rich in
austerity, always ready, and skilled in all his work. I will follow Kacha's
path. I will not eat. For handsome Kacha is dear to me, father."

"Surely the asuras hate me," said Shukra, "when they kill my pupil, who has done
no wrong. They are fierce, and they want to make me no brahmin at all, as the
danavas have plainly set out to do. There must be an end to this evil. Whom
would the killing of a brahmin not burn, even Indra?"

Urged on by Devayani, the great seer Kavya called again, in fury, to Kacha, the
son of Brihaspati. Called by the knowledge, but afraid of his teacher, Kacha
spoke softly from inside his belly.

"By what path were you brought here, to stand in my belly?" said Shukra. "Tell
me, brahmin."

"By your grace my memory has not left me," said Kacha. "I remember everything,
how it happened and as it happened. But my austerity must not be wasted, and so
I bear this terrible pain. The asuras killed me, burned me and ground me to
powder, and gave me to you in wine, Kavya. How could the magic of the asuras
overcome the magic of a brahmin, while you are here?"

"My child," said Shukra, "what can I do to please you now? If Kacha lives, I
die. Kacha, who is inside me, Devayani, cannot be seen except by splitting open
my belly."

"Two griefs, like fire, will burn me," said Devayani: "Kacha's death, and your
death. If Kacha dies, there is no peace for me. If you die, I cannot live."

"You have won what you sought, son of Brihaspati," said Shukra, "since Devayani
loves you as you love her. Take this knowledge of bringing back to life, unless
you are Indra in Kacha's form. No one else would come out of my belly alive, but
only a brahmin. So take the knowledge. Become my son, you whom I have brought
back to life, and bring me back to life when you have come out of this body, my
child. Take the knowledge from your teacher, and then, with the knowledge, look
on me with the eyes of dharma."

The brahmin took the knowledge from his teacher, split open his belly, and came
out, handsome Kacha, from the right side of the brahmin, like the moon at the
full moon at the end of the bright fortnight. And when Kacha saw that heap of
holiness lying dead, he raised him up too, with the knowledge he had won. Kacha
bowed to his teacher, and said, "The giver of the matchless truth, the treasure
of the four treasures: those who do not honour such a teacher, who deserves
honour, go to evil worlds where there is no footing."

Kavya Ushanas, the great one, had been deceived because he drank wine, and it
had taken away his senses, which was most terrible. He saw handsome Kacha, whom
he had drunk in the wine in his stupor. He rose in anger, wanting to do good to
the brahmins, and suspicious now of drinking wine, he himself spoke these words.

"Any brahmin who, from this day on, drinks wine here out of folly, in his
dullness, will have fallen from dharma, and will be a killer of brahmins. He
will be scorned in this world and in the next. I have set this bound, this limit
to the dharma of brahmins, in all the world. Let good brahmins, those who serve
their teachers, hear it, and the gods and all the worlds."

When he had said this, that great one, the treasure of all the treasures of
austerity, beyond all measure, called the danavas, their minds bewildered by
fate, and said, "I tell you, danavas, you are fools. Kacha has won what he
sought, and he will live with me. He has won the knowledge of bringing back to
life, of such great worth. He is equal in power to Brahma, and he has become
Brahman."

Kacha lived with his teacher a thousand years. Then, with his teacher's leave,
he made ready to go back to the home of the thirty gods.
