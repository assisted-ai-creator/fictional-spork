# The Churning of the Ocean

"Why did King Janamejaya, that tiger among kings, try to put an end to the
snakes with his snake sacrifice?" asked Shaunaka. "Tell me. And why did Astika,
the best of brahmins and the best of those who pray, save the snakes from that
blazing fire? Whose son was the king who held the snake sacrifice? And whose son
was that best of brahmins? Tell me."

"All this is told in the great story of Astika," said the bard. "Listen, and I
will tell you all of it."

"I want to hear the whole of this lovely tale," said Shaunaka, "the story of the
ancient and famous brahmin Astika."

"The elders who live in the Naimisha forest call this history ancient," said
Ugrashravas. "Krishna Dvaipayana told it. My father, the suta Lomaharshana, the
wise pupil of Vyasa, once told it when the brahmins asked him. I heard it from
him, and I will tell you this story of Astika exactly as it is, Shaunaka, since
you ask.

"Astika's father was a lord like the creator himself. He was a celibate student,
sparing in his food, and always given to fierce austerity. His name was
Jaratkaru. He was a great seer who never spilled his seed, the best of the
Yayavaras, a wandering order, and he knew dharma and kept strict vows.

"Once, as he wandered, he saw his own forefathers hanging head down in a great
pit, their feet in the air. As soon as he saw them, Jaratkaru spoke to them.

"'Who are you, hanging head down in this pit? You are clinging to a clump of
virana grass, and a rat hidden in the pit, who lives there always, has gnawed it
all round.'

"'We are the Yayavaras, seers of strict vows,' they said. 'Our line is dying
out, brahmin, and so we are sinking down into the earth. We have one descendant
left, called Jaratkaru, an unlucky son of unlucky fathers. He cares for nothing
but austerity. The fool does not want to take a wife and have sons. So we hang
in this pit, because our line is dying out. He is our protector, and yet we have
no protection, as if we were wrongdoers. Who are you, good man, who grieve for
us as if you were our kin? We want to know who you are, brahmin, standing here.
Why do you pity us, who are to be pitied?'

"'You are my forefathers, my fathers and grandfathers,' said Jaratkaru. 'Tell me
what I should do now. I myself am Jaratkaru.'

"'Try, son, and try hard, to keep our family going,' they said, 'for your own
sake and for ours, lord. That is dharma. Not by the fruits of dharma, son, and
not by austerities, however great the store of them, do people reach the place
that those who have sons reach. So make the effort to take a wife, and set your
mind on children, son. We command it. This is the greatest good you can do us.'

"'I will not take a wife,' said Jaratkaru. 'I have always been set on that. But
for your good I will take one, on one condition, and by the proper rites. If I
find a wife like this, I will marry her, and not otherwise. She must have the
same name as I do, and her kin must wish to give her. Then I will marry that
girl by the rules, as if she were alms. For who will give his daughter to a poor
man like me? But if someone gives her, I will take her as alms. This is how I
will set about marrying, forefathers, and I will never do it any other way. And
from her a child will be born who will save you. Let my forefathers reach a
place that lasts for ever, and be glad.'

"So that brahmin of strict vows wandered the earth to marry, looking for a wife,
and did not find one. One day he went into a forest, remembering what his
forefathers had said, and he called out softly three times, asking for a girl as
alms.

"Vasuki, the king of the snakes, came forward with his sister and offered her to
him. But Jaratkaru did not accept her, thinking, *She does not have my name.*
For the great Jaratkaru's mind was fixed: *I will take a wife who is offered to
me, and who has my name.*

"The wise Jaratkaru, great in austerity, said, 'What is your sister's name? Tell
me the truth, snake.'

"'Jaratkaru,' said Vasuki, 'this younger sister of mine is Jaratkaru. I have
kept her for you all this time. Take her, best of brahmins.'

"For long before, best of those who know Brahman, the snakes had been cursed by
their own mother: 'The fire, whose charioteer is the wind, will burn you at
Janamejaya's sacrifice.' To quiet that curse, the chief of the snakes gave his
sister to that ascetic seer of good vows. Jaratkaru took her by the rites the
scriptures lay down, and a son named Astika was born to her from that great man.
He was an ascetic and a great soul, and he knew the Vedas and their limbs
through and through. He treated the whole world alike, and he took away the fear
of both his father's family and his mother's.

"Then, after a long time, a king descended from Pandu held the great sacrifice
known as the snake sacrifice. So we have heard. When that sacrifice was under
way, to destroy the snakes, the famous Astika set them free from the curse. He
saved the nagas, his mother's brothers, and all his other kin. He saved his
father's forefathers too, by giving them descendants, and by his austerity.
Through his many vows and his study, he paid all his debts, brahmin. He
satisfied the gods with sacrifices and many gifts to the priests. He satisfied
the seers by his celibate study. And he satisfied the forefathers with children.

"So Jaratkaru, strict in his vows, took the heavy burden off his forefathers and
went to heaven with them. The sage Jaratkaru, having won a son, Astika, and the
highest dharma, went to heaven after a very long time.

"That is the story of Astika, as I have told it rightly. Tell me, tiger of the
Bhrigus, what more shall I tell?"

---

"Sauti," said Shaunaka, "tell us this story again, in full: the story of the
wise and good Astika. We are longing to hear it. You tell it sweetly, my friend,
with smooth words and syllables, and we are delighted. You speak as your father
spoke. Your father was always ready to serve us. Tell this story the way your
father told it."

"I will tell you this story of Astika, which brings long life," said the bard,
"just as I heard it from my father.

"Long ago, brahmin, in the age of the gods, there were two sisters, daughters of
the creator, lovely, wonderful and without fault. They were the wives of
Kashyapa, and their names were Kadru and Vinata. Their husband, who was like the
creator himself, was delighted with his two lawful wives, and he offered them
each a boon. When they heard that Kashyapa would give them a boon, a very great
one, the two fine women were filled with a joy past all comparing.

"Kadru chose to have a thousand nagas as her sons, all equal in splendour.
Vinata chose to have two sons, stronger than Kadru's sons, greater than they in
strength, in splendour and in courage. Her husband gave her the boon of one son
and a half, as she wished, and Vinata said to Kashyapa, 'So be it.'

"Vinata had what she wanted: two sons of greater strength. And Kadru had what
she wanted: a thousand sons of equal splendour. 'Carry the children with care,'
said Kashyapa, the great ascetic, and he went away into the forest, leaving his
two wives happy with their boons.

"After a long time, Kadru laid a thousand eggs, brahmin, and Vinata laid two.
Their serving women, delighted, put the eggs in warm, moist jars, and there they
lay for five hundred years. When the five hundred years were over, Kadru's sons
came out. But from Vinata's two eggs, no children could be seen.

"Vinata, the ascetic goddess, longed for a son, and she was ashamed. She broke
one of the eggs, and there she saw a son. The upper half of his body was fully
formed, and the rest was not yet there. So we have heard. The son was angry, and
he cursed her.

"'Mother, you were greedy, and so you have made me like this, with only half a
body. So you will be the slave of the woman you compete with, for five hundred
years. And this other son, mother, will free you from slavery, if you do not
break his egg too, as you broke mine, and leave him with no body, or maimed,
this ascetic son. If you want a son of great strength, you must have patience,
and wait more than five hundred years for his birth.'

"Having cursed Vinata, the son went up into the sky. He is Aruna, brahmin, whom
you can always see at the time of dawn.

"And Garuda too was born when his time came, the destroyer of snakes. As soon as
he was born, he left Vinata and flew up into the sky, hungry, eager to eat, to
take the food that the Maker had set aside for him, tiger of the Bhrigus.

---

"At this time the two sisters saw the horse Uchchaihshravas coming near them,
ascetic. All the hosts of the gods had honoured him with joy. He was the jewel
of horses, the finest, born when the nectar of immortality was churned: strong
as a great flood, the best of horses, the fastest of all that run, glorious,
ageless and divine, with every sign of good fortune."

"How did the gods churn the nectar?" asked Shaunaka. "And where? Tell me, where
that king of horses was born, so strong and so bright."

"There is a mountain," said the bard, "Meru, blazing and unshakeable, a heap of
light beyond compare. Its golden peaks outshine the sun. It is adorned with
gold, bright with colours, and the gods and gandharvas go there. It is beyond
measuring, and men who live in wrongdoing cannot come near it. Fierce beasts
roam it, and divine herbs light it up. It is a great mountain that stands so
high it covers the vault of heaven. No one else can reach it even in the mind.
It has rivers and trees, and it rings with the lovely songs of flocks of birds
of every kind.

"All the gods, in their great power, climbed up onto its bright back, which was
studded with jewels and rose up beyond all measure. They sat there together,
firm in austerity and self-control, and began to take counsel about the nectar
of immortality.

"While the gods were thinking and talking on every side, the god Narayana spoke
to Brahma.

"'Let the gods and the hosts of asuras churn the ocean, as if it were a great
pot. When the great ocean is churned, the nectar will come out of it. Gods,
throw in all the herbs and all the jewels, and churn the ocean. Then you will
find the nectar.'

"There was a mountain called Mandara, the best of mountains, adorned with peaks
like the tops of clouds and covered in nets of creepers. Birds of every kind
sang on it, and fanged beasts of every kind crowded it. The kinnaras, the
apsarases, and the gods themselves went there. It rose eleven thousand yojanas
high, and as many thousand went down below the earth.

"The gods, all of them together, could not lift it. So they went to Vishnu, who
sat with Brahma, and said, 'You two must find the best way for us. Make the
effort to lift Mandara, for our good.'

"'So be it,' said Vishnu, and Brahma with him, son of Bhrigu. Then Ananta rose
up. Brahma urged him to it, and Narayana gave him the task, and he was mighty.
He tore up by force that king of mountains, with its forests and all that lived
in them.

"The gods went with him to the ocean and said to it, 'We are going to churn your
waters for the nectar.'

"The lord of the waters answered, 'Then I must have a share of it too, for I
will bear the great crushing when Mandara whirls round.'

"The gods and the asuras said to Akupara, the king of the tortoises, 'You must
be the base for this mountain.'

"'So be it,' said the tortoise, and he offered his back. And Indra pressed down
the top of the mountain with a machine.

"So they made Mandara the churning stick and Vasuki the rope, and the gods began
to churn the ocean, the store of all the waters, for the nectar, brahmin. And
with them churned the daityas and the danavas, the tribes of the asuras. The
great asuras held one end of the snake king, and all the gods stood together at
the end where the tail was. The blessed god Ananta stood where Narayana was, and
again and again he lifted the snake's head and flung it down.

"As the gods jerked Vasuki hard, winds full of smoke and flame burst from his
mouth, again and again. The smoke gathered into clouds full of lightning, and
they rained on the hosts of gods, who were worn out with toil and heat. And from
the mountain's high peaks, flowers came showering down, and covered the gods and
the asuras with garlands on every side.

"The ocean, churned with Mandara by the gods and the asuras, gave out a great
roar, like the roar of great thunderclouds. Many kinds of water creatures were
crushed by the great mountain, and by the hundreds they perished in the salt
sea. The mountain destroyed many of Varuna's creatures of every kind, who lived
in the depths below the earth.

"As the mountain whirled, great trees full of birds rubbed against each other
and fell from its summit. The rubbing kindled a fire that flared up again and
again, and it wrapped Mandara in flame, the way lightning wraps a dark cloud. It
burned the elephants and the lions that fled from it, and every other kind of
creature lost its life. Indra, the best of the immortals, put the fire out
everywhere with water from the clouds.

"Then many kinds of resin from the great trees, and the juices of herbs, ran
down into the water of the ocean. By the milk of those juices, which had the
power of the nectar, and by the melted gold that flowed with them, the gods
became immortal. The ocean water turned to milk, mixed with the finest of those
juices, and from the milk came butter.

"Then the gods went to Brahma, the giver of boons, where he sat, and said,
'Brahma, we are worn out, and still the nectar does not come. The daityas and
the great snakes are worn out too, all of us but the god Narayana. And the
churning of the ocean has gone on for so long.'

"Brahma spoke to the god Narayana. 'Give them strength, Vishnu. You are our last
refuge here.'

"'I give strength to all who have taken up this work,' said Vishnu. 'Let
everyone stir the pot. Let Mandara be turned.'

"When they heard Narayana's words, they were strong, and all together they
churned the milk of the great ocean hard again, and threw it into turmoil. Then
the moon rose out of the ocean, cool-rayed and clear-shining, like a second sun
with a hundred thousand rays. After it, Shri rose from the butter, the goddess
of fortune, dressed in white. The goddess Sura rose, the goddess of wine, and a
white horse. The divine jewel Kaustubha rose, born from the nectar, glorious and
radiant, and it went to rest on Narayana's chest. Shri and Sura and the Moon,
and the horse as swift as thought, went to where the gods were, taking the path
of the sun.

"Then the god Dhanvantari rose in bodily form, carrying a white water pot, and
in it was the nectar of immortality.

"When the danavas saw that great wonder, a great uproar rose among them over the
nectar. 'It's mine!' they shouted.

"Then Narayana, the lord, used his power of illusion, a power that bewitches. He
took the form of a wonderful woman and went among the danavas. The danavas and
the daityas were bewildered, and their minds were all on her, and they gave the
nectar to that woman.

---

"Then the daityas and danavas took up their best armour and all kinds of
weapons, and together they attacked the gods. And mighty Vishnu, with Nara,
carried the nectar away from the lords of the danavas. Then all the gods drank
the nectar, taking it from Vishnu, in the uproar and confusion.

"While the gods were drinking the nectar they had longed for, a danava named
Rahu took the form of a god and drank it too. When the nectar had reached the
danava's throat, the Moon and the Sun told what he had done, wishing the gods
well. Then, with his might, the blessed one who carries the discus cut off
Rahu's head, with all its ornaments, while he was drinking the nectar. The
danava's head, huge as a mountain peak, cut off by the discus, fell and shook
the earth. Since then, Rahu's mouth has held a lasting feud with the Moon and
the Sun, and to this day he swallows them.

"Hari, the blessed one, gave up his matchless woman's form, and with terrible
weapons of many kinds he made the danavas tremble. Then near the salt sea a
great battle began between the gods and the asuras, the most terrible of all
battles. Huge sharp darts fell by the thousand, and sharp-pointed lances, and
weapons of every kind. The asuras, cut by the discus, fell to the ground
vomiting blood, struck down by swords and spears and maces. Heads cut off by
battle-axes, with nets of burnished gold on them, fell ceaselessly in that cruel
fight. The great asuras lay dead, their bodies smeared with blood, like mountain
peaks red with ore. On every side, by thousands, rose cries of pain, as they
hacked at each other with their weapons and the sun turned red. They killed each
other with iron clubs, and when they came close, with their fists, and the noise
seemed to touch the sky. 'Cut! Pierce! Run! Bring him down! At him!' The most
terrible cries were heard on every side.

"While that dreadful battle raged, the two gods Nara and Narayana came to the
fight. The blessed Vishnu saw the divine bow in Nara's hand, and he called to
mind his discus, the destroyer of the danavas. At once, as soon as he thought of
it, it came down from the sky: the great, shining discus Sudarshana, the
scorcher of enemies, bright as fire, its rim never blunted, terrible,
unconquerable, supreme. Achyuta, whose arms were like the trunks of elephants,
hurled that blazing, frightening, swift and shining discus, which breaks open
the cities of enemies. With the brightness of the fire at the end of time, it
fell again and again, and struck down the sons of Diti and Danu by the thousand,
hurled in that battle by the greatest of beings. Here it burned like fire, and
there it licked them up. It cut the hosts of the asuras to pieces. Thrown into
the sky and down onto the earth again and again, it drank their blood in battle
like a ghoul.

"Then the asuras, undaunted, attacked the gods again and again with mountains.
Mighty and dark as broken clouds, they rose into the sky by the thousand. And
from the sky there fell, terrifying, great mountains with their trees on them,
like clouds of every kind, their summits crumbling, crashing against each other
with a roar. The earth and its forests shook on every side, struck by the
falling mountains, as the fighters roared at one another on the field.

"Then Nara covered the path of the sky with great arrows tipped with gold, and
with his feathered shafts he split the mountain peaks in that terrible battle
with the hosts of the asuras. At last the great asuras, harried by the gods, saw
the blazing Sudarshana in the sky, full of anger, and they fled into the earth
and into the salt sea.

"So the gods won the victory. They took Mandara back to its own place with every
honour, and the clouds, which had filled the sky and heaven with their roar,
went back the way they had come. The gods, overjoyed, put the nectar away
safely. And Indra, slayer of Bala, with the immortals, gave that treasure of
nectar to the Diademed One to keep."

**Divergences.**

* **No poison, no Shiva.** Popular tellings of the churning have the poison
  Halahala rise and Shiva drink it, which turns his throat blue. None of this is
  in the Critical Edition's churning (1.15–1.17). Those lines are rejected
  passages (Adi Appendix I Nos. 9–10; \*268; \*274). See ledger **L-63**. The
  same rejected passages add Airavata, the elephant, to the treasures, and he
  is not listed here either.
* **The tortoise** is "Akupara, king of the tortoises" (1.16.10). The CE does not
  make him Vishnu's Kurma form. See ledger **L-64**.

**Choices.**

* **Garuda's birth told twice.** The CE mentions Garuda's birth at 1.14.22–23 and
  then tells it in full at 1.20.4. The novel keeps both, as the text does.
* 1.13.6 *naimiṣāraṇyavāsinaḥ* is read with the "elders" of the same verse,
  who call the history ancient; 1.13.7 says only that Lomaharshana told it
  "urged by brahmins".
* 1.16.6 *anantaḥ*: the text here says only "Ananta", "the blessed god" at
  1.16.14. The novel does not add "serpent".
* 1.17.6 *ojasā* ("with might") could go with the cutting or the drinking.
  The novel attaches it to the cutting.
* 1.14.9 *adhyardhaṃ putram*: literally "a son and a half". Rendered "the boon
  of one son and a half". The half is Aruna, born incomplete.
* 1.16.26 *amaratvaṃ surā jagmuḥ*: "the gods became immortal" through the milk
  of the juices and the flowing gold, before the nectar itself appears. The text
  says so, and the novel keeps it.
* 1.16.33 *śatasahasrāṃśuḥ samāna iva*: the moon "like one with a hundred
  thousand rays", rendered "like a second sun with a hundred thousand rays".
* 1.17.30 *kirīṭine*: "to the Diademed One". The CE does not say whether this
  is Nara (Arjuna's divine form, later called Kiritin) or Narayana. Ganguli's
  vulgate reads "to Narayana". Left as the CE has it. At 1.30.6 the same title
  names Indra, but here Indra is the giver, so it cannot mean him.
* Yojana: a measure of distance (glossary).
