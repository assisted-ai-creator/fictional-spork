# Sharmishtha's Price

"The man who always bears the harsh words of others," said Shukra, "know,
Devayani, that he has conquered all this world. The man who holds back his anger
when it rises, as a horse is held back, the good call a driver, not the one who
only hangs on to the reins. The man who drives off his anger when it rises by
not being angry, know, Devayani, that he has conquered all this world. The man
who throws off his anger when it rises by forgiving, as a snake throws off its
old skin, he is called a man. The man who holds in his wrath, who bears harsh
words, who does not burn back when he is burned, he is truly a vessel of
success. Of the man who sacrifices every month for a hundred years without
tiring and the man who is never angry with anyone, the one who is not angry is
the greater. When boys and girls make enmity in their thoughtlessness, a wise
man should not follow them. They do not know strength from weakness."

"I know, father, young as I am," said Devayani, "what the difference is between
the dharmas here. I know about not being angry and about harsh words, and I know
strength from weakness. But one who wants to thrive should not forgive a pupil
who does not behave like a pupil. So I do not want to live among people whose
conduct is so mixed. A wise man who seeks his own good should not live among
evil-minded men who scorn his conduct and his birth. One should live among good
people who know him by his conduct and his birth. That is said to be the best
place to live. The words that Vrishaparvan's daughter spoke were very terrible.
I do not think there is anything harder in all three worlds than for a man who
has lost his fortune to wait on his rival's blazing fortune."

---

Then Kavya, the best of the Bhrigus, went in anger to Vrishaparvan, where he
sat, and spoke to him without stopping to think.

"Wrong that is done does not bear fruit at once, king, as the earth does not.
But if he does not see it in himself, a man surely sees his wrong bear fruit in
his sons or his grandsons, like heavy food in the belly. You had the brahmin
Kacha killed, the son of Angiras, who did no evil and knew dharma, who served me
and was happy in my house. Because of the killing of one who did not deserve to
be killed, and the killing of my daughter, understand this, Vrishaparvan: I will
leave you and your kin. I cannot stay in your kingdom with you, king. Ah,
daitya, you think I am one who talks nonsense, since you do not check this fault
of yours, but let it pass."

"I know of no wrong in you, Bhargava, and no lie," said Vrishaparvan. "Dharma
and truth are in you. So be gracious to us. If you leave us and go away from
here, Bhargava, we will go into the ocean. We have no other refuge."

"Go into the ocean, asuras, or run to the ends of the earth," said Shukra. "I
cannot bear my daughter's pain, for she is dear to me. Make peace with Devayani,
for my life depends on her. Then I will see to your welfare, as Brihaspati does
for Indra."

"Whatever wealth the lords of the asuras have on earth, Bhargava," said
Vrishaparvan, "elephants, cattle or horses, you are lord of it all, and of me."

"Whatever wealth the lords of the daityas have, great asura," said Shukra, "I am
lord of it, if you make peace with Devayani."

"If you are lord of the king's wealth, father, Bhargava," said Devayani, "I do
not accept it from you. Let the king speak for himself."

"Whatever you wish for, Devayani, you of the bright smile," said Vrishaparvan,
"I will give it to you, however hard it is to give."

"I want Sharmishtha as my slave, with a thousand girls," said Devayani. "Let her
follow me wherever my father gives me in marriage."

"Get up, nurse," said Vrishaparvan, "and bring Sharmishtha quickly. Whatever
Devayani wishes, let her have it."

Then the nurse went and said to Sharmishtha, "Get up, good Sharmishtha, and
bring happiness to your kin. The brahmin is leaving his pupils, urged on by
Devayani. Whatever she wishes, sinless one, you must do it today."

"Whatever she wishes, I will do it today," said Sharmishtha. "Only let Shukra
not go away, and Devayani, because of me."

Then, at her father's command, she came out quickly from the fine city in a
litter, with a thousand girls around her.

"I, with a thousand girls, am your slave and your servant," said Sharmishtha. "I
will follow you wherever your father gives you."

"I am the daughter of one who praises you," said Devayani, "a bard who accepts
gifts. How will you, the daughter of one who is praised, be a slave?"

"Anyone should bring happiness to her kin when they are in trouble, in whatever
way she can," said Sharmishtha. "So I will follow you wherever your father gives
you."

When Vrishaparvan's daughter had promised to be her slave, best of kings,
Devayani said to her father, "I will go into the city, father. I am satisfied,
best of brahmins. Your knowledge never fails, and the power of your learning is
real."

When his daughter said this, the famous best of brahmins went into the city,
glad, honoured by all the danavas.

---

Then, after a long time, best of kings, the lovely Devayani went out to that
same forest to play. She came to the same place with Sharmishtha and the
thousand slave girls, and wandered there as she pleased, very happy, with all
her companions. They all played there happily, drinking the honey wine of
spring, eating all kinds of food and biting into fruit.

And again, by chance, King Yayati, the son of Nahusha, hunting deer, came to
that same place, tired and wanting water. He saw Devayani and Sharmishtha and
the women, drinking and playing, adorned with heavenly ornaments. He saw
Devayani of the bright smile sitting among the women, a lovely woman of beauty
beyond compare, with Sharmishtha waiting on her, rubbing her feet and so on.

"Two girls, surrounded by two thousand girls," said Yayati. "I ask you both your
families and your names."

"I will tell you," said Devayani. "Listen to me, lord of men. Know that I am the
daughter of Shukra, the teacher of the asuras. And this is my friend and my
slave, who goes wherever I go: Sharmishtha, the daughter of Vrishaparvan, the
king of the danavas."

"How can this girl, so lovely, a daughter of the king of the asuras, be your
friend and your slave, fair one?" said Yayati. "I am very curious."

"Everything follows what is ordained, tiger among men," said Devayani. "Take it
as ordained by fate, and do not tell wondering tales about it. You look and
dress like a king, and you speak the words of the Veda. What is your name, where
are you from, and whose son are you? Tell me."

"I have heard the whole Veda in my years as a student," said Yayati. "I am a
king, and a king's son, known as Yayati."

"Why have you come to this place, king?" said Devayani. "To pick lotuses, or to
hunt?"

"I was hunting, good lady, and I came for water," said Yayati. "And I have been
questioned a great deal. Give me leave to go."

"With two thousand girls, and my slave Sharmishtha, I am yours," said Devayani.
"Bless you, be my friend and my husband."

"Know, daughter of Ushanas, bless you, that I am not worthy of you, proud one,"
said Yayati. "Kings may not marry your father's daughters, Devayani."

"The kshatriya is joined with the brahmin, and the brahmin bound to the
kshatriya," said Devayani. "You are a seer, and a seer's son. Marry me, son of
Nahusha."

"The four classes were all born from one body, fair lady," said Yayati. "But
their dharmas are separate, and their rules of purity are separate, and the
brahmin is the highest of them."

"No man has ever taken this hand of mine, son of Nahusha," said Devayani. "You
took it first. So I choose you. How could another man touch the hand of a proud
woman like me, when it has been taken by you, a seer's son, or a seer yourself?"

"A man who knows," said Yayati, "knows that a brahmin is harder to face than an
angry snake full of poison, or a fire blazing on every side."

"How can you say, bull among men," said Devayani, "that a brahmin is harder to
face than an angry snake full of poison, or a fire blazing on every side?"

"A snake kills one," said Yayati, "and a weapon kills one. But an angry brahmin
kills cities and kingdoms too. That is why I think a brahmin is harder to face,
timid one. So I will not marry you, good lady, unless your father gives you."

"Then marry me when my father gives me to you," said Devayani, "for I have
chosen you, king. There is no danger for a man who does not ask, but takes what
is given."

Then Devayani quickly sent word to her own father. As soon as he heard, the
Bhargava came to see the king. As soon as he saw Shukra come, Yayati, lord of
the earth, bowed to the brahmin Kavya and stood with his palms joined, bending
low.

"Father, this is King Yayati, the son of Nahusha," said Devayani. "He took my
hand when I was in trouble. I bow to you. Give me to him. I will choose no other
husband in the world."

"My dear daughter has chosen you as her husband, hero," said Shukra. "Take her,
son of Nahusha. I give her to you as your queen."

"Then let no great wrong touch me here, Bhargava," said Yayati, "born of the
mixing of the classes, brahmin. That is what I ask of you."

"I free you from the wrong," said Shukra. "Choose what you wish. Do not be
troubled about this marriage. I drive away your sin. Take Devayani of the
slender waist as your wife, by dharma. With her you will find joy beyond
compare. And this girl, Sharmishtha, Vrishaparvan's daughter, must always be
honoured, king. But do not call her to your bed."

When Shukra had said this, Yayati walked round him keeping him on his right, and
with the great one's leave, he went back to his city, glad.
