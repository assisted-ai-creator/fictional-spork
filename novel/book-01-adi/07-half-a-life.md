# Half a Life

"Chyavana, the son of Bhrigu," the bard went on, "had a son by Sukanya: great
Pramati, blazing with power. Pramati had a son by Ghritachi, named Ruru. And
Ruru had a son by Pramadvara, named Shunaka. Now I will tell you the whole life
of Ruru, who had such great power, brahmin, in full. Listen to all of it.

"Long ago there was a great seer named Sthulakesha, rich in austerity and
learning, who delighted in the good of all creatures. At that time the king of
the gandharvas, whose name was Vishvavasu, fathered a child on Menaka, the
apsaras. When her time came, Menaka gave birth near Sthulakesha's hermitage, and
she left the baby, a girl, on the bank of a river and went away. The girl shone
like a child of the immortals, blazing with beauty.

"The great seer Sthulakesha, full of power, saw her abandoned on the river bank,
in a lonely place, with no one to care for her. When that best of brahmins saw
the girl, he was filled with pity, and he took her in and raised her. The lovely
girl grew up in his hermitage. She was the finest of all young women, and she
had every beauty and every virtue, so the great seer named her Pramadvara,
Finest of Women.

"Ruru saw Pramadvara in Sthulakesha's hermitage, and that righteous young man
fell in love with her. He sent word to his father through his friends, and his
father Pramati heard it and went to the famous Sthulakesha. So her father gave
the girl Pramadvara to Ruru, and fixed the wedding for the day of the star of
Bhaga.

"A few days before the wedding, the lovely girl was playing with her friends.
She did not see a snake lying asleep across her path. She stepped on it. She was
going to die. Time had driven her to it. Driven by the law of Time, the snake
sank its poisoned fangs deep into the careless girl's body.

"She was bitten. She fell to the ground at once, senseless, lifeless. She had
been the loveliest of sights, and now no one could bear to look at her. And yet,
lying on the ground, struck down by the snake's poison, she seemed only asleep,
and the slender girl was more lovely than ever.

"Her father saw her fallen on the ground, lying still, as bright as a lotus, and
so did the other ascetics. All the best of the brahmins came there, full of
pity: Svastyatreya, Mahajanu, Kushika, Shankhamekhala, Bharadvaja, Kaunakutsa,
Arshtishena, Gautama, Pramati with his son, and others who lived in the forest.
When they saw the girl lying dead, killed by the snake's poison, they wept with
pity. But Ruru, in his pain, went out alone.

---

"While the brahmins sat all round her, Ruru went deep into the forest, and there
he cried out in his great sorrow. Struck down by grief, he wept and lamented,
and thought of his beloved Pramadvara.

"'She lies on the ground, my slender one, and my grief grows and grows, and so
does the grief of all our kin. What sorrow could be greater than this? If I have
given gifts, if I have practised austerity, if I have truly honoured my elders,
then by that merit let my love live again. From my birth I have held myself in
check and kept my vows. By that, let lovely Pramadvara rise up today.'

"Then a messenger of the gods spoke to him.

"'Ruru, what you say in your grief is useless. A mortal whose life is spent has
no life left, righteous one. This poor girl, the daughter of a gandharva and an
apsaras, has come to the end of her life. So do not give your mind to grief, my
son. But the great gods have laid down a way, long ago. If you are willing to
follow it, you will have Pramadvara back.'

"'What is the way the gods have made?' said Ruru. 'Tell me truly, you who move
through the sky. When I have heard it, I will do it. You must save me.'

"'Give half your life to the girl, son of the Bhrigus,' said the messenger.
'Then your wife Pramadvara will rise up, Ruru.'

"'I give half my life to the girl, best of sky-travellers,' said Ruru. 'Let my
love rise up in her beauty, in her finery and her jewels.'

"Then the king of the gandharvas and the messenger of the gods, those two good
beings, went to Yama, the king of dharma, and spoke to him. 'King of dharma, let
Ruru's wife Pramadvara, who is dead, rise up again with half of Ruru's life, if
you agree.'

"'If you wish it, messenger of the gods,' said the king of dharma, 'let
Pramadvara, Ruru's wife, rise up, with half of Ruru's own life.'

"As soon as he had said it, the girl, lovely Pramadvara, rose up with half of
Ruru's life, as if she had only been asleep. For this was what had been seen
long before in the future of Ruru, whose power was great: that of his very long
life, half should be cut away for his wife's sake.

"Then, on a chosen day, the fathers of the two held the wedding with joy, and
the two of them were happy together, each wishing only good for the other.

"Ruru had won a wife who was hard to win, bright as the heart of a lotus. And he
made a vow, firm in his vow: to destroy the snakes. Whenever he saw a snake, he
was seized with fierce anger, and he always took up whatever weapon was at hand
and struck it down.

---

"One day the brahmin Ruru came to a great forest, and there he saw an old
dundubha lying asleep, a snake of the harmless kind. Ruru was furious. He raised
his stick, like the staff of Death, and struck it.

"Then the dundubha spoke to him. 'I have done you no wrong today, ascetic. Why
do you strike me in such rage?'

"'My wife, dear to me as my own life, was bitten by a snake,' said Ruru. 'And
then, snake, I made myself a terrible vow: that I would always kill every snake
I saw. So I mean to kill you. You will lose your life.'

"'The snakes that bite men are other snakes, brahmin,' said the dundubha. 'You
should not kill dundubhas just because they smell like snakes. We share the
other snakes' troubles but not their purposes, their sorrows but not their joys.
You know dharma. You should not kill dundubhas.'

"When Ruru heard the snake speak like this, he was frightened. He thought the
dundubha must be a seer, and he did not kill it. Instead the blessed Ruru spoke
to it, gently, as if to calm it. 'Tell me, snake, if you will: who are you, who
have come to this changed form?'

"'Long ago, Ruru, I was a seer named Sahasrapat,' said the dundubha. 'I became a
snake through a brahmin's curse.'

"'Why did the brahmin curse you in his anger, best of snakes?' asked Ruru. 'And
how long will you keep this body?'

"'Long ago I had a friend,' said the dundubha, 'a brahmin named Khagama. His
words were very sharp, my son, and he was strong with the power of his
austerity. Once, when we were boys, I was playing, and I made a snake out of
grass. He was busy at his fire offering, and I frightened him with it, and he
fainted.

"'When the ascetic came to himself again, true in his words and strict in his
vows, he spoke to me, as if he would burn me up with his anger. "You made this
snake to frighten me, and it has no power. So by my anger you will become a
snake with no power."

"'I knew the power of his austerity, ascetic, and my heart was full of dread. I
stood before that forest-dweller, bowing, with my palms joined, shaking and
careful. "Friend," I said, "I did it for fun. I was laughing. Please forgive me,
brahmin. Take back this curse."

"'He saw how frightened I was. He was shaken himself, and he sighed hot sighs
again and again. "What I have said is not false," he told me. "It will surely
happen. But listen to what I say to you, you who keep your vows, and keep these
words in your heart, ascetic. A pure son will be born to Pramati, named Ruru.
When you see him, you will soon be freed from the curse."

"'You are the one called Ruru, the pure son of Pramati.'

"And the seer, having taken back his own form, said, 'Now I will tell you what
is good for you.

"'Not harming any living thing is the highest dharma of all creatures that
breathe. That is the teaching. So a brahmin should never harm any living
creature anywhere. A brahmin is born gentle in this world; that is the highest
teaching of the Veda. He knows the Vedas and their limbs, and he gives safety to
every creature. Not harming, speaking the truth, and forgiving: this is settled
as a brahmin's highest dharma, higher even than holding the Vedas in his memory.

"'The dharma of a kshatriya is not meant for you: to carry the rod of
punishment, to be fierce, and to protect the people. Listen, Ruru. That was a
kshatriya's work: Janamejaya's killing of the snakes, long ago, righteous one.
And listen to how the frightened snakes were saved, too, and saved by a brahmin:
by Astika, chief of the brahmins, strong in the power of his austerity, master
of the Vedas and their limbs, at the snake sacrifice, best of brahmins.'

"'How did the kshatriya Janamejaya harm the snakes?' asked Ruru. 'And why were
the snakes harmed, best of brahmins? And why did Astika set them free? Tell me.
I want to hear all of it.'

"'You will hear the whole great story of Astika, Ruru,' said the seer, 'from
brahmins telling it.' And with that he vanished.

"Ruru ran all over the forest, searching everywhere, trying to find the seer,
and at last he fell to the ground, exhausted. When he came to himself, he went
home and told his father what had happened. And when he asked, his father told
him the whole story."

**Choices.**

* 1.8.13 *nakṣatre bhagadaivate*: "the star of Bhaga", the lunar mansion
  whose god is Bhaga.
* 1.9.12 *dharmarāja*: in this scene the "king of dharma" is Yama, lord of
  the dead. The name Yama is added so readers are not confused with
  Yudhishthira, who has the same title later.
* 1.9.16: that half of Ruru's life would be cut away "had been seen" in his
  future. It was fated, and the text says so.
* 1.10.4 *ekānarthān pṛthagarthān ekaduḥkhān pṛthaksukhān*: rendered "we share
  the other snakes' troubles but not their purposes, their sorrows but not their
  joys".
* 1.11.11 *svarūpaṃ pratilabhya*: the seer takes back his own form at this
  point. The text says no more than that.
* **Chronology.** The seer calls Janamejaya's killing of the snakes something
  from "long ago" (*purā*, 1.11.16), though Ruru is Shaunaka's ancestor and the
  sacrifice is recent in the frame story. The novel keeps the text's words and
  does not smooth over the tension.
* *Dundubha*: a harmless water snake. Glossed in the sentence as "a snake of
  the harmless kind".
