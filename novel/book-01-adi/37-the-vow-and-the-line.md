# The Vow and the Line

Satyavati was wretched and grieving, longing for sons. With her two
daughters-in-law she performed the rites for her son. Then the proud and noble
lady thought about dharma, and about the line of the fathers and the line of the
mothers. And she spoke to Ganga's son.

"The funeral offerings, the fame and the line of Shantanu rest on you now. He
was a Kaurava, famous and always devoted to dharma. A good deed surely leads to
heaven, and truth surely leads to long life. In the same way, dharma is sure in
you. You know dharma, both in brief and in full, you who know dharma. You know
the many sacred teachings, and you know all the Vedas. I see how firm you are in
dharma and in the customs of the family. In hard times you know what to do, like
Shukra and Angiras. So I trust you completely, best of those who uphold dharma.
I will give you a task. Hear it, and you should do it.

"My son, your brother, was brave and very dear to you. He has gone to heaven
while still a boy, bull among men, and he had no son. Here are your brother's
queens, the lovely daughters of the king of Kashi. They are young and beautiful,
and they long for sons, Bharata. Father children on them, to carry on our line.
At my bidding, noble one, you should do this dharma. Be consecrated king and
rule the Bharatas. Take a wife by dharma. Do not let your forefathers sink."

His mother said this to him, and so did his friends. But the righteous tamer of
enemies answered with words true to dharma.

"Mother, there is no doubt that what you speak of is the highest dharma. But you
know the great vow I made about children. You know what happened when the bride
price was set for you. Satyavati, I promise you again the truth I promised then.
I would give up the three worlds, or the kingdom of the gods, or anything
greater than either. But I would never give up the truth.

"The earth might give up its smell, and water its taste. Light might give up its
form, and wind its touch. The sun might let go of its brightness, and fire its
heat. Space might give up its sound, and the moon its coolness. The slayer of
Vritra might give up his courage, and the king of dharma his dharma. But I could
never bring myself to give up the truth."

When her son, rich in wealth and splendour, had said this, his mother Satyavati
answered him.

"I know how firmly you stand by the truth, you whose courage is truth. If you
wished, you could make three other worlds by your own power. I know the truth
you spoke for my sake. But think of the dharma for times of trouble. Carry the
burden of your fathers. Act so that the thread of the family and its dharma do
not fail, tamer of enemies, and so that our friends may be glad."

She was wretched, longing for sons, and she went on pleading. She was speaking
against dharma. So Bhishma spoke to her again.

"Queen, look to dharma. Do not destroy us all. For a kshatriya, to fall from the
truth is never praised in the books of dharma. But there is a way for Shantanu's
line to live on unbroken on the earth. I will tell you the kshatriya dharma for
this, queen. It is age-old. Hear it, and then decide, with wise family priests
who are skilled in the dharma for times of trouble. And look to the way of the
world."

---

"Rama, the son of Jamadagni, could not bear his father's death," Bhishma said,
"and in his anger he killed the lord of the Haihayas. He cut off the thousand
arms of Arjuna, the Haihaya king. Then he took up his bow again and let loose
great weapons. Conquering the earth in his chariot, he burned up the kshatriyas
again and again. So in the old days, with weapons high and low, the great
Bhargava emptied the earth of kshatriyas thrice seven times.

"Then brahmins of great self-control lay with kshatriya women everywhere and
fathered children on them. It is laid down in the Vedas that the son belongs to
the man who took the mother's hand in marriage. Keeping that dharma in mind, the
women went to the brahmins. In the world too, kshatriyas have been seen born
again in this way.

"Long ago there was a wise seer named Utathya. He had a wife named Mamata, whom
he loved dearly. Utathya's younger brother was Brihaspati, of great splendour,
the family priest of the gods. And Brihaspati approached Mamata.

"Mamata said to her husband's brother, the best of speakers, 'I am with child by
your elder brother. Stop! And this child of Utathya's, here in my womb, great
one, has already learned the Veda with its six limbs. Your seed is surely never
wasted either. So as things are, you should hold back.'

"She spoke rightly. But Brihaspati, for all his splendour, could not master
himself. He was ruled by desire. So he lay with her, against her will. As he was
releasing his seed, the child in the womb spoke to him.

"'Sir, father's younger brother, stop! There is no room for two here. Your seed
is never wasted, and I came here first.'

"When he was spoken to like this, the blessed seer Brihaspati was angry. He
cursed Utathya's son in the womb and reviled him. 'At such a time, which all
beings long for, you speak to me like this! So you will enter a long darkness.'

"So by the curse of famous Brihaspati the seer was born, and he was named
Dirghatamas, Long Darkness. In power he was like Brihaspati himself. Famous in
his turn, he fathered sons, Gautama and others, to make the line of the seer
Utathya grow. But those sons, Gautama and the rest, were overcome by greed and
folly. They put him on a raft of wood and set him adrift on the Ganga. 'Why
should we keep a blind old man?' they thought. Then those cruel men went home.

"The blind seer floated downstream on that raft and passed through many lands. A
king named Bali, who knew every dharma, had gone to bathe, and he saw him
carried close by the current. Righteous Bali, whose courage was truth, took hold
of him. And when he learned who he was, he chose him to give him sons.

"'Great one,' he said, 'giver of honour, you should father sons on my wives, to
carry on my line, sons skilled in dharma and in wealth.'

"The splendid seer said, 'So be it.' Then the king sent his own wife, Sudeshna,
to him. But the queen thought him blind and old, and she did not go. She sent
him her nurse's daughter instead, to the old man. On her, a shudra woman, the
righteous seer, master of himself, fathered eleven sons, Kakshivat and the
others.

"The mighty king saw Kakshivat and the others all learning the Veda. 'They are
mine,' he said to the seer.

"'No,' said the great seer. 'They are mine. I fathered Kakshivat and the others
on a shudra woman. Your queen, Sudeshna, thought me blind and old. The foolish
woman despised me, and she gave me a shudra, her nurse's daughter.'

"Then Bali won the best of seers over again, and he sent his wife Sudeshna to
him once more. Dirghatamas touched the queen on her limbs, and said to her, 'You
will have a splendid son who speaks the truth.' So the royal seer named Anga was
born to Sudeshna.

"In this way other kshatriyas too were born on the earth to brahmins: great
archers, who knew dharma well, brave and very strong. You have heard this,
mother. Now do here as you wish."

**Divergences.**

* **No marriage rule from Dirghatamas.** The vulgate adds a long passage in which
  Dirghatamas's wife Pradveshi refuses to keep him, and he lays down the rule that a
  woman shall have one husband for her whole life (App. I No. 56, after 1.98.17). It
  is not in the CE, and the novel leaves it out. See ledger L-71.
* **Bali's five sons.** The vulgate names Anga, Vanga, Kalinga, Pundra and Suhma as
  Sudeshna's sons, with the lands named after them (\*1042). The CE names only Anga
  (1.98.32).

**Choices.**

* 1.97.6 *śukrāṅgirasayor iva*: "like Shukra and Angiras". Possibly "like Shukra and
  (Brihaspati) the son of Angiras", the two gods' and demons' priests. Kept literal.
* 1.97.11 *mā nimajjīḥ pitāmahān*: "do not let your forefathers sink", i.e. for want
  of descendants to make offerings to them.
* 1.97.14 *śulkahetos*: "because of the bride price", the bargain with the fisher
  chief (1.94).
* 1.97.23 *dharmād apetaṃ bruvatīm*: the narrator says plainly that she "was
  speaking against dharma".
* 1.98.1 *arjunasya*: Arjuna Kartavirya, the Haihaya king, not the Pandava. The
  novel says "Arjuna, the Haihaya king" to keep them apart.
* 1.98.12 *tayā sārdham akāmayā*: "with her, who was unwilling". The novel says so
  plainly.
* 1.98.13 *kanyasa*: "younger one", addressed to his father's younger brother.
* 1.98.15 *tamo dīrgham*: "a long darkness", the source of the name *Dīrghatamas*.
  The CE implies but does not say here that he was born blind; 1.98.19 and 25 call
  him blind.
* 1.98.20, 22: Bhishma's story addresses its listener as *rājan*, "king", and
  *manujarṣabha*, "bull among men", though he is speaking to Satyavati. The
  novel drops these two vocatives.
* 1.98.25 *dhātreyikām*: "the nurse's daughter", a foster sister and servant.
