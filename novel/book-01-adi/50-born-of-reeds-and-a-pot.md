# Born of Reeds and a Pot

"Great brahmin," said Janamejaya, "tell me about Kripa's birth too. How was he
born from a clump of reeds, and how did he win his weapons?"

The great seer Gautama had a son named Sharadvat, great king, who was born, so
they say, with arrows. His mind did not turn to the study of the Vedas so much,
tamer of enemies, as it turned to archery. As those who speak of Brahman master
the Vedas by austerity, so he, rich in austerity, won all the weapons. By his
devotion to archery and his great austerity, Gautama greatly troubled the king
of the gods.

So the lord of the gods sent a divine girl named Jalapadi. "Put an obstacle in
the way of his austerity," he said, Kaurava. She came to Sharadvat's lovely
hermitage, and the young girl tempted Gautama, who held a bow and arrows. When
Gautama saw the apsaras in the forest, wearing a single cloth, with a figure
unmatched in the world, his eyes opened wide. His bow and arrows fell from his
hands to the ground, and a trembling came over his body at the sight of her.

But his knowledge was so great, and his austerity so strong, that the wise man
held firm with the utmost steadiness. Yet from that sudden stirring, king, his
seed flowed, and he did not notice it. The sage left the hermitage and the
apsaras, and went away. His seed fell on a clump of reeds. Falling on the reeds,
it split in two, king, and from it twins were born to Gautama Sharadvat.

By chance, while King Shantanu was out hunting, one of his soldiers saw the
twins in the forest. He saw the bow and arrows, and the black antelope skins,
and decided they were the children of a brahmin who had mastered archery. So he
showed the twins, with the arrows, to the king. The king took the twins, full of
pity, and went home, saying, "They are my children."

So the king brought them up and gave them the rites of passage. And Gautama,
having gone away, gave himself to archery. "I have brought these two children up
out of pity, *kripa*," said the lord of the earth, so he gave them names from
that very word. Gautama learned by his austerity that they had been left there,
and he came and told Kripa everything, his family and his lineage. He taught him
in full all the fourfold science of archery and every kind of weapon, and all
their secrets. Before long Kripa became a great teacher. Then all the great
warriors learned archery from him: Dhritarashtra's sons and the mighty Pandavas,
the Vrishnis, and other kings who came from many lands.

---

Wanting something better, Bhishma looked for teachers of arrows and weapons, men
respected for their courage, to train his grandsons. *No one of little wit, no
one who is not great and not skilled in many weapons, no one without a god's
strength of spirit, could train the mighty Kurus in weapons*, he thought.

Long ago the great seer Bharadvaja was performing a rite, and he saw the apsaras
Ghritachi herself, who had just bathed. The wind blew up and pulled her garment
away. Then his seed fell, and the seer put it in a pot, a *drona*. In that pot
of the wise man, Drona was born. He studied the Vedas and all their limbs.

The mighty Bharadvaja, the best of those who uphold dharma, handed on the weapon
of Fire to the noble Agniveshya. Then the sage who was born of Fire, best of the
Bharatas, handed on that great weapon of Fire to Bharadvaja's son.

Bharadvaja had a friend, a king named Prishata. At that time a son named Drupada
was born to him. Every day Prishata's son, the bull of the kshatriyas, went to
the hermitage and played and studied with Drona. When Prishata died,
strong-armed Drupada became king, the lord of men among the northern Panchalas.
And the blessed Bharadvaja went up to heaven.

Then, at his father's bidding and wanting a son, the famous Drona married Kripi,
Sharadvat's daughter. Gautama's daughter, who always delighted in the fire rite,
in dharma and in self-control, bore a son, Ashvatthama. As soon as he was born
he whinnied like the horse Ucchaihshravas. When it heard this, an unseen being
in the sky spoke. "Since the strength of this child's cry has reached every
quarter like a horse's, this boy will be called Ashvatthama, Horse-strength."

Bharadvaja's son was delighted with his son. Living there, the wise man gave
himself to archery. He heard, king, that the great Rama, the son of Jamadagni,
the tamer of enemies, was giving away all his wealth to brahmins. Rama was
setting out for the forest when Bharadvaja's son came and said to him, "Know
that I am Drona, a bull among brahmins. I have come for wealth."

"All the gold I had, and whatever other wealth, I have given to brahmins, rich
in austerity," said Rama. "And this goddess, the earth, to the ocean's edge,
with her towns and her garland of cities, I have given whole to Kashyapa. Now I
have only my body left, and my precious weapons, and arms of every kind. Choose,
Drona. What shall I give you? Tell me quickly."

"Give me all your weapons, Bhargava," said Drona, "every one, with the art of
calling them back, and with the secrets of their use."

"So be it," said the Bhargava, and he gave him the weapons, and the whole
science of archery with its secrets and its vows. When he had received it all
and mastered the weapons, the best of brahmins was delighted, and he went to his
dear friend Drupada.

**Divergences.**

* **The vulgate's reasons and bargains.** The vulgate expands the meeting with Rama:
  Rama welcomes Drona on Mount Mahendra, and Drona chooses weapons over "his body"
  (\*1341–\*1346, App. I No. 74). The CE's version is short and given in full here.

**Choices.**

* 1.120.2 *jātaḥ saha śaraiḥ*: "born with arrows", as the text says.
* 1.120.11 *sahasā*: "sudden".
* 1.120.9–11: the text says plainly that his seed flowed without his noticing.
* 1.120.18: the names Kripa and Kripi come from *kṛpā*, "pity". The CE gives the
  etymology but not the girl's name here; she is named at 1.121.11 (*kṛpīm*).
* 1.121.4 *droṇa*: "a pot", the source of Drona's name.
* 1.121.7 *agniṣṭuj jātaḥ*: Agniveshya is "born of Fire" (or of the Agnishtut rite).
  The novel says "born of Fire", with the text.
* 1.121.10 *uttareṣu*: Drupada rules the northern Panchalas.
* 1.121.13: Ucchaihshravas is the horse from the churning of the ocean (chapter 8).
* 1.121.19: Rama says he gave the earth to Kashyapa.
* 1.121.21 *sasaṃhārāṇi*: "with their withdrawal", the art of recalling a weapon once
  released.
