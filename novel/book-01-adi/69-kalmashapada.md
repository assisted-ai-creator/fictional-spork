# Kalmashapada

"There was a king in this world named Kalmashapada," said the gandharva, "born
in the Ikshvaku line, Partha, whose splendour had no equal on earth. Once the
king went out of his city into the forest to hunt, and the crusher of enemies
roamed about shooting deer and boar. Thirsty and hungry, on a path wide enough
for only one, the king, unconquered in battle, saw a sage coming towards him:
great Vasishtha's son, the best of seers, the fortunate Shakti, the joy of
Vasishtha's family, the eldest of great Vasishtha's hundred sons.

"'Get out of our way,' said the king. The seer answered him gently, speaking
softly. But the seer, standing on the path of dharma, did not step aside, and
the king, in his pride and anger, would not give way to the sage either. When
the seer would not leave the path, the best of kings, in his folly, struck the
sage with his whip, like a rakshasa.

"Struck by the whip, the best of sages, Vasishtha's son, faint with rage, cursed
the best of kings. 'Lowest of kings, because you strike an ascetic like a
rakshasa, from today you will be a man-eater. You will roam this earth hungry
for human flesh. Go, lowest of kings!' So said Shakti, whose strength was his
power.

"Now there was a quarrel between Vishvamitra and Vasishtha over who should be
this king's priest, and Vishvamitra was following him. While the two were
disputing like this, Partha, Vishvamitra, the seer of fierce austerity, the
mighty one, came near. Only afterwards did the best of kings realise that the
seer was Vasishtha's son, as bright as Vasishtha himself. And Vishvamitra,
making himself invisible, Bharata, came up to them both, wanting to do what
would please him.

"The best of kings, cursed by Shakti, went to Shakti for shelter, trying to win
his favour and paying him honour. But Vishvamitra, knowing what the king had in
mind, joy of the Kurus, ordered a rakshasa into the king. By the curse of that
brahmin seer and at Vishvamitra's command, a rakshasa named Kinkara entered the
king. When he knew that the rakshasa had seized him, the sage Vishvamitra left
that place, tamer of enemies.

"The king, who was wise, tried to guard himself, though he was sorely troubled
by the rakshasa within him. Then a brahmin saw the king setting out again, and,
being hungry, asked him for a meal with meat. The royal seer Mitrasaha answered
the brahmin gently, 'Stay here a moment, brahmin. When I come back I will give
you the food you want.' With that the king went on, and the best of brahmins
waited. The brahmin's words slipped from the king's mind. He went into the inner
rooms and lay down.

"At midnight he got up, called the cook quickly, and, remembering what he had
promised the brahmin, said, 'Go to such and such a place. A brahmin is waiting
for me there, wanting food. Give him food with meat.' The cook could find no
meat anywhere, and he told the king so in distress. But the king, possessed by
the rakshasa, said to the cook without concern, 'Then feed him with human
flesh,' again and again. 'So be it,' said the cook, and without fear he went
quickly to the place of the executioners and brought human flesh. He prepared it
properly, served it with rice, and gave it quickly to the hungry ascetic
brahmin.

"The best of brahmins looked at the food with the eye of perfection. His eyes
troubled with anger, he said, 'This is not fit to eat. Because that king gives
me food that is not fit to eat, the fool himself will crave it. He will be given
over to human flesh, as Shakti said before, and he will roam this earth, a
terror to all creatures.'

"Spoken twice against the king, the curse grew strong. He was possessed by the
rakshasa's strength and lost his senses. Soon after, Bharata, the best of kings,
his senses struck down by the rakshasa, saw Shakti and said to him, 'Because you
have laid this unfitting curse on me, I will begin my eating of men with you.'
With that he took his life at once and ate Shakti, as a tiger eats the beast it
wants.

"When he saw Shakti killed, Vishvamitra again set the rakshasa on Vasishtha's
other sons. In his fury he ate the younger sons of great Vasishtha, the hundred,
as a lion eats small beasts.

"When Vasishtha heard that his sons had been killed through Vishvamitra, he bore
that grief as the great mountain bears the earth. The best of sages, the wisest
of the wise, set his mind on his own destruction. But he never thought of wiping
out the Kushikas. The blessed seer threw himself from the peak of Meru, but his
head fell on the rock as if on a heap of cotton. When he did not die from the
fall, Pandava, the blessed one lit a fire and went into it in the great forest.
But the fire, though it blazed high, did not burn him. Though it blazed, tamer
of enemies, it became cool. Then, overcome by grief, the great sage went to the
sea, tied a heavy stone round his neck, and threw himself into the water. The
force of the ocean's waves threw the great sage onto the land, and weary, he
went back to his hermitage.

---

"When the sage saw his hermitage empty of his sons, he left it again in deep
grief. He saw a river full of new water in the rainy season, sweeping along many
trees of many kinds that grew on its banks, Partha. Then, joy of the Pauravas,
he thought again in his grief, *I will drown myself in its waters.* So the great
sage bound himself tightly with cords and, in deep grief, sank in the water of
that great river. But the river cut his cords, crusher of enemy armies, set the
seer on the bank and let him go, free of his bonds. The great seer came out,
released from his bonds, and he named the river Vipasha, the Unbound.

"He set his mind on grief and could not stay in one place. He went to mountains,
rivers and lakes. Then the seer saw a Himalayan river, full of fierce
crocodiles, and threw himself into its current. But the best of rivers, thinking
the brahmin was like fire, fled from him in a hundred streams, and so it is
known as Shatadru, the Hundredfold. When he found himself on dry land even
there, he said, 'I cannot die,' and went back towards his hermitage.

"As he went back towards the hermitage, followed by his daughter-in-law
Adrishyanti, he heard behind him the sound of the Veda being recited, full of
meaning and graced with its six limbs. 'Who is this following me?' he said.

"'I am Adrishyanti,' his daughter-in-law answered him, 'Shakti's wife, great
one, devoted to austerity.'

"'Daughter,' said Vasishtha, 'whose is this voice reciting the Veda with its
limbs? I have heard it before, the Veda with its limbs, from Shakti.'

"'This is the child of your son Shakti,' said Adrishyanti, 'conceived in my
womb. He has been practising the Vedas here for twelve years, sage.'

"When she said this, Vasishtha, the seer of the best fortune, was glad. 'There
is a line,' he said, and he turned back from death, Partha.

"As he was going back with his daughter-in-law, sinless one, he saw Kalmashapada
sitting in the empty forest. As soon as he saw him, the king rose up in anger,
Bharata, possessed by the fierce rakshasa, and wanted to eat him. When
Adrishyanti saw the man of cruel deeds in front of them, she said to Vasishtha,
in a voice shaking with fear, 'Lord, here comes a terrible rakshasa, like Death
with his fierce staff, holding a stick of wood. No one on earth but you can stop
him today, great one, best of all who know the Veda. Save me, lord, from this
evil creature, terrible to look at. This rakshasa surely means to eat us both.'

"'Do not be afraid, daughter,' said Vasishtha. 'You need not fear the rakshasa
at all. What you see coming is no danger from a rakshasa. This is King
Kalmashapada, mighty and famous on earth. He lives in this part of the forest,
very terrible.'

"When the blessed seer Vasishtha saw him rushing at them, the splendid one
stopped him with the single sound *hum*, Bharata. Then he sprinkled him with
water made holy by a mantra, and freed the best of kings from the terrible
rakshasa. For twelve years the king had been seized by Vasishtha's own power, as
the sun is seized by Rahu at the time of an eclipse. Freed from the rakshasa,
the king lit up that great forest with his splendour, as the sun lights up the
clouds at dusk. When he came to his senses, he bowed to Vasishtha, the best of
seers, with joined hands, and spoke to him at the proper time.

"'I am Saudasa, great one, the king whose priest you are, best of brahmins. Tell
me what you wish at this time. What can I do for you?'

"'What has happened happened in its time,' said Vasishtha. 'Go and rule your
kingdom. And never despise brahmins, lord of men.'

"'I will never despise the best of brahmins, brahmin,' said the king. 'I will
keep to your command always and honour the twice-born. But, best of brahmins,
best of those who know the Veda, I want a boon from you, by which I may be free
of my debt to the Ikshvakus. You should go to my queen, who is wanted for a
child, and who has character, beauty and virtue, to make the Ikshvaku line
grow.'

"'I will give it,' said Vasishtha, the best of brahmins, true to his word, to
that king, the great archer.

"Then, in time, sinless one, the lord of men went back with Vasishtha to
Ayodhya, the best of cities, famous in the worlds. All the people came out
joyfully to meet him, the great man freed of his sin, as the gods meet their
lord. Soon the lord of men entered the city of people of holy deeds, together
with great Vasishtha. The people of Ayodhya saw him, king, like the sun risen
with the constellation Pushya. The best of the glorious filled Ayodhya with his
glory, as the cool-rayed moon, risen in autumn, fills the sky. The fine city,
its roads sprinkled and swept, decked with banners raised high, gladdened his
heart. Crowded with contented, well-fed people, the city shone with him, joy of
the Kurus, as Amaravati shines with Shakra.

"When the king of kings had entered the city, the queen came to Vasishtha at the
king's command. In her season the great seer, Vasishtha of the best fortune, lay
with the queen by the divine rite. When a child had been conceived in her, the
best of sages, honoured by the king, went back to his hermitage. The queen
carried the child a long time, and when she did not give birth, she split open
her womb with a stone. Then, in the twelfth year, bull among men, the child was
born: the royal seer named Ashmaka, who founded Potana."

**The CE's own doublets.**

* **Ashmaka's birth twice.** At 1.113.21–22 Pandu tells Kunti that Saudasa's wife Madayanti
  went to Vasishtha and bore Ashmaka. Here the queen is unnamed, and splits her womb with a
  stone (*aśman*) after twelve years. Both are kept.
* **Whose power held the king?** The rakshasa enters by Shakti's curse and Vishvamitra's order
  (1.166.17), yet at 1.168.5 the king is said to have been seized "by Vasishtha's own power". The
  CE leaves this unexplained.

**Choices.**

* 1.166.4 *śaktim*: the CE also declines the name as *Śaktin* (1.166.36–37). The novel uses
  Shakti throughout.
* 1.166.21 *mitrasahaḥ*: another name of Kalmashapada. His names Saudasa and Mitrasaha are kept
  where the text uses them.
* 1.166.33 *dvir anuvyāhṛte*: "spoken twice", Shakti's curse and the brahmin's.
* 1.167.6 *vipāśā*: "without cords", the river's name. 1.167.9 *śatadru*: "running in a hundred
  streams".
* 1.167.14 *samā dvādaśa*: the child has been reciting the Veda in the womb "for twelve years".
* 1.168.3 *huṃkāreṇa*: "with the sound hum".
* 1.168.25 *potanam*: the city Ashmaka founded, given in the CE's form, Potana.
