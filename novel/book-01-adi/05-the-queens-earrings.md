# The Queen's Earrings

Some time later, two kshatriyas came to the brahmin Veda, King Janamejaya
and King Paushya, and chose him as their teacher and priest.

Once, when Veda was setting out on some business for one of his patrons, he put a pupil of his named Uttanka in charge. "Uttanka," he said,
"whatever runs short in our house while I am away, I want you to see that
it does not run short."

With these orders to Uttanka, Veda went away on his journey. Uttanka stayed
in his teacher's house, serving his teacher and doing as he had been told.

While he was living there, the women of his teacher's household called him
to them. "Your teacher's wife is in her season," they said, "and your teacher
is away. Do what must be done so that her season is not wasted. It is making
her miserable."

"I will not do a wrong thing because women tell me to," Uttanka answered.
"My teacher never told me, 'Do what should not be done.'"

After a time his teacher came home from his journey. He heard everything
that had happened, and he was pleased.

"Uttanka, my child," he said, "what can I do for you in return? You have
served me as dharma asks, and so the love between us has grown. I give you
leave to go. You will win every success. Go."

"What can I do to please you?" Uttanka answered. "For they say this: 'When
one man teaches and another learns against dharma, one of the two will die,
or become the other's enemy.' You have given me leave, and I want to bring you
a gift for my teaching, whatever you wish."

"Uttanka, my child," said the teacher, "stay a while."

Some time later Uttanka spoke to his teacher again. "Tell me, sir," he said.
"What would you like me to bring you, as the fee for my teaching?"

"Uttanka, my child," said the teacher, "you keep pressing me about bringing me
a fee. Go in, then, and ask your teacher's wife what you should bring. Bring
whatever she tells you."

So Uttanka asked his teacher's wife. "Lady, my teacher has given me leave to
go home. I want to bring you a fee for my teaching, whatever you would like,
and leave free of debt. Tell me, lady, what shall I bring?"

"Go to King Paushya," she told him. "Ask him for the earrings his queen wears,
and bring them here. Four days from now there will be a holy day, and I want
to wear them when I serve the brahmins their food. See to it that I shine in
those earrings on that day. Do this, and good will come to you."

---

With these words from his teacher's wife, Uttanka set out. As he went along
the road, he saw a bull of enormous size, and riding on it a man, also
enormous.

The man spoke to him. "Uttanka, eat this bull's dung."

Uttanka did not want to.

The man spoke again. "Eat it, Uttanka. Don't stop to think about it. Your
teacher ate it too, before you."

"Very well," said Uttanka. He ate the bull's dung and drank its urine, and
went on to where the kshatriya Paushya was.

He came to him and found him seated. He greeted him with blessings and said,
"I have come to you to ask for something."

The king bowed to him. "Holy one, I am Paushya. What can I do for you?"

"I have come to ask for a pair of earrings, as a fee for my teacher," said
Uttanka. "Please give me the earrings your queen wears."

"Go into the women's quarters," said Paushya, "and ask the queen."

Uttanka went into the women's quarters, but he could not see the queen. He
came back to Paushya.

"It is not right for you to treat us with a lie," he said. "Your queen is not
in the women's quarters. I cannot see her."

"You are unclean just now," said Paushya. "Think back. A woman who is true to
her husband cannot be seen by someone who is unclean or impure. Because of
her faithfulness, she does not come into the sight of the unclean."

Uttanka thought back. "It is true," he said. "I rinsed my mouth after eating,
but I did it quickly, while I was walking."

"That is it," said Paushya. "Rinsing while walking does not make you clean,
and neither does rinsing while standing."

"Very well," said Uttanka. He sat down facing east. He washed his hands and
feet and face thoroughly. He sipped water three times without a sound, water
that reached his heart, and wiped his lips twice, and touched water to the
openings of his head. Then he went into the women's quarters, and he saw the
queen.

As soon as she saw Uttanka, she rose and greeted him. "Welcome, holy one," she
said. "Tell me what I can do for you."

"Please give me these earrings," he said. "I have come to ask for them as a
fee for my teacher."

She was pleased by his goodness. *He is a worthy one*, she thought, *and he
must not be refused.* She took off the earrings and gave them to him.

"Takshaka, the king of the snakes, wants these earrings," she told him. "You
must carry them with great care."

"Put your mind at rest, lady," Uttanka answered. "Takshaka, the king of the
snakes, cannot get the better of me."

---

Uttanka took leave of the queen and went back to Paushya. "Paushya," he said,
"I am pleased with you."

"Holy one," said Paushya, "a worthy guest comes to us only once in a long
while, and you are a guest of true merit. I will hold a feast for you. Stay a
moment."

"I am ready now," said Uttanka. "But I am in a hurry. Have food
served to me quickly, whatever is ready."

"Yes," said Paushya, and he fed him with whatever food was ready.

Uttanka saw that the food was cold and had a hair in it. He decided it was
unclean, and he said to Paushya, "You have given me unclean food. So you will
go blind."

"And you have called clean food unclean," answered Paushya. "So you will have
no children."

Then Paushya looked into it, and found out that the food was indeed unclean:
it had been brought by a woman whose hair was loose, and so it had a hair in
it. He tried to calm Uttanka.

"Holy one," he said, "I did not know. The food was brought to you cold and with
a hair in it. I ask your forgiveness. Let me not go blind."

"I do not speak lies," said Uttanka. "You will go blind, and then before long you
will see again. Now let your curse on me be taken back too."

"I cannot take back my curse," said Paushya. "Even now my anger has not cooled.
Don't you know the saying?

> *A brahmin's heart is butter,*
> *but a sharp-edged razor is in his words.*
> *In a kshatriya both are the other way round:*
> *his words are butter, his heart a sharp-edged razor.*

"That is how it is. My heart is sharp, and I cannot change the curse. Go."

"You yourself found out that the food was unclean," said Uttanka, "and then
you tried to calm me. And before that, what you said was, 'You have called
clean food unclean, so you will have no children.' But the food was unclean.
So your curse will not touch me."

"We must be going," said Uttanka. And he set out with the earrings.

---

On the way, Uttanka saw a naked beggar coming towards him, a wandering monk.
Again and again he could be seen, and then he could not be seen. Uttanka put
the earrings down on the ground and went to fetch water.

While he was gone, the beggar ran up quickly, snatched the earrings and fled.
Uttanka ran after him and caught him. But the beggar dropped that shape and
became Takshaka, and at once he slipped into a great hole that had opened in
the earth. He went down into the world of the nagas, the snake-people, to his
own home.

Uttanka followed him down through the same hole. When he had come into the
world of the nagas, he praised the nagas with these verses:

> *Snakes whose king is Airavata, shining in their gatherings,
> pouring down like rain-clouds driven by lightning and wind;
> handsome ones and strange ones, with spotted earrings,
> born of Airavata, you shine like suns on the vault of heaven.
> Many are the paths of the nagas on the north bank of the Ganga.
> Who but Airavata would choose to move among the armies of the sun's rays?
> When Dhritarashtra the naga moves, twenty-eight thousand, eight hundred snakes
> go with him as his reins.
> I bow to those who come near him, and to those who have gone far away:
> I bow to the elder brothers of Airavata.
> I have praised Takshaka, son of Kadru, for the sake of the earrings,
> who has always lived in Kurukshetra and in the Khandava forest.
> Takshaka and Ashvasena, two who are always together,
> live in Kurukshetra, along the river Ikshumati.
> And his younger brother, known as Shrutasena,
> who lived at Mahadyumna, longing to be chief of the nagas:
> to that great one, too, I bow always.*

He praised the nagas like this, but still he did not get the earrings back.
Then he saw two women weaving cloth on a loom. The threads on the loom were
black and white. He saw a wheel, turned by six boys. And he saw a man,
beautiful to look at.

He praised them all with these verses of power:

> *Three hundred and sixty spokes are set in the middle of this fixed wheel,
> the wheel of twenty-four joints, which six boys turn.
> Two young women weave this loom of every form,
> turning the threads without rest, the black threads and the white,
> turning out creatures and worlds, without end.
> Bearer of the thunderbolt, guardian of the world,
> slayer of Vritra and slayer of Namuchi,
> great one who wears two black garments,
> who parts truth from falsehood in the world;
> who took for his mount the horse, the ancient child of the waters,
> the fire Vaishvanara:
> I bow always to him, lord of the world, lord of the three worlds,
> Purandara, breaker of cities.*

Then the man spoke to him. "I am pleased with you for this hymn of praise.
What can I do for you?"

"Let the nagas come under my power," said Uttanka.

"Blow into this horse's hindquarters," said the man.

Uttanka blew into the horse's hindquarters. As he blew, flames and smoke
burst out of every opening of the horse's body, and the world of the nagas
filled with smoke.

Takshaka was thrown into confusion. Terrified of the heat of the fire, he took
the earrings and rushed out of his home. "Here are the earrings, sir," he said
to Uttanka. "Take them."

Uttanka took them. Holding the earrings, he thought: *Today is my teacher's
wife's holy day, and I have come so far. How can I possibly get back in time?*

While he was still thinking this, the man spoke to him. "Uttanka, get up on
this horse. It will take you to your teacher's house in a moment."

"Yes," said Uttanka. He got up on the horse and rode back to his teacher's
house.

His teacher's wife had bathed and was sitting and having her hair done.
*Uttanka is not coming*, she thought, and she had made up her mind to curse
him. Then Uttanka came in and greeted her, and gave her the earrings.

"Uttanka," she said, "you have come to the right place at the right time.
Welcome, my child. I very nearly cursed you, but I did not. Good fortune is
waiting for you. May you succeed."

---

Then Uttanka greeted his teacher.

"Welcome, Uttanka, my child," said his teacher. "What kept you so long?"

"Sir," said Uttanka, "Takshaka, the king of the nagas, got in my way, and he
took me to the world of the nagas. There I saw two women weaving on a loom,
and the threads were black and white. What was that? I saw a wheel with
twelve spokes, and six boys were turning it. What was that too? And I saw a
man. Who was he? And a horse of great size. Who was that?

"And on the road, as I went, I saw a bull with a man riding on it. He spoke
to me kindly. 'Uttanka,' he said, 'eat this bull's dung. Your teacher ate it
too.' So, because he told me to, I ate the bull's dung. I would like you to
tell me what that was."

"The two women are Dhatri and Vidhatri, the Maker and the Arranger," said his
teacher. "The black and white threads are the nights and the days. The wheel
with twelve spokes, turned by six boys, is the year, and the boys are the six
seasons. The man is Parjanya, the god of rain. The horse is Agni, the fire.

"The bull you saw on the road was Airavata, king of the nagas, and the man
riding it was Indra. The dung you ate was the nectar of immortality. That is
why you came to no harm in the home of the nagas. Indra is my friend. By his
kindness you got the earrings and came back. Now go, my dear. I give you
leave. You will win what is good."

---

With his teacher's leave, Uttanka set out for Hastinapura. He was angry, and
he wanted to pay Takshaka back.

When he reached Hastinapura, that best of brahmins went before King
Janamejaya. The king had come back a little while before from Takshashila,
undefeated and victorious, and his ministers stood all round him. Uttanka
first blessed him and wished him victory, in the proper way. Then, at the
right moment, he spoke to him, and his voice was clear and strong.

"Best of kings, there is one thing you ought to be doing. Yet you are busy with
something else, like a child."

The king received his words kindly and honoured the sage with due respect.
"I keep my own dharma as a kshatriya by protecting these people of mine," he
answered. "But tell me, best of brahmins, what should I do? I am ready to
listen."

Then that best of brahmins, the most virtuous of men, told the king, whose
spirit was never low, what the king had to do and what he himself wanted.

"Lord of kings, it was Takshaka who killed your father. Pay back that evil
snake. I think the time has come for the deed that the scriptures lay down.
Go, king, and take vengeance for your great father. He had done no wrong, and
that wicked-hearted snake bit him, and he died, like a tree struck by
lightning. Proud and puffed up with his strength, Takshaka, lowest of snakes,
did an evil thing when he bit your father. That wicked one killed the king,
the protector of the line of royal seers, a king like the gods, and he turned
Kashyapa back.

"You must burn that evil one in the blazing fire, great king, at a snake
sacrifice. That is the deed ordained for you. So you will pay what you owe to
your father, and you will do me a great kindness too, lord of the earth. For
that wicked one got in my way, great king, when I was trying to bring my
teacher his fee."

When the king heard this, he was furious with Takshaka. Uttanka's words fed his
anger like butter poured on a fire. In great sorrow, with Uttanka standing
beside him, the king questioned his ministers about how his father had gone to
heaven. For the king was flooded with grief and pain at the very moment he
heard from Uttanka how his father had died.

**Choices.**

* 1.3.100 *puṇyaka*: "a holy day", a religious observance at which the
  teacher's wife would feed brahmins.
* 1.3.112 *ucchiṣṭa*: ritually unclean after eating, before rinsing the
  mouth properly. Rendered "unclean".
* 1.3.115: the purification is kept in its detail (facing east, sipping three
  times, wiping twice, touching water to the "openings"), because the story
  turns on it.
* 1.3.136 *nagnaḥ śramaṇaḥ*: "a naked wandering monk", literally a naked
  *śramaṇa*.
* **The two hymns (1.3.139–146 and 1.3.150–153)** are given by their images
  and in their order. 1.3.146 *avasadyo mahad dyumni* is obscure and is read
  as a place name, "Mahadyumna". 1.3.152 *kṛṣṇe vasāno vasane*: "who wears
  two black garments".
* 1.3.142 *śatāny aśītir aṣṭau ca sahasrāṇi ca viṃśatiḥ*: read as "eighty-eight
  hundreds and twenty thousands", which is 28,800. Ganguli's vulgate gives
  28,008.
* 1.3.168: Uttanka calls it a wheel of **twelve** spokes. The hymn
  (1.3.150) speaks of three hundred and sixty. Both are kept as the text has
  them.
* 1.3.174 *airāvato nāgarājaḥ*: "Airavata, king of the nagas". *Nāga* can
  mean both snake and elephant. The text says only "king of the nagas", so
  the English does not choose.
* 1.3.186 *vidhidṛṣṭasya karmaṇaḥ*: "the deed the scriptures lay down" (that
  is, the snake sacrifice).
* 1.3.189 *kāśyapaṃ … nyavartayata*: Takshaka "turned Kashyapa back". Who
  Kashyapa was, and why this mattered, is told in the Astika chapters
  (1.38–1.40).
