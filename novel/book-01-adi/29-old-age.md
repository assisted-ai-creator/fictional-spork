# Old Age

When Yayati came to his city, which was like the city of great Indra, he went
into the women's quarters and settled Devayani there. With Devayani's consent he
had a house built near the ashoka grove for Vrishaparvan's daughter, and he
settled Sharmishtha, the asura's daughter, there, with her thousand slave girls,
well cared for, giving her clothes, food and drink. With Devayani, the king, the
son of Nahusha, took his pleasure for many years, very happy, like a god. When
her season came, the lovely Devayani conceived, and she bore a son, the first.

When a thousand years had passed, Sharmishtha, Vrishaparvan's daughter, saw that
she had come to her youth, and that her season had come, and she thought, *My
season has come, and I have not chosen a husband. What has happened to me? What
should I do? What can I do to make it right? Devayani has had a child. I have
come to my youth for nothing. As she chose a husband, so I will choose him. The
king should give me the fruit of a son. That is what I have decided. Will that
righteous man come to see me now, alone?*

Then the king came out at that time, by chance, and came to the ashoka grove,
and stood there, having found Sharmishtha. When Sharmishtha of the lovely smile
saw him alone, in a lonely place, she went to meet the king, joined her palms,
and said, "In the house of Soma, or Indra, or Vishnu, or Yama, or Varuna, or in
yours, son of Nahusha, who would dare to touch a woman? You know me always,
king, for my beauty, my family and my character. I beg you to be kind to me.
Give me my season, lord of men."

"I know you are a daitya's daughter of good character, and without fault," said
Yayati. "I do not see a fault in your beauty the size of a needle's point. But
when I married Devayani, Kavya Ushanas said to me, 'You must not call
Vrishaparvan's daughter to your bed.'"

"A word spoken in jest does no harm, king," said Sharmishtha, "nor one spoken to
women, nor at the time of marriage, nor when life is in danger, nor when all
one's wealth is being taken. They say these five lies are not sins. But when a
man is asked as a witness and speaks otherwise, they call that a lie, lord of
men. When the matter concerns one and the same purpose, the lie destroys the man
who speaks it."

"The king is the measure for all beings," said Yayati. "He would perish if he
lied. Even if I were in great need, I would not dare to act falsely."

"These two are held to be the same, king," said Sharmishtha: "one's own husband
and one's friend's husband. They call it the same marriage. My friend chose you
as her husband."

"I have taken a vow that I must give to those who ask," said Yayati. "And you
ask me for what you desire. Tell me, what shall I do for you?"

"Save me from adharma, king," said Sharmishtha, "and bring me to dharma. If I
have a child by you, I will walk the highest dharma in the world. Three have no
wealth of their own, king: a wife, a slave and a son. Whatever they come by
belongs to the one they belong to. I am Devayani's servant, and the daughter of
Bhrigu's line is in your power. She and I are both yours to support, king. Love
me."

When she said this, the king knew that it was true. He honoured Sharmishtha and
brought her to dharma. He lay with Sharmishtha and had what he wished, and they
honoured each other and went back the way they had come. From that union, lovely
Sharmishtha of the charming smile conceived her first child by that best of
kings. In time, king, the lotus-eyed woman bore a boy like a child of the gods,
with eyes like lotuses.

---

When Devayani of the bright smile heard that a boy had been born, she was hurt,
and she thought about Sharmishtha, Bharata. She went to Sharmishtha and said,
"What is this wrong you have done, fair one, in your greed for love?"

"A seer came here," said Sharmishtha, "a righteous man, a master of the Vedas.
He gave boons, and I asked him for what I desired, as dharma allows. I do not
take my pleasure against the rules, you of the bright smile. So my child is from
that seer. I tell you the truth."

"That is well, timid one, if it is true," said Devayani, "and if the brahmin is
known. I want to know that brahmin, his family, his name and his birth."

"He blazed with power and splendour, like the sun," said Sharmishtha. "When I
saw him, I did not have the strength to ask, you of the bright smile."

"If this is so, Sharmishtha," said Devayani, "then I have no anger, if you have
had a child from an elder and a better brahmin."

When they had said this to each other, and laughed together, the daughter of
Bhrigu's line went home, believing it was true.

King Yayati fathered two sons on Devayani, Yadu and Turvasu, like a second
Shakra and Vishnu. And from that same royal seer, Sharmishtha, Vrishaparvan's
daughter, bore three boys: Druhyu, Anu and Puru.

Then one day, king, Devayani of the bright smile went out into the great forest
with Yayati. There she saw boys like gods, playing without a care. She was
amazed, and said, "Whose are these children, king? They are like the sons of
gods, and lovely. In splendour and in looks they seem to me like you." And when
she had asked the king, she asked the boys, "What are your names and your
family, my children? Who is your father? Tell me the truth. I want to hear."

The children pointed with their forefingers at that best of kings, and told her
their mother was Sharmishtha. When they had said this, they went to the king
together. But in front of Devayani the king did not greet them, and the little
boys went crying to Sharmishtha.

When the queen saw the boys' love for the king, and understood the truth, she
said to Sharmishtha, "You are in my power. Why have you done what displeases me?
You have gone back to the ways of the asuras. Are you not afraid?"

"What I told you, that he was a seer, was true, you of the charming smile," said
Sharmishtha. "I act by right and by dharma, and I am not afraid of you. When you
chose the king, I chose him too. By dharma, a friend's husband becomes a
husband, lovely one. You are to be honoured and respected by me, for you are the
eldest and the best, a brahmin woman. But the royal seer is to be honoured by me
more than you. Do you not know that?"

When she heard her, Devayani said, "King, I will not live here now. You have
done what displeases me."

She rose up suddenly, the dark young woman, her eyes full of tears, and set out
in haste for Kavya. The king was troubled when he saw her go, and he followed
her in alarm, trying to calm her. But she did not turn back, her eyes red with
anger. The lovely-eyed woman said not a word to the king, and soon she came to
Kavya Ushanas. As soon as she saw her father, she bowed to him and stood before
him, and after her Yayati honoured the Bhargava.

"Dharma has been beaten by adharma," said Devayani. "The low has risen above the
high. I have been wronged by Sharmishtha, Vrishaparvan's daughter. This king,
Yayati, has fathered three sons on her, and on me, unlucky that I am, only two,
I tell you, father. This king is famous for knowing dharma, best of the Bhrigus,
and he has broken the bounds. I tell you this, Kavya."

"Great king," said Shukra, "since you know dharma, and yet you did adharma as if
it were dear to you, old age, which is hard to conquer, will soon overpower
you."

"She asked me for her season, blessed one," said Yayati, "and I did not do it
with any other thought. What I did for the daughter of the king of the danavas
was lawful. A man who is asked for her season by a woman, and does not give it,
the knowers of Brahman here call a killer of an unborn child, brahmin. A man who
is asked in private by a willing woman who is lawful to him, and does not go to
her, the wise call a killer of an unborn child in matters of dharma. I
considered these reasons, best of the Bhrigus, and in fear of adharma I went to
Sharmishtha."

"Should you not have looked to me?" said Shukra. "You are in my care, king. In
matters of dharma, false conduct is theft, son of Nahusha."

So Yayati, the son of Nahusha, was cursed by Ushanas in his anger, and at once
he lost his youth and took on old age.

"I have not had my fill of youth with Devayani, best of the Bhrigus," said
Yayati. "Be gracious to me, brahmin. Let this old age not enter me."

"I do not speak falsely," said Shukra. "You have come to old age, lord of the
earth. But if you wish, pass this old age on to someone else."

"Let the son who gives me his youth," said Yayati, "have the kingdom, and the
merit, and the fame, brahmin. Give me your consent to this."

"You will pass on your old age as you wish, son of Nahusha," said Shukra. "Think
of me with love, and you will not fall into sin. The son who gives you his youth
will be king. He will be long-lived and famous, and he will have many children."

---

When Yayati had come to old age and reached his own city, he spoke to his eldest
and best son, Yadu. "Old age, wrinkles and grey hair have come over me, my son,
by the curse of Kavya Ushanas, and I have not had my fill of youth. Take my
wrong, Yadu, together with my old age. Let me enjoy the world with your youth.
When a thousand years are over, I will give you back your youth, and take my
wrong back again, with my old age."

"With a white beard and white hair, wretched, made slack by old age," said Yadu,
"his body covered in wrinkles, ugly, weak and thin, unable to do his work,
scorned by the young and by those who live off him: I do not want that old age."

"You were born from my heart," said Yayati, "and you will not give me your
youth. So your children, my son, will have no share in kingship. Turvasu, take
my wrong, with my old age. Let me enjoy the world with your youth, my son. When
a thousand years are over, I will give you back your youth, and take my wrong
back again, with my old age."

"I do not want old age, father," said Turvasu. "It destroys desire and pleasure.
It puts an end to strength and beauty. It destroys the mind and the breath."

"You were born from my heart," said Yayati, "and you will not give me your
youth. So your line, Turvasu, will be cut off. You fool, you will be king among
people of mixed conduct and dharma, who live against the grain, eaters of raw
flesh, outcasts. You will be lord among the sinful barbarians who lie with their
teachers' wives, who are born as beasts and live the dharma of beasts."

When Yayati had cursed his own son Turvasu like this, he said to Sharmishtha's
son, Druhyu, "Druhyu, take my old age, which destroys colour and beauty, for a
thousand years, and give me your youth. When a thousand years are over, I will
give you back your youth, and take back again my own wrong, with my old age."

"An old man cannot enjoy an elephant, a chariot, a horse, or a woman," said
Druhyu, "and his speech fails him. I do not want that old age."

"You were born from my heart," said Yayati, "and you will not give me your
youth. So, Druhyu, the love you desire will never come to you anywhere. You and
your line will live where men cross the water always by raft and boat, and
there, no king, you will have the name of Bhoja. Anu, take my wrong, with my old
age. Let me enjoy the world with your youth for one thousand years."

"An old man takes his food at the wrong times, like a child, and unclean," said
Anu, "and he does not make his offering in the fire at the proper time. I do not
want that old age."

"You were born from my heart," said Yayati, "and you will not give me your
youth. You have told the faults of old age, and so you will come to them. And
your children, Anu, will die as soon as they reach their youth. And you too will
be one who lets the sacred fire go out.

"Puru, you are my dear son. You will be the best. Old age, wrinkles and grey
hair have come over me, my son, by the curse of Kavya Ushanas, and I have not
had my fill of youth. Puru, take my wrong, with my old age. Let me enjoy the
world for a while with your youth. When a thousand years are over, I will give
you back your youth, and take my wrong back again, with my old age."

When he said this, Puru answered his father at once, "I will do as you say,
great king. I will take your wrong, king, with your old age. Take my youth from
me, and enjoy your desires as you wish. Covered with old age, taking on your age
and your looks, I will give you my youth and live as you tell me."

"Puru, my child, I am pleased with you," said Yayati. "And because I am pleased,
I give you this: your children will be rich in all they desire in your kingdom."

**Choices.**

* 1.77.6 *gate varṣasahasre*: "when a thousand years had passed". The text's
  span of time is kept.
* 1.77.16–17: the "five lies that are not sins" are Sharmishtha's argument, given
  as she states it.
* 1.77.22: "a wife, a slave and a son have no wealth of their own". This too is
  Sharmishtha's argument, kept without comment.
* 1.78.3, 1.78.19: Sharmishtha's "a seer" is a half-truth. Yayati is a royal seer
  (*rājarṣi*), which she explains at 1.78.21.
* 1.78.30 *adharmam akṛthāḥ priyam*: "you did adharma as if it were dear". Kept.
* 1.78.32–33 *bhrūṇahan*: "killer of an unborn child", the term used for a man
  who refuses a woman's request in her season. Yayati's argument is given as he
  makes it.
* 1.78.35: Shukra's reply, "false conduct is theft", is kept.
* 1.79.12–13, 1.79.19, 1.79.23: Yayati's curses on Turvasu's, Druhyu's and Anu's
  lines are given in the text's own terms. The next chapter names the peoples
  said to descend from them (1.80.26). The novel reports these as Yayati's curse
  and the text's statements, and does not endorse or soften them.
* 1.79.19 *uḍupaplavasaṃtāraḥ*: a land "where the crossing is always by raft and
  boat". *Bhoja*: Druhyu's line will be called Bhojas, not kings.
* 1.79.23 *agnipraskandanaparaḥ*: "given to letting the fire fall", i.e. neglect
  of the sacred fire.
