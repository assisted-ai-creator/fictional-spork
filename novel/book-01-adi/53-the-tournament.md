# The Tournament

When Drona saw that Dhritarashtra's sons and Pandu's sons had mastered their
weapons, Bharata, he spoke to King Dhritarashtra, lord of the people, in the
presence of Kripa, Somadatta, wise Bahlika, Ganga's son, Vyasa and Vidura.

"King, best of the Kurus, the princes have completed their training. With your
leave, king, let them show what they have learned."

The great king was glad at heart. "Son of Bharadvaja," he said, "you have done a
great thing, best of brahmins. Whenever you think the time is right, and
wherever, and however, command me yourself to arrange it. Today, in my despair,
I envy men who have eyes, who will see my sons show their courage in arms.
Steward, do exactly what the teacher says. I do not think anything could please
me more, you who love dharma."

Then Bharadvaja's son, very wise, took his leave of the king. He went out with
Vidura and had the ground measured. It was level, without trees or bushes,
sloping to the north. On a day when the stars were favourable he made an
offering on that ground, and it was proclaimed in the city, best of speakers. On
the arena, craftsmen built a very large hall for the spectators, well made, as
the books of building require. It was for the king, with every kind of weapon,
and for the women, bull among men. The people of the country built platforms
there, large and high, and costly litters.

When the day came, the king went with his ministers, putting Bhishma and Kripa,
the best of teachers, at the front. He came to the splendid golden hall, hung
with nets of pearls and decked with beryl. Noble Gandhari and Kunti, best of
conquerors, and all the king's women climbed happily onto the platforms with
their maids and their trains. They were like the wives of the gods climbing
Meru. The four classes, brahmins, kshatriyas and the rest, came hurrying from
the city, eager to see the princes' skill in arms. With music playing and the
people's excitement, the gathering became like a great ocean churned up.

Then the teacher entered the middle of the arena with his son, wearing white,
with a white sacred thread, white hair and a white beard, white garlands and
white paste. He was like the sun with Mars in a cloudless sky. The strongest of
the strong made an offering as the time required, and had brahmins who knew the
mantras recite the blessing.

After the sound of the holy blessing, men came in carrying weapons and equipment
of every kind. Then the bulls of the Bharatas came in, the mighty princes, with
their armour on, their belts tied, their quivers fastened and their bows in
hand. There, in order of age, with Yudhishthira at their head, the princes of
great courage showed their most wonderful skill in arms. Some of the people
ducked their heads for fear of the arrows flying. Others watched boldly, amazed.
Riding their horses fast, the princes struck the targets with arrows of every
kind, beautiful with their names marked on them, loosed with speed. The people
saw that company of princes with their bows and arrows, like a city of the
gandharvas, and they were amazed. Suddenly men by the hundred thousand shouted,
"Well done! Well done!", Bharata, their eyes wide with wonder.

The mighty princes showed the ways of the bow again and again, in the chariot
drills, on elephants and on horses, and in wrestling. Then, taking sword and
shield, the fighters went through the sword drills as they had been taught, all
over the ground. The people saw how quick they all were, how well they moved,
how handsome, how steady and how firm of grip, in the use of sword and shield.

Then those two who always delighted in it, Suyodhana and Wolf-belly, came down
into the ring, maces in hand, like two mountains, each with a single peak. With
their belts tied, strong-armed, standing on their manhood, they roared like two
rutting elephants over a cow. The mighty pair circled left and right with their
shining maces, like two bulls in rut.

Vidura told Dhritarashtra everything the princes did, and Kunti, the fire-stick
that kindled the Pandavas, told Gandhari.

---

With the king of the Kurus and Bhima, the strongest of the strong, in the ring,
the crowd was split in two, as their loyalties took them. "Ah, the hero, the
king of the Kurus!" some roared. "Ah, Bhima!" roared others. And suddenly huge
shouts went up from the men.

When wise Bharadvaja saw the arena heaving like a stormy sea, he said to his
dear son Ashvatthama, "Stop these two mighty men. They have both trained well.
Do not let the arena break out in fury because of Bhima and Duryodhana."

So the teacher's son held them back, with their maces raised, like two oceans
lashed by the winds at the end of an age.

Then Drona went into the courtyard of the arena. He silenced the band, whose
noise was like a great cloud, and said, "Now see Partha, dearer to me than my
son, the best of all masters of weapons, Indra's son, the equal of Indra's
younger brother!"

At the teacher's word, after the blessings had been said, the young man
appeared. It was Phalguna, with his lizard-skin finger guard tied on, his quiver
full and his bow in hand, wearing golden armour. He was like a cloud at dusk,
with the sun, the rainbow and the lightning.

Then a great cry went up from the whole arena, and music played everywhere, with
conches. "This is Kunti's glorious son! This is the middle Pandava! This is
great Indra's son! This is the protector of the Kurus! This is the best of the
masters of weapons, the best of those who uphold dharma! This is the highest
treasure of good conduct and knowledge among men of good conduct!"

As Kunti heard these matchless words from the spectators, her breast grew wet
with tears mixed with milk.

Dhritarashtra, best of men, with his ears full of that great noise, said happily
to Vidura, "Steward, what is this huge noise, like a stormy sea, that has
suddenly risen in the arena, as if to split the sky?"

"Great king," said Vidura, "it is Partha, Phalguna, Pandu's son, who has come
down in his armour. That is the great noise."

"I am blessed," said Dhritarashtra, "and favoured, and protected, wise one, by
the three Pandava fires sprung from Pritha's fire-stick."

When the excited arena had somehow grown calm, Bibhatsu showed the speed with
weapons he had learned from his teacher. With the weapon of Fire he made fire.
With the weapon of Varuna he made water. With the weapon of Wind he made wind,
and with the weapon of the Rain-cloud he made clouds. With the weapon of Earth
he went into the ground, and with the weapon of the Mountain he made mountains.
With the weapon of Vanishing he vanished again. In a moment he was tall, in a
moment short. In a moment he was at the yoke of his chariot, in a moment in the
middle of it, and in a moment he leaped down to the ground. His teacher's
favourite struck targets delicate, small and heavy with arrows of every kind,
with perfect grace. Into the mouth of an iron boar as it swung round he shot
five arrows, not touching one another, as if they were a single arrow. Into the
hollow of a cow's horn hanging from a rope and swaying, the mighty man drove
twenty-one arrows. These and other great feats he showed with the sword and the
bow, and he showed his skill with the mace, a master of every weapon.

When that display was almost over, Bharata, and the crowd was growing quiet and
the music was dying down, a sound was heard from the gate. It told of greatness
and strength. It was the slapping of arms, like the crash of a thunderbolt. *Are
the mountains splitting?* the whole arena thought for a moment, lord of the
earth. *Is the earth cracking open? Is the sky filling with clouds heavy with
rain?* And all the spectators turned to face the gate.

Drona stood there surrounded by the five Partha brothers, like the moon with the
five stars of the constellation Savitra. And the proud hundred brothers, with
Ashvatthama, gathered round Duryodhana, the killer of enemies, as he rose. He
stood there with his mace in his hand, surrounded by his brothers with their
weapons raised. He shone like Indra long ago among the companies of the gods,
when the danavas were destroyed.

**Divergences.**

* **No mention of Krishna here.** The vulgate adds to the crowd's praise of Arjuna
  that he is "the friend dear as life to the slayer of Kamsa" (\*1416). The CE does
  not bring Krishna in at this point.

**Choices.**

* 1.124.6: Dhritarashtra's envy of "men with eyes" is his own, in his "despair"
  (*nirvedāt*).
* 1.124.18 *sāṅgāraka ivāṃśumān*: "like the sun with Mars (Angaraka)".
* 1.124.30 *ekaśṛṅgāv ivācalau*: "like two single-peaked mountains".
* 1.124.33 *pāṇḍavāraṇiḥ*: Kunti, "the fire-stick of the Pandavas". The verb is dual:
  Vidura told Dhritarashtra and Kunti told Gandhari.
* 1.125.2 *sahasā*: "suddenly"; also 1.124.26 and 1.125.15.
* 1.125.7 *indrānujasamaḥ*: "the equal of Indra's younger brother", i.e. Vishnu.
* 1.125.8 *baddhagodhāṅgulitrāṇaḥ*: with his "lizard-skin finger guard" tied on.

  names three of them, Kunti's sons.

  uncertain.
