# The Kings Attack

When Drupada wished to give his daughter to that great brahmin, anger rose in
the kings, and they looked at one another close at hand.

"He has passed us over," they said, "and treats us, gathered here, like straw.
He wants to give Draupadi, the best of women, to a brahmin. Let us kill this
wicked man, who does not respect us. By his qualities he deserves neither honour
nor the respect due to age. Let us kill this evil-doer, this enemy of kings,
together with his son. He invited all the kings, honoured them, and fed them
well, and afterwards he insults them. In this gathering of kings, like a meeting
of the gods, did he see no king who was a fit match? Brahmins have no right to
be chosen. The bridegroom choice is for kshatriyas. That is the well-known
tradition. Or if this girl does not want any of us here, let us throw her into
the fire and go back to our kingdoms, kings.

"But the brahmin, even if he has done this thing that displeases the lords of
kings out of childishness or greed, must not be killed on any account. For our
kingdoms, our lives, our wealth, our sons and grandsons and whatever else we
have, are there for the sake of brahmins. We do this for fear of dishonour, and
to guard our own dharma, so that other bridegroom choices do not go this way."

When they had said this, the tigers among kings, with arms like iron bars, ran
gladly at Drupada with their weapons, meaning to seize him. When Drupada saw the
angry kings rushing at him, their bows and quivers in their hands, he took
shelter with the brahmins in fear.

The two sons of Pandu, of great courage, the tamers of enemies, went to meet the
kings as they came on at speed, like elephants in rut. Then the kings, their
finger guards bound on, rose up with their weapons raised. They could not bear
it, and they meant to kill the two Kuru princes, Arjuna and Bhimasena.

Then Bhima, of wonderful courage and deeds, of great strength, with a strength
like the thunderbolt, the lone hero, tore up a tree with his arms and stripped
off its leaves, like a lord of elephants. Taking that tree, the long-armed
Partha, the crusher of enemies, stood near Partha, the bull among men, like the
king of the dead with his fierce staff.

---

When he saw that deed of Jishnu's brother, beyond the reach of human thought,
Damodara, whose deeds are beyond imagining, spoke to his brother Halayudha, of
fierce courage.

"That man who walks like a bull in rut, and draws the great bow as tall as a
palm tree, is Arjuna. There is no doubt of it, Samkarshana, if I am Vasudeva.
That one who tore up the tree with force and turned suddenly when the kings made
their move is Vrikodara. No one else on earth born to die could do such a thing
today. The one who went out before them, Achyuta, with long lotus eyes, slender,
moving like a great lion, well-mannered, fair-skinned, with a long, bright,
handsome nose, is the Dharma King. And those two young men, like Karttikeya, I
think are the sons of the Ashvins. For I heard that the sons of Pandu and Pritha
escaped from the burning of the lac house."

Then Halayudha, like a cloud without a stain, said gladly to his younger
brother, "I am pleased. By good fortune our father's sister Pritha has escaped
with the best of the Kurus."

---

The bulls of the brahmins, shaking their deerskins and water pots, said, "Do not
be afraid. We will fight the enemy."

Arjuna, as if laughing, said to the brahmins as they spoke, "Stand to one side
and watch. I will scatter these angry men with hundreds of straight arrows and
hold them back, as snakes are held back with mantras."

With that the great warrior took up the bow he had won as the bride-price, and
stood with his brother Bhima, as unmoving as a mountain. Then the two of them,
without fear, rushed at the kshatriyas led by Karna, who had risen in anger, as
elephants rush at rival elephants.

The kings, wanting to kill, spoke harsh words. "In battle even a brahmin who
wants to fight may be killed. It has been seen."

Then Karna Vaikartana went at Arjuna with force, wanting to fight, like an
elephant going at a rival elephant for the sake of a cow elephant. Shalya, the
strong lord of the Madras, went at Bhimasena. Duryodhana and the others fought
with the brahmins in that battle, lightly and without effort.

Then wise Arjuna drew his strong bow and struck Karna Vaikartana with three
arrows as he came on. Dazed by the force of those sharp, fiercely bright arrows,
Radheya pressed after him with an effort. Those two, the best of victors, fought
in fury, each wanting to defeat the other, so fast that no one could follow
them.

"See how I answer what you do!"

"See the strength of my arms!"

So they called to each other in heroes' words. Then Karna Vaikartana, knowing
that the strength of Arjuna's arms had no equal on earth, fought on in anger. He
struck down the swift arrows Arjuna shot at him and roared aloud, and the
fighters cheered him.

"I am pleased, chief of brahmins," said Karna, "with the strength of your arms
in battle, with your courage, and with your training in arms and weapons. Are
you the science of the bow in person, best of brahmins, or Rama? Or are you
Indra of the bay horses himself, or Vishnu, Achyuta, himself? You have put on a
brahmin's form to hide yourself, and you rely on the strength of your arms to
fight me. For when I am angry in battle, no other man can fight me except
Shachi's husband himself, or the Pandava Kiritin."

When he said this, Phalguna answered him. "I am not the science of the bow,
Karna, and I am not mighty Rama. I am a brahmin, the best of fighters, the best
of all who carry weapons. By my teacher's teaching I am grounded in the Brahma
weapon and in Indra's weapon. I stand here today in battle to defeat you. Stand
firm, hero."

When he was told this, Radheya Karna, the great warrior, turned back from the
fight, thinking that the power of a brahmin could not be overcome.

Meanwhile Shalya and Vrikodara, king, fought there. Both were strong, and both
were maddened with rivalry and strength. Challenging each other like two great
elephants in rut, they struck each other with fists and knees, and for a while
they dragged each other about the field. Then Bhima, the best of the strong,
lifted Shalya in his arms and threw him down, and the brahmins laughed. And the
bull among men, Bhimasena, did a wonderful thing there: when strong Shalya had
fallen to the ground, the strong man did not kill him.

With Shalya thrown down by Bhimasena, and Karna afraid, all the kings grew
uneasy and surrounded Vrikodara. "Well done, these bulls among brahmins," they
said together. "Let us find out where they were born and where they live. For
who can fight Radha's son Karna in battle, except Rama, or Drona, or Kripa,
Sharadvat's son? Or Krishna, son of Devaki, or Phalguna, the tamer of enemies?
And who can fight Duryodhana in battle? Or Shalya, the king of the Madras, the
best of the strong, except the hero Baladeva, or the Pandava Vrikodara? Let us
draw back from this fight with the brahmins. When we have found out who they
are, we will fight them again."

Krishna, who had seen Bhima's deed, suspected that those two were Kunti's sons.
He held the kings back, persuading them all with the words, "She was won by
dharma."

So those kings, skilled in war, turned back from the fight, and all the best of
kings went back to their lodgings, amazed. Those who had gathered there went
away saying, "The arena has gone to the brahmins. The Panchala princess is
surrounded by brahmins."

Hidden among the brahmins, who wore deerskins, Bhimasena and Dhananjaya made
their way there with difficulty. Free at last from the press of people, wounded
by their enemies, the two heroes shone, and Krishna followed them.

Their mother, meanwhile, thought of every kind of disaster, as her sons did not
come and the time for alms went by. *Have the Dhartarashtras recognised the
bulls of the Kurus and killed them? Or fierce rakshasas, full of magic, bitter
enemies? Has even great Vyasa's word turned out the opposite?* So Pritha
thought, in her love for her sons.

Then, late in the afternoon, like the sun hidden by clouds, Jishnu came in with
the brahmins, with brahmins in front of him.

**Divergences.**

* **Karna is not defeated.** The vulgate has Arjuna cut Karna's bow, or a hundred bows, and
  Karna say "I am beaten" (\*1872–\*1876). In the CE Karna leaves the fight of his own accord,
  "thinking that the power of a brahmin could not be overcome" (1.181.21). See ledger L-95.
* The vulgate pairs off more fighters: Duryodhana against Yudhishthira, Shakuni against Nakula,
  Duhshasana against Sahadeva (\*1866). In the CE the twins have already gone home with
  Yudhishthira (1.179.21), and Duryodhana and the rest fight the brahmins "lightly" (1.181.8).
* The vulgate has Yudhishthira signal Bhima not to uproot another tree (App. I No. 99). Not in
  the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.180.3 *vṛddhakramam*: "the respect due to age", which Drupada is said not to deserve.
* 1.180.6: the kings' claim that "brahmins have no right to be chosen" is theirs. Yudhishthira
  answers it in the next chapters (1.185.23).
* 1.180.15–16: both brothers are "Partha" in the text. Bhima stands "near Partha, the bull among
  men".
* 1.180.18 *tālamātram*: "as tall as a palm tree".
* 1.180.19 *sahasā nivṛttaḥ*: "turned suddenly"; *sahasā*.
* 1.180.20: Krishna addresses Balarama as *acyuta* here.
* 1.181.3: "as snakes with mantras", *mantrair āśīviṣān iva*.
* 1.181.7 *vāśitāhetoḥ*: "for the sake of a cow elephant".
* 1.181.16 *harihayaḥ*: Indra "of the bay horses". *rāmaḥ*: Rama Jamadagnya.
* 1.181.24 *nyavadhīt*: "threw down". The next verse makes clear he did not kill him.
* 1.181.32 *dharmeṇa labdhā*: "she was won by dharma", Krishna's argument.
