# The Snake Sacrifice

"Tell me again, in full," said Shaunaka, "what King Janamejaya asked his
ministers about his father's going to heaven."

"Listen, brahmin," said the bard, "to how the king asked his ministers, and how
they all told him of Parikshit's death.

"'You know how my father lived,' said Janamejaya, 'and how, when his time came,
that famous man met his death. When I have heard from you the whole story of my
father, I will choose what is good, and never the opposite.'

"Then the ministers, who knew all of dharma, and were wise, answered King
Janamejaya, the great-souled one who had asked them.

"'Your father was righteous and great-souled, a protector of his subjects. Hear
how that great man lived here. He kept the four classes each in its own dharma,
and guarded them. He knew dharma, and he ruled by dharma, like dharma in bodily
form. Glorious and matchless in valour, he guarded the goddess, the earth. He
had no enemies, and he hated no one. He was even-handed to all beings, like the
lord of creatures. Brahmins, kshatriyas, vaishyas and shudras kept to their own
work, content, king, well governed by that king.

"'He supported widows and orphans, the poor and the maimed. He was a joy to all
creatures to look at, like a second moon. His people were content and well fed.
He was glorious, true in his word, firm in his courage. The king learned the
science of the bow from Sharadvata's son. And your father, Janamejaya, was dear
to Govinda, and dear to the whole world, a man of great fame.

"'When the Kurus had been all but destroyed, he was born to Uttara. So he became
Parikshit, "born when the line was wasted", the mighty son of Subhadra's son.
The king was skilled in the dharma and the business of kings, and he had every
virtue. He was master of his senses and of himself, wise, and he served his
elders. He knew the six means of statecraft, and he had a great mind, the best
of those who know policy and dharma. Your father protected these subjects for
sixty years. Then he met his appointed end, from a snake, which no one can
escape. Then you, best of men, received by dharma this kingdom, which has come
down in the Kuru line for a thousand years. You were born of noble blood, and
while you were still a child you became the protector of all beings.'

"'There has never been a king in this family,' said Janamejaya, 'who did not
work for his people's good and was not loved by them, especially when you look
at the great conduct of my grandfathers, who were devoted to good conduct. How
did my father, such a man, come to his death? Tell me as it happened. I want to
hear the truth.'

"When the king urged them like this, the ministers, who wanted only to please
and serve him, told the lord of men all that had happened.

"'Your father, king, always loved to hunt, as the fortunate Pandu did, the
finest bowman in battle. He left all the business of the kingdom to us. Once,
roaming the forest, he shot a deer with a feathered arrow, and after he had shot
it he followed the deer quickly into the thick forest, on foot, with his sword
at his side and his quiver of weapons on his back. Your father could not find
the lost deer in the thick forest.

"'He was tired, and old. He was sixty, and age was upon him, and he was hungry.
In the great forest he saw a sage nearby. The king of kings asked the sage, who
was keeping a vow of silence. The sage said nothing to him, though he asked.
Then the king, worn out with hunger and weariness, was suddenly overcome with
anger at the calm sage, who was standing like a post, keeping his vow of
silence. For the king did not know that the sage was keeping a vow of silence.
In his anger, your father insulted him. He lifted a dead snake from the ground
with the tip of his bow and laid it on the shoulder of that pure-hearted man,
best of the Bharatas. The wise man said nothing to him, good or bad. He stood
there just as he was, not angry, carrying the snake on his shoulder.'

---

"'Then the king, faint with hunger, left the snake on the sage's shoulder, king
of kings, and went back to his city. That seer had a son, born of a cow, named
Shringin, of great fame and great power, fierce in his strength and very quick
to anger. He had gone to Brahma and done him honour. When he was given leave and
went home, Shringin heard from a friend how your father had insulted his father.

"'He heard, tiger of the Kurus, that his father was carrying on his shoulder a
dead snake that your father had hung on him, though he had done no harm. The
sage was a great ascetic, king, the best of sages, master of his senses, pure,
and engaged in wonderful works. His whole being shone with austerity, and every
limb was under control. His conduct was good and his speech was good. He was
steady, free of greed, not mean, not spiteful, old, keeping a vow of silence, a
refuge to all creatures. And your father had wronged him.

"'When the seer's son heard this, he cursed your father in his anger. He was
only a boy, but he was greater than the elders, and full of power. Quickly he
touched water, and in his rage, blazing as if with fire, he spoke these words at
your father: "He who laid a dead snake on my teacher, who had done no wrong, the
naga Takshaka will destroy in his fury with his fire, that wicked man, seven
nights from now. See the power of my austerity!"

"'When he had said this, he went to where his father was. And when he saw his
father, he told him of the curse. And that tiger among sages sent word to your
father: "You have been cursed by my son. Be on your guard, lord of the earth.
Takshaka will destroy you with his fire, great king."

"'When your father heard those terrible words, Janamejaya, he was on his guard,
and he was very afraid of Takshaka, the best of snakes.

"'Then, when the seventh day came, the brahmin seer Kashyapa wanted to go to the
king. And Takshaka, the king of snakes, saw Kashyapa. The king of snakes said to
Kashyapa, who was hurrying along, "Where are you going in such a hurry? What do
you mean to do?"

"'"Where King Parikshit, the best of the Kurus, is going to be burned, they say,
by the snake Takshaka, brahmin," said Kashyapa, "there I am going, quickly, to
cure him of the poison at once. When I have come to him, no snake will harm
him."

"'"Why do you want to bring him back to life when I have bitten him?" said
Takshaka. "Tell me what you want, and I will give it to you today. Go back to
your own home."

"'"I am going there because I want wealth," said Kashyapa. And Takshaka spoke to
the great man gently, and honoured him: "As much wealth as you would ask from
that king, take more from me, sinless one, and turn back."

"'When the naga said this, Kashyapa, the best of men, received from Takshaka as
much wealth as he wanted, and turned back. When the brahmin had gone back,
Takshaka came in disguise, best of kings, to your righteous father, the king,
who was on his guard in his palace, and burned him with the fire of his poison.
Then you were consecrated for victory, tiger among men.

"'What we have seen and heard, best of kings, we have told you truly, all of it,
terrible as it is. Now that you have heard of the king's defeat, best of kings,
do what must be done next for this seer Uttanka too.'

"'I want to hear about this,' said Janamejaya, 'the talk between the king of
snakes and Kashyapa, in a forest where no one lives. Who saw it and heard it, so
that it came to your ears? When I have heard, I will set my mind on the
destruction of the snakes.'

"'Listen, king,' said the ministers, 'to how we were told long ago of the
meeting on the road between the lord of brahmins and the king of snakes. Before
they came, lord of the earth, a man looking for firewood had climbed that very
tree, a lord of the forest with dry branches. The snake and the brahmin did not
know that he was up in the tree. He was burned to ash along with the tree. By
the brahmin's power, king of kings, he was brought back to life with the lord of
the forest. He came to this city, best of kings, and told us everything that had
happened between Takshaka and the brahmin. This is what we have told you, king,
as it happened and as we heard it. Now that you have heard it, tiger among
kings, do as you wish.'

---

"When King Janamejaya heard his ministers' words, he was filled with pain and
sorrow, and he pressed his hands together, one against the other. The lotus-eyed
king sighed long and hot, again and again, and tears ran from his eyes without
stopping. And the lord of the earth, full of pain and grief, said, 'I have heard
what you say about my father's going to heaven. Now hear what I have firmly
decided.

"'I think that vengeance must be taken at once on the wicked Takshaka, who
killed my father. He carried out the word of the seer Shringin, and burned the
king. If that wicked one had only gone away, my father would surely be alive.
What would it have cost him if the king had lived, by Kashyapa's grace and the
good policy of his ministers? But in his folly he turned back the best of
brahmins, Kashyapa, who had come to bring back to life the king who had never
been defeated. This is a great crime of wicked Takshaka, that he gave the
brahmin wealth so that he would not bring the king back to life. To please
Uttanka, and to please myself greatly, and to please you all, I will take
revenge for my father.'

"When he had said this, and his ministers had agreed, the glorious king made a
vow to hold the snake sacrifice, brahmin, that tiger of the Bharatas, the son of
Parikshit.

"Then the lord of the earth called his family priest and his ritual priests, and
said to them, with well-chosen words, words that would bring his purpose about,
'Tell me how I can take revenge on wicked Takshaka, who killed my father. Do you
know a rite by which I can bring the snake Takshaka, with his kin, into a
blazing fire? As he burned my father before with the fire of his poison, so I
want to burn that wicked snake.'

"'There is a great sacrifice, king, that the gods made for you,' said the
priests. 'It is called the snake sacrifice, and it is told of in the old lore,
king. No one but you can hold that sacrifice, lord of men. So say those who know
the old lore. And we know that rite.'

"When they said this, the royal seer believed that the snake Takshaka had as
good as entered the blazing mouth of the fire, best of men. Then the king said
to those brahmins, who knew the mantras, 'I will hold that sacrifice. Let the
things for it be brought for me.'

"Then his priests, best of brahmins, measured out the ground for the place of
sacrifice by the rules of the science. They knew the lore rightly, and all of
them had gone far in wisdom. The place was richly furnished, pleasing, crowded
with brahmins, full of wealth and grain, and well laid out by the priests. When
they had built the place of sacrifice he wanted, by the rules, they consecrated
the king for the snake sacrifice.

"But before the snake sacrifice took place, something happened there, a great
sign that foretold a hindrance to the sacrifice. While the place of sacrifice
was being built, a builder spoke, a wise man, skilled in the science of
building. The master of the measuring cord, a suta who knew the old lore, said
this: 'Because of the place and the time at which this measuring was begun, this
rite will not be completed. A brahmin will be the cause.'

"When he heard this, the king, before his consecration, said to the doorkeeper,
'Let no one I do not know come in here.'

"Then the work of the snake sacrifice began, according to the rules, and the
sacrificing priests moved about in due order, each at his own task. They wore
black clothes, and their eyes were red from the smoke, and they poured offerings
with mantras into the blazing fire. Making the hearts of all the serpents
tremble, they called all the snakes there into the mouth of the fire.

"Then the snakes fell into the blazing fire, writhing and pitiful, calling to
one another. Quivering and hissing, others twined themselves together, and they
dropped head first and tail first into the bright fire. White ones, black ones
and dark blue ones, old ones and young ones, they fell into the blazing fire,
crying out with dreadful cries. So they perished helplessly, best of brahmins,
hundreds of thousands of snakes, and millions, and hundreds of millions.

"Some there were like rats, others like the trunks of elephants, and others huge
and mighty, like elephants mad in rut. Many, great and small, of many colours,
full of poison, terrible, like iron clubs, mighty biters, the serpents fell into
the fire, struck down by the rod of their mother's word."

**Choices.**

* **The story told a second time.** The ministers retell Parikshit's death
  (1.45–46), which the bard told in chapter 13 (1.36–40). The details differ
  a little: here the king is sixty (1.45.15, 23), Shringin is "born of a cow"
  (1.46.2), and Takshaka's test on the tree is replaced by a simple bargain.
  The novel keeps the ministers' version as they tell it.
* 1.45.11 *śāradvatasya*: "of Sharadvata's son", i.e. Kripa, who is not
  named here.
* 1.45.13 *parikṣīṇeṣu kuruṣu … parikṣit*: the name is explained from the
  wasting away (*parikṣīṇa*) of the Kurus. Rendered with a gloss.
* 1.45.15 *ṣaḍvarga*: the six means of statecraft. Kept as a phrase.
* 1.46.2 *gavi jātaḥ*: "born of a cow". The text gives no more, and neither
  does the novel.
* 1.46.25, 1.46.41: Uttanka is present and wants revenge on Takshaka (see
  chapter 5, where he urges the king to it at 1.3.177–195).
* 1.47.8: the king "thought Takshaka had entered the blazing fire's mouth".
  Rendered "had as good as entered".
* 1.47.15 *sūtradhāraḥ sūtaḥ paurāṇikaḥ*: the builder is "the holder of the
  measuring cord, a suta learned in the old lore".
* 1.47.16 *kṣattā*: the doorkeeper or chamberlain.
* 1.47.24 *indurāḥ*: "like rats (or mice)".
* 1.47.25 *mātṛvāgdaṇḍapīḍitāḥ*: "struck down by the rod of their mother's
  word". Kept.
