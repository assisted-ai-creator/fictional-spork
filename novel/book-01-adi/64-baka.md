# Baka

When Bhima had promised, "I will do it," Bharata, the Pandavas all came back
with the alms. Yudhishthira, Pandu's son, knew from his brother's face alone
what was afoot. He sat down alone with his mother and asked her in private,
"What is this deed that Bhima, of terrible courage, means to do? Does he want to
do it with your consent?"

"He will do it at my word," said Kunti, "this tamer of enemies. It is a great
deed, for a brahmin's sake, and to free the town."

"What rash, harsh, ill-done thing is this you have done?" said Yudhishthira.
"Good people do not praise giving up a son. How can you want to give up your own
son for someone else's? By giving up your son you have done what goes against
the ways of the world. We all sleep safely, sheltered by his arms. We hope to
win back the kingdom the wretches stole from us. Duryodhana cannot sleep a
single night, he and Shakuni, for grief when they think of his boundless
strength. By that hero's strength we were freed from the house of lac and other
evils, and Purochana was killed. Relying on his strength we think of this earth,
full of wealth, as already won, with Dhritarashtra's sons killed. What were you
thinking, to decide to give him up? Has your mind been swept away by troubles?
Have you lost your senses?"

"Yudhishthira," said Kunti, "do not worry about Wolf-belly. I have not made this
decision from weakness of mind. We have lived here happily, son, in the
brahmin's house. I have thought of this, son, as a way of repaying him. A man is
worth as much as the good done to him is not lost on him. I saw Bhima's great
courage then, at the house of lac, and in the killing of Hidimba, and I trust
Wolf-belly. The strength of Bhima's arms is great, equal to ten thousand
elephants. By it you, who are like elephants yourselves, were carried out of
Varanavata. No one has been or will be as strong as Wolf-belly. He could stand
up in battle to the best of them all, even to the wielder of the thunderbolt
himself. As soon as he was born, long ago, he fell from my lap onto the
mountain, and his body was so heavy that his limbs broke the rock to pieces. I
remembered Bhima's strength with good sense, Pandava, and so I made up my mind
to repay the brahmin.

"I have not decided this out of greed, nor ignorance, nor folly. I have made
this decision for dharma, knowing what I do. Two aims will be achieved,
Yudhishthira: we will repay our lodging, and a great act of dharma will be done.
A kshatriya who helps a brahmin in any of his affairs wins the fair worlds, so I
have heard. A kshatriya who saves a kshatriya from death wins great fame in this
world and the next. A kshatriya who helps a vaishya in battle will surely please
the people in all the worlds. And a king who saves a shudra who comes to him for
shelter is born here into a rich family, honoured by kings. So the blessed Vyasa
told me long ago, joy of the Kauravas, very wise as he is. That is why I want to
do this."

"What you have done, mother, is right," said Yudhishthira, "for you have thought
it out, and done it out of pity for a brahmin in distress. Bhima will surely
come back when he has killed the man-eater. But the brahmin must be told, and
made to take care, that the townspeople do not find out."

---

When the night was over, the Pandava, Bhimasena, took the food and went to where
the man-eater lived. When he reached the rakshasa's forest, the mighty Pandava
called out his name, and began to eat the food himself.

When the rakshasa heard Bhimasena's words, he came in a fury to where Bhima
stood. He was huge and fast, as if he would split the earth. He frowned so that
three lines stood on his brow, and bit his lip. When the rakshasa saw Bhimasena
eating the food, he opened his eyes wide in anger and said, "Who is this fool
eating the food meant for me, before my eyes? He wants to go to the house of
Yama!"

When Bhimasena heard this, he almost laughed, Bharata. He ignored the rakshasa
and went on eating, with his back turned. Then the man-eater gave a terrible
yell, raised both his arms and ran at Bhimasena to kill him. Even so,
Wolf-belly, the Pandava, the killer of enemy heroes, took no notice of the
rakshasa. He did not even look at him, and went on eating. Then the rakshasa,
full of fury, stood behind Kunti's son and struck him on the back with both
hands. But though the mighty one struck him hard with both hands, Bhima did not
even look at the rakshasa. He went on eating.

Then the mighty rakshasa, angrier than ever, took up a tree and ran at Bhima
again to strike him. Then Bhima, the bull among men, slowly finished the food,
rinsed his mouth with water, and stood up, pleased, ready for battle. When the
angry rakshasa threw the tree, mighty Bhima caught it with his left hand, almost
laughing, Bharata. Then the mighty rakshasa tore up trees of every kind again
and hurled them at Bhimasena, and the Pandava Bhima hurled them back. That
battle of trees between Baka and the Pandava was terrible, great king, and it
destroyed the forest.

Then Baka shouted his name, ran at the Pandava, and seized mighty Bhimasena in
his arms. Strong-armed Bhimasena threw his arms round the rakshasa too, and
though he writhed with all his great force, the mighty man dragged him along by
main strength. Dragged by Bhima and dragging the Pandava, the man-eater grew
terribly tired. The earth shook with their great force, and they smashed huge
trees to pieces.

When Wolf-belly saw the rakshasa weakening, bull of the Bharatas, he threw him
to the ground and beat him with his hands. Then he pressed his knee hard into
his back, and seized him by the neck with his right arm. With his left hand he
took hold of his clothes at the waist, and the Pandava doubled the rakshasa up,
while he bellowed terrible bellows. Then blood came out of his mouth, lord of
the people, as Bhima broke that terrible rakshasa.

---

Terrified by that noise, the rakshasa's people came running out of his house
with their servants, king. They were frightened and out of their wits, and
Bhima, the best of fighters, calmed them and made them agree to terms. "You must
never again harm men here. Those who do will be killed quickly, just like this."
When they heard him, Bharata, those rakshasas said to him, "So be it," and
accepted his terms. From then on the people of the town saw the rakshasas there
as gentle, Bharata.

Then Bhima took the lifeless man-eater, left him at the city gate, and went away
unseen. When he had killed him, Bhima went to the brahmin's house and told the
king everything that had happened, from beginning to end.

Early in the morning, people coming out of the town saw the rakshasa lying dead
on the ground, soaked in blood. He looked like a mountain peak, sprawled there,
terrifying. Others went to Ekachakra and gave the news. Then the townspeople
came by the thousand, king, with their women, their old people and their
children, to see Baka. They were all amazed at the more than human deed, and
they all worshipped their gods, lord of the people.

Then they worked out whose turn it had been that day to give the meal. When they
knew, they all came to the brahmin and asked him about it. When they asked him
again and again, that bull among brahmins, protecting the Pandavas, said to all
the townspeople:

"When I was ordered to give the food, and was weeping with my family, a brahmin
saw me, a mighty man, perfected in mantras. He asked me first about the town's
trouble. Then that best of brahmins comforted me, almost laughing, and said, 'I
will take this food to the wicked one. Do not be afraid for my sake.' That
strong man took the food and went to Baka's forest. It must have been he who did
this deed, for the good of the world."

Then all the brahmins and kshatriyas were amazed, and the vaishyas and shudras
rejoiced, and they held a festival to Brahman. Then all the country people came
to the town to see that great wonder. And the Parthas went on living there.

**Divergences.**

* **No tearing apart.** The vulgate has Bhima tear off Baka's limbs and head and throw
  them at the four gates (\*1664), and tell his mother he has eaten his fill for ten
  years (\*1668). In the CE he breaks Baka's back, leaves the whole body at the gate
  (1.152.6), and tells "the king", Yudhishthira, what happened (1.152.7).
* The vulgate adds Kunti's dream of Bhima's victory (\*1643) and verses promising that
  those who hear the story will be safe from rakshasas (\*1673). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.150.13: Kunti repeats her maxim from 1.145.14.
