# The Return to the City

When they had performed the last rites for Pandu, the great seers, who were like
gods, met together and took counsel.

"That great man, great in austerity, gave up his kingdom and his country," they
said, "and came here to practise austerity and take refuge with the ascetics.
King Pandu has gone from here to heaven. He has left in our keeping his sons,
just born, and his wife."

So those generous siddhas, who delighted in the good of all beings, talked it
over among themselves. They decided to go to the city named after the elephant,
taking Pandu's sons with them, to give the Pandavas to Bhishma and to
Dhritarashtra. At that very moment the ascetics all set out, taking with them
Pandu's wife and sons, and his body.

Kunti had always lived in comfort, and she loved her sons dearly. But now, on
the road, the long way seemed short to her. Before long the famous woman reached
Kurujangala and came to the Vardhamana gate of the city.

When the people in the City of the Elephant heard that thousands of charanas and
sages had come, they were amazed. When the sun had been up a little while, all
the townsfolk came out with their wives to see the ascetics, with dharma in
their minds. Crowds of women and crowds of kshatriyas came out in crowds of
carriages, and brahmins with their wives. There was a great press of vaishyas
and shudras too. No one felt envy. All had their minds on dharma.

Bhishma, son of Shantanu, came out, and Somadatta the Bahlika, and the royal
seer whose eyes were his wisdom, and Vidura the steward himself. Queen Satyavati
came out, and famous Kausalya, and Gandhari, surrounded by the royal ladies.
Dhritarashtra's sons came out, all hundred of them, led by Duryodhana, decked in
bright ornaments.

All the Kauravas and their family priests bowed their heads to the companies of
great seers, and sat down near them. The people of the city and the country also
bowed their heads to the ground, and sat down near them too. When Bhishma saw
that the whole crowd had fallen silent, he offered the kingdom and the country
to the great seers.

Then the eldest of them, a great seer with matted hair and a deerskin, rose. He
knew what the great seers had decided, and he spoke.

"The heir of the Kauravas, the king named Pandu, gave up the pleasures of desire
and went from here to Shatashringa. He was keeping a vow of celibacy, and by a
divine cause this son, Yudhishthira, was born to him from Dharma himself. And
the Wind gave that great king this son, Bhima, the mighty, the strongest of the
strong. This one, whose courage is truth, was born to Kunti from Indra, who is
called on by many. His crown will outshine all great archers. And these two, the
best of the Kurus, great archers, tigers among men, whom Madri bore from the
Ashvins, stand here too.

"Pandu, the famous, always devoted to dharma, living in the forest, raised up
again this line of his grandfathers. As he watched the birth of his sons, and
their growing, and their study of the Veda, Pandu's joy grew without end. He
kept to the way of the good, and he won sons. And Pandu went to the world of the
ancestors seventeen days ago. When she saw him laid on the pyre and offered into
the mouth of the fire, Madri gave up her life and entered the fire. She has gone
with him to her husband's world, true to her vow. Now let the rites for her and
for him be done. Here are the bodies of the two of them, and here are their fine
sons. Let the tamers of enemies and their mother be received with honour. When
the funeral rites are over, let Pandu, the famous upholder of the Kuru line, who
knew every dharma, have the rite for the ancestors."

When they had said this to all the Kurus, the charanas and the guhyakas all
vanished in a moment, while the Kurus watched. The companies of seers and
siddhas vanished there like a city of the gandharvas, and the Kurus were utterly
amazed.

---

"Vidura," said Dhritarashtra, "have all the funeral rites performed for Pandu,
that lion among kings, as for a king, and above all for Madri too. Give cattle,
clothes, jewels and wealth of every kind for Pandu and Madri, to whoever wants
it and as much as they want. And see that Madri is honoured as Kunti would
honour her. Let her be so well covered that neither the wind nor the sun can see
her. Pandu, the sinless king, is not to be grieved for. He is to be praised.
Five heroic sons were born to him, like the sons of gods."

Vidura said, "So be it," Bharata, and with Bhishma he had Pandu's rites
performed in a place well shut in. Then the royal family priests quickly brought
blazing fires out of the city, with offerings of ghee going before them. They
decked a litter with the flowers of the season, with fragrances and with fine
garlands of every kind, and covered it all over with cloth. When it was decked
with garlands and costly cloths, the ministers, the kinsmen and the friends
gathered round it. They carried that lion among men, well covered, with Madri,
on a splendid carriage drawn by men and richly adorned. They decked it with a
white parasol and a yak-tail fan and the sound of every kind of music. And men
took jewels by the hundred and gave them to all who asked, for Pandu's last
rites. They brought fine white parasols, great ones, and beautiful clothes, for
the Kaurava's sake. Priests in white robes went ahead of him with the sacred
fires, blazing and well adorned, pouring offerings into them.

Brahmins, kshatriyas, vaishyas and shudras by the thousand followed the king,
weeping and burning with grief. "Where is our king going," they cried, "leaving
us and giving us endless sorrow, the best of protectors, leaving us without a
protector?"

All the Pandavas were weeping aloud, and Bhishma and Vidura. In a lovely stretch
of woodland on the bank of the Ganga, on smooth, fair ground, those truthful men
set down the litter of Pandu, the lion among men, whose deeds were never harsh,
and his wife.

Then they quickly sprinkled his body with water from golden pots. It had been
treated with every fragrance, smeared with pure kaliyaka paste and scented with
the finest bathing oils. They anointed him with the best white sandal, mixed
with black aloe and tunga juice. Then they dressed him in white cloths of the
country. Wrapped in the cloths, the tiger among men shone as though he were
alive, fit for a costly bed. When the priests who performed the rites for the
dead gave leave, they sprinkled the king with ghee, adorned him with Madri
beside him, and burned them with fragrant sandal mixed with tunga and padmaka,
and with many other perfumes in plenty.

When she saw the two bodies, Kausalya was overcome. "Alas, my son!" she cried,
and she fell suddenly to the ground. When the people of the city and the country
saw her fallen in her grief, they all wept aloud, full of pity and of love for
their king. All creatures cried out, as if worn out with the noise of grief, the
animals with the men. Bhishma, son of Shantanu, wise Vidura and all the Kauravas
cried aloud in deep sorrow.

Then Bhishma, Vidura and the king with his kinsmen offered water to him, and so
did all the Kuru women. When they had offered water, all the subjects, grieving,
took the Pandavas, worn thin with sorrow, and stood round them, king. As the
Pandavas lay on the ground with their kinsmen, so the townsfolk, brahmins and
all, lay on the ground too, king. For twelve nights, down to the children, the
city and the Pandavas were joyless, unwell and without cheer.

**The CE's own puzzles.**

* **Two burnings.** At 1.116.31 Madri mounts Pandu's pyre on Shatashringa, and the
  seers' speech agrees (1.117.28). But the seers bring "his body" to the city
  (1.117.6), speak of "the bodies of the two of them" (1.117.30), and in 1.118 both
  are burned again by the Ganga. The CE leaves this as it is, and so does the novel.
* **"Seventeen days."** Pandu died "seventeen days ago" (1.117.27), yet the long
  journey from the Himalaya "seemed short" to Kunti (1.117.7).

**Choices.**

* 1.117.1 *avabhṛtha*: the closing bath of a rite, here the last rites on the
  mountain.
* 1.117.3 *upanidhi*: "a deposit", something left in trust.
* 1.117.13 *prajñācakṣuḥ*: "whose eyes are his wisdom", Dhritarashtra.
* 1.117.19: the seer is unnamed. The vulgate gives the speech to Shuka (\*1267).
* 1.117.21 *brahmacaryavratasthasya … divyena hetunā*: "while he kept the vow of
  celibacy, by a divine cause". The seer's own explanation.
* 1.117.23 *kirīṭī* (printed *kīritr*): Arjuna, "the Crowned".
* 1.117.33 *gandharvanagara*: "a city of the gandharvas", a mirage.
* 1.118.3: "so covered that neither wind nor sun could see her" is the text's.
* 1.118.18–22: the funeral substances (*kālīyaka*, *tuṅgarasa*, *padmaka*, black
  aloe) are named as in the text.
* 1.118.23 *sahasā*: "suddenly". Kausalya is Pandu's mother.
