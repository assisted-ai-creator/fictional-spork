# Bhima Keeps Watch

When the night was over, all the people of the town came hurrying to see the
sons of Pandu. As they put out the fire, they saw that the house of lac had
burned down, and the minister Purochana with it. "This was surely done by the
wicked Duryodhana to destroy the Pandavas," the people cried. "There is no doubt
Dhritarashtra's son burned Pandu's heirs with Dhritarashtra's knowledge, for he
did not stop him. Surely Bhishma, son of Shantanu, does not follow dharma, nor
Drona, nor Vidura, nor Kripa, nor the other Kauravas. Let us send word to the
wicked Dhritarashtra: 'Your great wish has come true. You have burned the
Pandavas.'"

Then, as they cleared away the fire looking for the Pandavas, they saw the
innocent Nishada woman burned, with her five sons. And the same miner, while
cleaning the house, had covered the tunnel with earth, so that those men did not
see it.

Then the townspeople sent word to Dhritarashtra that the Pandavas had been
burned in the fire, and the minister Purochana. When King Dhritarashtra heard
that terrible news, the death of Pandu's sons, he wept in deep grief. "Today
King Pandu has died, my brother, so hard to find, now that those heroes have
been burned, and their mother with them. Let men go quickly to the town of
Varanavata. Let them give due honour to those heroes and to the daughter of King
Kuntibhoja. Let them build fine large pools for them. And let the friends of
those who died there honour them too. Whatever good I can have done, now that
this has happened, for the Pandavas and for Kunti, let all of it be done, with
wealth."

When he had said this, Dhritarashtra, Ambika's son, surrounded by his kinsmen,
offered water to the sons of Pandu. All the Kauravas cried aloud, lost in grief.
But Vidura grieved only a little, for he knew better.

---

The Pandavas, meanwhile, came out of the town of Varanavata and went fast, king,
heading south. They found their way by the stars in the night, facing south, and
with great effort, king, they reached a deep forest. Then, tired, thirsty and
blind with sleep, the sons of Pandu spoke again to mighty Bhimasena.

"What could be harder than this? We are in a deep forest, we do not know which
way to go, and we cannot go on. And we do not know whether that wicked Purochana
was burned. How can we escape this danger without being seen? Carry us again,
Bharata, as before. You alone are strong, like the ever-moving wind."

When the king of dharma said this to him, mighty Bhimasena took up Kunti and his
brothers and went on quickly. As he strode on fast, the wind stirred by the
force of his thighs blew, king, as it blows at the start of the hot months. He
crushed the trees in flower and in fruit, and tore up the wooded thickets near
his path. So, breaking the trees, he went on with boundless strength, and at his
speed the Pandavas nearly fainted. Many times he crossed rivers with far banks,
using his arms as rafts, and on the way they went in hiding, for fear of
Dhritarashtra's sons. With difficulty he carried his mother alone, delicate and
famous, on his back, over rough river banks.

In the evening the bulls of the Bharatas came to a stretch of forest with few
roots, fruits or water, full of cruel birds and beasts, a terrible place. A
fearful dusk came on. The beasts and birds were fierce. The directions were
dark, and unseasonable winds blew. The Kuru princes were worn out with weariness
and thirst, and overcome with sleep, and they could go no further.

Then Bhima went into the vast, terrible, empty forest and came to a lovely
banyan tree with wide shade. There he set them all down, and the bull of the
Bharatas said, "I will look for water here. Rest. These water birds, the cranes,
are calling sweetly. I am sure there is a great lake here."

"Go," said his eldest brother, Bharata, and he went where the water birds were
calling. He drank water there, and bathed, bull of the Bharatas, and he brought
water back in his upper cloth, king. He came quickly back to his mother from a
distance of two miles. When he saw his mother and his brothers asleep on the
ground, Wolf-belly was stricken with grief and wept.

"They could not sleep before on costly beds in Varanavata, and now they sleep on
the ground. Look at Kunti, lying on the earth today, the sister of Vasudeva, the
crusher of hosts of enemies, the daughter of Kuntibhoja, honoured for every
lucky mark, the daughter-in-law of Vichitravirya, the wife of great Pandu. She
was always used to a bed in a palace, as radiant as the inside of a lotus. She
is the most delicate of women, fit for the costliest bed. Look at her lying here
on the ground, where she does not belong. She bore these sons to Dharma, to
Indra and to the Wind, and she lies here on the ground, worn out, where she does
not belong. What sadder sight could I see than this, when I see these tigers
among men sleeping on the ground today?

"The king, who knows dharma, deserves to rule the three worlds. How can he lie
here on the ground, worn out, like a common man? This one, dark as a rain cloud,
has no equal among men on earth, and he sleeps on the ground like a common man.
What could be sadder than this? These two, who are like the Ashvins among the
gods for beauty, are asleep today on the earth like common men.

"The man who has no crooked kinsmen to shame his family lives happily in the
world, like a single tree in a village. A single tree in a village, with leaves
and fruit, becomes a shrine, and since it has no kin, it is worshipped and
honoured. And those who have many brave kinsmen who keep to dharma live happily
in the world, free from sickness. Strong and prosperous, delighting their
friends and kin, they live leaning on one another, like trees that grow in a
forest. But we have been driven out by wicked Dhritarashtra and his son, and
only just escaped burning at his command. We have escaped the fire and taken
shelter under this tree. We have come to the utmost hardship. Which way shall we
go?

"I can see a town not far from this forest. They are asleep, and someone must
keep awake. I will keep awake myself. They will drink the water afterwards, when
they wake and their weariness has gone."

So Bhima made up his mind, and he kept watch himself.

**Divergences.**

* **No boat from Vidura.** In the popular story Vidura sends a boatman with a boat
  "fitted with engines and flags" to take them across the Ganga, with a password from
  his riddle (App. I No. 85; G 1.143, 1.151). In the CE Bhima simply carries them, and
  crosses rivers "using his arms as rafts" (1.138.4). See ledger L-88.
* **No secret joy.** The vulgate says Dhritarashtra rejoiced inwardly while weeping
  outwardly, "like a deep pool in summer, cold within and hot without" (\*1488). The CE
  says only that he wept in deep grief (1.137.10). See ledger L-89.
* The vulgate adds long laments by Bhishma (App. I No. 86) and by Kunti, who says she
  can go no further (\*1503–\*1506). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.137.13 *kulyāni*: "pools", or possibly funeral rites for the family; the word is
  uncertain. Rendered "pools".
* 1.137.16 *viduras tv alpaśaś cakre śokaṃ veda paraṃ hi saḥ*: "Vidura grieved only a
  little, for he knew more".
* 1.138.1 *śuciśukrāgame*: "at the coming of the months Shuchi and Shukra", the hot
  season.
* 1.138.4 *bhujaplavaiḥ*: "with arm-rafts".
* 1.138.8 *kauravyāḥ*: "the Kauravas", here the Pandavas, as descendants of Kuru.
  Rendered "the Kuru princes" to avoid confusion.
* 1.138.14 *gavyūti*: a measure of about two miles.
* 1.138.16 *svasāraṃ vasudevasya*: Kunti is Vasudeva's sister (1.104.1).
* 1.138.22: "this one, dark as a rain cloud" is Arjuna.
* 1.138.24–27: the image of the lone village tree and the forest trees is Bhima's.
