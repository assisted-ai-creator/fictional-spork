# Angaraparna

While the great Pandavas were living in hiding, Vyasa, Satyavati's son, came to
see them. When they saw him coming, the tamers of enemies went to meet him,
bowed down and greeted him, and stood before him with joined hands. The sage
told them all to be seated. Pleased at being honoured by the Parthas, he spoke
to them kindly.

"Are you living by dharma and by the books, tamers of enemies? Do you honour
brahmins who deserve honour?"

When the blessed seer had spoken words full of dharma and good sense, and told
them many wonderful stories, he spoke again.

"There was once, in a forest of austerities, the daughter of a great seer. She
was slender-waisted, with lovely hips and fine brows, and she had every good
quality. But because of her own deeds she was unlucky. Though she was beautiful
and good, she found no husband. So, unhappy, she began to practise austerity to
win a husband. By her fierce austerity, so they say, she pleased Shankara.

"The blessed god was pleased with her, and said to the ascetic girl, 'Choose a
boon, bless you, lovely one. I am the giver of boons.' Then she said to the lord
what she thought was good for her: 'I want a husband with every good quality.'
And she said it again and again.

"Then Ishana, the best of speakers, answered her, 'You will have five husbands,
good lady.' She answered Shankara, 'Give me one husband.' But the god spoke
again, with these fine words: 'Five times you said to me, "Give me a husband."
When you have gone into another body, it will be as you said.'

"That girl, lovely as a goddess, has been born in Drupada's family. Krishna,
Prishata's blameless daughter, is appointed to be your wife. So go into the city
of the Panchalas, mighty ones. When you have won her, you will surely be happy."

When he had said this, the great and fortunate grandfather of the Pandavas took
leave of the Parthas and of Kunti, and the great ascetic went away.

---

The bulls among men set out with their mother in front, going north by level
roads, as they had been told. Travelling day and night, the tigers among men,
the sons of Pandu, came to Somashravayana, a holy bathing place on the Ganga.
Dhananjaya went ahead of them, holding up a torch, to light the way and to guard
them.

There, in the lovely waters of the Ganga, in a lonely place, the jealous king of
the gandharvas had come to play in the water with his women. He heard the noise
of them coming down to the river, and at the sound the mighty one flew into a
rage. When he saw the Pandavas there with their mother, the tamers of enemies,
he twanged his terrible bow and said:

"The fearful dusk that reddens at the start of the night, except for eighty
moments, is the hour set aside for yakshas, gandharvas and rakshasas, who go
where they please. The rest belongs to men to go where they like. Men who go
about at those hours out of greed, we attack and seize, with the rakshasas, the
fools. So men who know the Veda blame all men who come to the water at night,
even strong men and kings. Keep away. Do not come near me. Why do you not know
that I have come to the water of the Bhagirathi? Know that I am the gandharva
Angaraparna, who relies on his own strength. I am proud and jealous, and a dear
friend of Kubera. This forest is called Angaraparna after me, and here along the
Ganga I live happily. Neither corpses nor horned beasts, neither gods nor men
come near this place. So why are you coming near?"

"Fool," said Arjuna, "on the ocean, on the flanks of the Himalaya, and on this
river, by night, by day or at dusk, who has any claim of ownership? We have
strength, and we are not afraid of you out of season. It is the weak who worship
you at your cruel hour. This Ganga came out of the golden peak of the Himalaya
long ago, and when she reaches the ocean she becomes seven streams. She becomes
one stream again, pure, and goes through the sky, and among the gods, gandharva,
the Ganga becomes the Alakananda. And among the ancestors, gandharva, the Ganga
becomes the Vaitarani, hard for sinners to cross, as Dvaipayana said. The river
of the gods is open to all, holy, the way to heaven. How can you want to bar
her? That is not the eternal dharma. How can we be kept by your word from
touching the holy water of the Bhagirathi as we please, when no one may stop it
and it is open to all?"

When Angaraparna heard this, he flew into a rage, bent his bow, and let loose
blazing arrows like poisonous snakes. Whirling the torch quickly, and with his
fine shield, Dhananjaya the Pandava turned all his arrows aside.

"This is a scare for those who do not know weapons, gandharva," said Arjuna.
"Used against those who know weapons, it melts away like foam. I see that you
gandharvas are all greater than men, gandharva. So I will fight with a divine
weapon, not with magic. Brihaspati, the son of Indra's teacher, gave this fire
weapon long ago to Bharadvaja, gandharva. From Bharadvaja it went to Agniveshya,
and from Agniveshya to my teacher. And he, Drona, the best of brahmins, gave it
to me."

When he had said this, the Pandava, in anger, let loose on the gandharva the
blazing fire weapon, and it burned up his chariot. The mighty gandharva, thrown
from his chariot, stunned by the power of the weapon, was falling head first.
Dhananjaya caught him by his hair, wreathed with garlands, and dragged him,
senseless from the weapon, towards his brothers.

Then his wife, named Kumbhinasi, came to Yudhishthira for shelter, wanting to
save her husband. "Save me, great king," said the gandharvi, "and set my husband
free. I am a gandharvi named Kumbhinasi, and I have come to you for shelter,
lord."

"Beaten in battle, stripped of his fame, protected by a woman, without courage,"
said Yudhishthira, "who would kill such an enemy? Let him go, killer of
enemies."

"Take your life, gandharva," said Arjuna. "Go, and do not grieve. Yudhishthira,
king of the Kurus, grants you safety today."

"I am beaten," said the gandharva. "I give up my old name, Angaraparna, Leaf of
Embers. I will not boast of my strength now, nor of my name, in any gathering of
people. I have made a good gain, meeting one who carries divine weapons. I
wanted to fight him with gandharva magic, when he is so much the better. My fine
painted chariot has been burned by the fire of his weapon. So I, who was
Chitraratha, Painted Chariot, have become Dagdharatha, Burned Chariot, by name.

"There is a knowledge I gathered here long ago by austerity. I will give it
today to the great man who gave me my life. For the man who holds back an enemy
by force, beats him, and then, when he comes for shelter, gives him back his
life, what good does he not deserve? This knowledge is called Chakshushi, the
Seeing. Manu gave it to Soma, Soma gave it to Vishvavasu, and Vishvavasu gave it
to me. Given by a teacher, it perishes if it passes to a coward. I have told you
where it came from. Now hear what it can do. Whatever a man wants to see with
his eyes in the three worlds, he can see it, and see it as he wishes. A man who
stands on one foot for six months may win this knowledge. But I will bring it to
you myself, without your keeping the vow. By this knowledge, king, we are
greater than men, and by the power it gives we are not less than the gods.

"And I will give each of your five brothers, best of men, a hundred horses born
of the gandharvas. They carry gods and gandharvas. They have heavenly scents and
go as fast as thought. Even when they grow thin, they lose none of their speed.
Long ago Indra's thunderbolt was made to kill Vritra. It broke into ten, into a
hundred pieces on Vritra's head. Then the gods divided it into parts, and each
part of the thunderbolt is honoured. Whatever gets a thing done in the world is
called a body of the thunderbolt. The brahmin's thunderbolt is in his hand. The
kshatriya's is his chariot. The vaishyas' thunderbolt is giving, and the lowest
class's is work. The kshatriya's thunderbolt is his horses, and horses are said
not to be killed. The mare gives birth to the chariot's limb, and those counted
among horses are the sutas. These horses born of the gandharvas take any colour
and any speed, and come at will. They will fulfil your wishes."

"If a gift is given out of affection," said Arjuna, "or when a man's life is at
risk, whether knowledge, wealth or learning, I do not want it, gandharva."

"Friendship is seen to bring joy when people meet," said the gandharva. "I give
you the knowledge because I am glad you gave me my life. And I will take from
you the fine fire weapon, and your friendship too, for a long time, Bibhatsu,
bull of the Bharatas."

"In exchange for the weapon, I choose the horses," said Arjuna. "Let our
friendship last for ever. And tell me, friend gandharva, why men need fear you."

**Divergences.**

* **The girl is unnamed.** In the CE the woman of Draupadi's former life is "a seer's
  daughter" (1.157.6). The name "Nalayani" given her in later retellings is in neither
  the CE nor Ganguli. See ledger L-93. The fuller story of Draupadi's former life is told
  again at 1.189.

**Choices.**

* 1.157.13 *pañcakṛtvaḥ*: "five times" she said "give me a husband". Shiva's reasoning is
  his.
* 1.158.2 *somaśravāyaṇam*: the bathing place.
* 1.158.7 *aśītibhis truṭair hīnam*: "less eighty *truṭi*s", tiny units of time. Rendered
  "except for eighty moments".
* 1.158.14 *kuṇapāḥ*: "corpses", perhaps meaning corpse-eaters; *śṛṅgiṇaḥ*, "horned
  beasts". Kept literal.
* 1.158.17–19: Arjuna's account of the three Gangas, on earth, in heaven (Alakananda) and
  among the ancestors (Vaitarani), is his, "as Dvaipayana said".
* 1.158.26: the line of the fire weapon, Brihaspati to Bharadvaja to Agniveshya to Drona,
  is Arjuna's.
* 1.158.37: *Citraratha*, "Painted Chariot", and *Dagdharatha*, "Burned Chariot".
* 1.158.40 *cākṣuṣī*: "the Seeing", a knowledge.
* 1.158.47–50: the gandharva's lore of the thunderbolt's parts and of horses is obscure
  in places. It is given as closely as the text allows.
* 1.158.52: Arjuna refuses gifts given "out of affection or when a life is at risk".
