# King of Anga

The people made way for him, their eyes wide with wonder, and Karna, the
conqueror of enemy cities, came into the great arena. He wore the armour he was
born with, and his face was lit by his earrings. He carried his bow and had his
sword belted on, and he moved like a mountain on foot. He was the child Pritha
had borne as a girl, famous and wide-eyed, a part of the hot-rayed Sun. Karna,
the destroyer of hosts of enemies, had the strength and courage of a lion, a
bull and a lord of elephants. In brilliance, in beauty and in splendour he was
like the sun, the moon and fire. He was tall, like a golden palm tree, young,
with a lion's frame, glorious, with virtues beyond counting, the Sun's own son.

The strong-armed man looked all round the ring. Then he bowed to Drona and
Kripa, but without much respect. The whole gathering stood still, staring. *Who
is this?* they thought, shaken and very curious.

Then, in a voice as deep as a cloud, the best of speakers, the son of the Sun,
spoke to his brother, Indra's son, whom he did not know as his brother.

"Partha, I will do every feat you have done, and better, while the people watch.
Do not be so amazed at yourself."

Before he had finished speaking, best of speakers, the people everywhere leaped
up at once, as if thrown up by a machine. Joy touched Duryodhana, tiger among
men. Shame and anger filled Bibhatsu in a moment.

Then, with Drona's leave, the mighty Karna, who always loved a fight, did all
that Partha had done there. Then Duryodhana and his brothers embraced Karna
gladly, Bharata, and said, "Welcome, strong-armed one! You have come by good
fortune, giver of honour. Enjoy me and the kingdom of the Kurus as you wish."

"I have done all I set out to do," said Karna. "I choose your friendship. And I
want to fight Partha in single combat, Bharata."

"Enjoy the pleasures of life with me," said Duryodhana. "Do your kinsmen good,
and put your foot on the heads of all your enemies, tamer of enemies."

Then Partha, feeling himself insulted, spoke to Karna, who stood among the crowd
of brothers like a mountain. "Karna, the worlds that go to men who come
uninvited and speak uninvited, you will go to, when I have killed you."

"This arena is open to everyone," said Karna. "What is it to you, Phalguna?
Kshatriyas are best by courage, and dharma follows strength. Why these insults,
the comfort of the weak? Speak with arrows, Bharata, till today, in front of
your teacher, I take your head with my arrows."

Then, with Drona's leave, Partha, the conqueror of enemy cities, quickly
embraced by his brothers, went forward to fight him. And Karna, embraced by
Duryodhana and his brothers, stood ready for battle, gripping his bow and arrow.

Then the sky was covered with clouds, with lightning and thunder, with rainbows
racing before them, and with lines of white cranes like laughter. Then the Sun,
seeing Indra looking down lovingly on the arena, drove away the clouds that had
come near. So the Pandava was seen hidden in the shadow of the cloud, and Karna
was seen bathed in sunlight.

Dhritarashtra's sons stood on the side where Karna was. Bharadvaja's son, Kripa
and Bhishma stood where Partha was. The arena was divided in two, and so were
the women. Kuntibhoja's daughter understood what was happening, and she fainted.
Vidura, who knew every dharma, revived Kunti as she lay in a faint, sprinkling
her with water mixed with sandal. When her breath came back to her, she saw her
two sons in their armour, and she was in anguish. She could do nothing at all.

As the two stood with their great bows raised, Kripa, the son of Sharadvat,
spoke. He knew the rules of single combat and every dharma.

"This is Pritha's younger son, the son of Pandu, a Kaurava. He will fight you in
single combat. You too, strong-armed one, must tell us your mother and your
father, and the family of kings you are the pride of. When he knows who you are,
Partha will fight you, or not."

When Karna was told this, his face bowed down with shame, like a lotus bruised
and drooping under the rain.

"Teacher," said Duryodhana, "the books lay down three sources of kingship: to be
born of a royal family, to be a hero, and to lead an army. If Phalguna will not
fight in battle with one who is not a king, then I will consecrate this man king
of the land of Anga."

Then and there, at that very moment, the great warrior Karna, glorious and
mighty, was consecrated king of Anga by men who knew the mantras, on a golden
throne, with golden pots of water, parched grain and flowers. With a parasol and
a yak-tail fan, amid cries of victory, the king, the bull among men, said to the
Kaurava king, "What can I give you, tiger among kings, to match this gift of a
kingdom? Tell me, king, and I will do it."

"I want your friendship for ever," said Suyodhana.

"So be it," Karna answered. And the two of them embraced in their joy, and were
very happy.

---

Then Adhiratha came into the arena. His upper cloth had slipped, he was sweating
and trembling, and he leaned on his staff, as if calling out. When Karna saw
him, he put down his bow. Bound by reverence for his father, he bowed his head
to him, his head still wet from the consecration. The charioteer hurriedly
covered his feet with the end of his cloth and said, "My son!", as if that said
everything. Trembling with love, he embraced him, and wet his head again, still
wet from the consecration as king of Anga, with his tears.

When he saw him, the Pandava Bhimasena decided that Karna was a suta's son. He
said to him, as if laughing, "Suta's son, you do not deserve to die in battle at
Partha's hands. Quickly, pick up a whip, as suits your family. And you do not
deserve to enjoy the kingdom of Anga, lowest of men, any more than a dog
deserves the sacrificial cake that lies near the fire at a sacrifice."

When Karna heard this, his lower lip trembled a little. He sighed, and looked up
at the Sun in the sky.

Then mighty Duryodhana leaped up in anger from among his brothers, like a
rutting elephant from a bed of lotuses. He said to Bhimasena, of terrible deeds,
as he stood there, "Wolf-belly, you should not say such things. Strength is the
chief thing for kshatriyas. Even a low-born kshatriya must be fought. The
sources of heroes, like the sources of rivers, are hard to know, so they say.
The fire that fills all that moves and all that stands rose from water. The
thunderbolt that kills the danavas was made from Dadhicha's bones. The blessed
god Guha, who holds all secrets, is said to be the son of Fire, the son of the
Krittikas, the son of Rudra and the son of Ganga. There are famous brahmins born
of kshatriya women. Our teacher was born from a pot, and Kripa, our master, from
a clump of reeds. And how you yourselves were born, kings have heard too.

"How could a doe give birth to a tiger like the sun, with earrings and armour,
marked with divine signs? This lord of men deserves to rule the whole earth, not
just Anga, by the strength of his arms and with me to follow his orders. If
there is any man who cannot bear what I have done, let him get into his chariot,
or on foot, and bend his bow."

Then a great uproar went up from the whole arena, mixed with cries of approval.
And the sun set.

Then Duryodhana took Karna by the hand, king, and went out of the arena, lit by
torchlight. And the Pandavas, with Drona and Kripa and Bhishma, all went to
their own homes, lord of the people. As the people left, some cried "Arjuna!",
some "Karna!", and some "Duryodhana!", Bharata.

Kunti had recognised her son, the lord of Anga, from the divine signs on him,
and in her love a hidden joy grew in her. And Duryodhana's fear of Arjuna
quickly vanished, king, now that he had Karna. The hero, who had worked so hard
at arms, spoke to Suyodhana in the friendliest way. And even Yudhishthira came
to think that there was no archer on earth equal to Karna.

**Divergences.**

* **Karna's shame, not his challenge, stops the fight.** In the CE the duel never
  begins. Kripa's question about his lineage bows Karna's head (1.126.33); Duryodhana
  answers by crowning him (1.126.35–36); then Adhiratha's arrival lets Bhima taunt him
  (1.127.5–7); and night falls (1.127.18).
* The vulgate adds a list of the virtues of the four classes to Karna's retort
  (\*1421) and a rite with ten thousand cows (\*1424). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.126.3 *kanyāgarbhaḥ*: "the child of a girl", i.e. born to Pritha before marriage.
* 1.126.8 *bhrātā bhrātaram ajñātam*: "a brother to his unknown brother". The
  narrator says what neither knows.
* 1.126.31 *kanīyān*: "the younger" son of Pritha. Kripa means younger than his
  brothers; the reader knows he is also Karna's younger brother.
* 1.126.18: Arjuna's taunt refers to the worlds (after death) of those who come and
  speak uninvited.
* 1.126.24 *harihayam*: Indra, "whose horses are bay". The Sun drives off Indra's
  clouds so that his own son stands in sunlight.
* 1.126.34: the three sources of kingship are Duryodhana's argument.
* 1.127.1 *yaṣṭiprāṇaḥ*: "whose life hung on his staff", i.e. leaning on it, old.
* 1.127.3 *paripūrṇārtham*: "with full meaning".
* 1.127.11 *kṣatrabandhunā*: "by a mere kshatriya", a low-ranked one. Rendered
  "even a low-born kshatriya".
* 1.127.13 *guhaḥ*: Skanda, whose several parentages Duryodhana lists.
* 1.127.14: "how you yourselves were born" is Duryodhana's jab at the Pandavas' birth
  from gods.
* 1.127.22 *channā prītiḥ*: Kunti's "hidden joy".
