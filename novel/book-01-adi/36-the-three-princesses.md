# The Three Princesses

When the wedding was over, King Shantanu settled the lovely girl in his house.
Then a wise son of Shantanu was born to Satyavati, the hero named Chitrangada,
who surpassed all men in courage. And the mighty lord fathered on Satyavati
another son, a great archer, King Vichitravirya. Before the boy had reached his
youth, bull of the Bharatas, wise King Shantanu went the way of Time.

When Shantanu had gone to heaven, Bhishma, keeping to Satyavati's wishes, set
Chitrangada, the tamer of enemies, on the throne. In his pride of courage
Chitrangada insulted all the kings, for he thought no man his equal. As he
insulted gods, men and asuras alike, the mighty king of the gandharvas, who had
the same name, came against him. A very great battle took place between them at
Kurukshetra. Those two mighty ones, the chief of the gandharvas and the chief of
the Kurus, fought on the bank of the river Hiranvati. The fight went on for
three years. The struggle was wild, thick with the rain of weapons. In the end
the gandharva, who was the stronger in magic, killed the hero, the best of the
Kurus. He brought to his end Chitrangada, the best of the Kurus, with his many
arrows and his bow. Then the gandharva went up to heaven.

That tiger among kings, of great splendour, was dead. Bhishma, the son of
Shantanu, had the funeral rites performed. Then the strong-armed one consecrated
Vichitravirya, still a boy and not yet grown, as king of the Kurus.
Vichitravirya, keeping to Bhishma's word, ruled from the seat of his father and
grandfather. Skilled in the books of dharma, the king honoured Bhishma, son of
Shantanu, by dharma, and Bhishma protected him.

---

With Chitrangada dead and his brother still a boy, Bhishma ruled the kingdom,
keeping to Satyavati's wishes. When he saw that his brother, the wisest of the
wise, had come to his youth, Bhishma set his mind on Vichitravirya's marriage.

Then Bhishma heard that the three daughters of the king of Kashi, as lovely as
apsarases, were all choosing husbands together at a bridegroom choice. So with
his mother's leave, the best of chariot fighters put on his armour. He went in a
single chariot to the city of Varanasi. There Bhishma, the joy of Shantanu, saw
the kings who had come together from every side, and he saw the girls.

The names of the kings were being called out by the thousand. Then the lord
Bhishma himself chose the girls. Bhishma, the best of fighters, lifted them onto
his chariot. And he spoke to the kings, with a voice like the thunder of the
clouds.

"The wise say that a girl may be given to a worthy man by invitation. She is
adorned as well as one can, and wealth is given with her. Others give a girl for
a pair of cattle. Others give her for an agreed sum of wealth. Some take her by
force, and some by winning consent. Some approach a girl who is off her guard,
and some find a husband for themselves. The poets remember this as the eighth
kind of marriage, as you know. Kshatriyas praise the bridegroom choice, and they
go to it. But those who speak of dharma say that a bride carried off by force is
the best. These girls, kings of the earth, I mean to carry off by force. Strive
with all your strength, to win or to lose. Here I stand, kings of the earth,
resolved to fight."

So the mighty Kaurava spoke to the kings and to the king of Kashi. He set all
the girls on his own chariot and bade the kings farewell. Then he drove quickly
away, taking the girls with him.

Then all the kings leaped up in fury, touching their own arms and biting their
lips. There was a great commotion as they hurried to throw off their ornaments
and put on their armour. With the bright ornaments and armour on every side,
Janamejaya, it was like a falling of stars. Their armour and ornaments flashed
here and there. Their brows were knitted in anger and indignation, and their
eyes were red. The heroes mounted the fine chariots their drivers had made
ready, with good horses yoked to them. Carrying every kind of weapon, with their
weapons raised, they followed the lone Kaurava as he went.

Then the battle began between them and him, the one against the many. It was
wild and terrible, Bharata. They shot ten thousand arrows at him all at once.
Before they reached him, Bhishma quickly cut them all down. Then all the kings
surrounded him on every side, and they rained arrows on him as clouds rain on a
mountain. He held off that rain of arrows on every side with his own arrows, and
then he pierced every one of the kings, three arrows each. As a chariot fighter
he was quicker than any other man, and he guarded himself well in battle. Even
his enemies honoured him for it.

So the master of every weapon conquered them in battle. Then the Bharata set off
back towards the Bharatas with the girls. But the great warrior, the king of the
Shalvas, of boundless spirit, struck at Bhishma, son of Shantanu, from behind.
He was like a bull elephant, the leader of a herd, who follows a cow in heat and
strikes another elephant from behind with his tusks.

"You who lust after women, stop! Stop!" said the strong-armed king of the
Shalvas to Bhishma, driven by his fury.

Then that tiger among men, Bhishma, the destroyer of enemy armies, was stung by
those words. He blazed in anger like a fire without smoke. Keeping to the dharma
of a kshatriya, free of fear and alarm, the great warrior turned his chariot to
face Shalva. When they saw him turn, all the kings became onlookers at the
meeting of Bhishma and Shalva. Like two bulls bellowing over a cow in heat,
those two, strong and brave, went at each other.

Then the king of the Shalvas, the best of men, covered Bhishma, son of Shantanu,
with swift arrows by the hundred thousand. When the kings saw Bhishma struck
first by Shalva, they were amazed, and cried, "Well done! Well done!" All the
kings, seeing his speed in battle, praised Shalva gladly.

When he heard the kshatriyas' words, Bhishma, son of Shantanu, conqueror of
enemy cities, was angry, and he said, "Stop! Stop!" And he said to his driver in
anger, "Drive to where that king is, so that I may kill him today, as the king
of birds kills a snake."

Then the Kaurava fitted the weapon of Varuna rightly, and with it he crushed the
four horses of the king of the Shalvas. He held off the Shalva king's weapons
with his own. Then the Kaurava Bhishma killed his driver, and with one weapon he
killed his fine horses. For the sake of the girls, Bhishma, son of Shantanu,
conquered that best of kings and let him go alive. Then Shalva went back to his
own city, bull of the Bharatas. And the kings who had come to see the bridegroom
choice went back to their own kingdoms too.

---

So Bhishma, the best of fighters, won those girls. He set out for Hastinapura,
where the Kaurava king was. Before long he had passed forests, rivers, mountains
and trees of many kinds. He had destroyed his enemies in battle and was
unwounded. So the son of the river that runs to the sea, of courage beyond
counting, brought home the daughters of the king of Kashi. The righteous man
went to the Kurus, holding them as his daughters-in-law, or his younger sisters,
or his daughters. All three were full of virtues, and his courage had won them.
Bhishma, the elder brother, gave them to his younger brother, Vichitravirya.

By the dharma of the good he had done a deed more than human. Now Bhishma, who
knew dharma and was master of himself, made up his mind together with Satyavati.
He began to prepare for his brother Vichitravirya's wedding.

As Bhishma was about to hold the wedding, the eldest of the Kashi king's
daughters, a good woman, said to him, "I had already chosen the lord of Saubha
as my husband, in my heart. And he had chosen me. This was my father's wish too.
I was to choose Shalva at that bridegroom choice. Now that you know this, you
who know dharma, do what dharma requires."

When the girl said this to him in the assembly of brahmins, the hero Bhishma
thought over what was right to do. He made his decision with brahmins who had
mastered the Vedas. Then Bhishma, who knew dharma, gave leave to Amba, the
eldest daughter of the king of Kashi, to go.

Bhishma gave Ambika and Ambalika as wives to his younger brother, Vichitravirya,
by the rites the scriptures lay down. When he had taken their hands, righteous
Vichitravirya, proud of his beauty and youth, gave himself up to desire. The two
were tall and dark, with dark curling hair, raised red nails, and full hips and
breasts. *We have won a husband worthy of us*, they thought, and they honoured
the fine Vichitravirya. He was like the Ashvins in beauty, with the courage and
strength of a god, and he stirred the hearts of all women.

The lord of the earth took his pleasure with them for seven years, and then the
young Vichitravirya fell ill with consumption. His friends and trusted
physicians tried to save him. But the Kaurava went to the house of Yama, like
the setting sun. Keeping to Satyavati's wishes, Bhishma had all the funeral
rites performed rightly for King Vichitravirya. The priests and all the bulls of
the Kurus were with him.

**Divergences.**

* **Amba's story stops here in Book 1.** The CE says only that Bhishma "gave her
  leave" (1.96.51). Her journey to Shalva, his rejection, her vow and her rebirth
  as Shikhandi are told in Book 5 (the *Ambopākhyāna*, 5.170–193). The novel does
  not bring them forward.

**Choices.**

* 1.95.7 *tulyanāmā*: the gandharva king "of the same name", also Chitrangada.
* 1.95.8 *samās tisraḥ*: the fight lasted "three years".
* 1.96.8–11: Bhishma's list of kinds of marriage is his argument, given as he
  makes it. The claim that a bride "carried off by force" (*pramathya hṛtām*) is the
  best is his, attributed to "those who speak of dharma".
* 1.96.9 *balenānye 'numānya ca*: "some by force, and some by winning consent".
* 1.96.10: the list names "the eighth" kind of marriage without spelling out all
  eight. Kept.
* 1.96.7 and 13: Bhishma lifts the girls onto his chariot twice, before and after
  his speech. Both are kept.
* Many vocatives ("king", "lord of men", "conqueror of enemy cities") are
  dropped where they clog a sentence; Vaishampayana's "Janamejaya" (1.96.16) is
  kept.
* 1.96.26, 31: the images of rutting bull elephants and bulls over a cow in heat are
  the text's own.
* 1.96.37 *astraṃ vāruṇam*: the weapon of Varuna.
* 1.96.43 *sāgaragāsutaḥ*: "son of the river that runs to the sea", i.e. of Ganga.
* 1.96.48 *saubhapatiḥ*: Shalva, "lord of Saubha".
* 1.96.57 *yakṣmāṇam*: consumption (a wasting disease).
