# The Storyteller in the Forest

> *Bow to Narayana, and to Nara, the best of men,*
> *and to the goddess Sarasvati.*
> *Then let the word* Victory *be spoken.*

In the Naimisha forest, the sage Shaunaka and the brahmins of his household
were holding a sacrifice that was to last twelve years. One day a visitor
came to them there. His name was Ugrashravas. He was the son of
Lomaharshana, and he was a suta, one of the bards who keep the old stories.
He knew those stories well. People called him Sauti, the suta's son.

He came humbly, with his head bowed. The hermits who lived in the forest saw
him arrive and gathered round him, eager to hear wonderful tales. He joined
his palms and greeted every one of them, and asked how their austerities
were going, and they made him welcome. When all the hermits had sat down, he
took the seat they showed him, as a well-mannered guest should.

They let him rest. When they saw that he was comfortable, one of the seers
began the talk.

"Where have you come from, Sauti?" he asked. "And where have you been all
this time? Tell us."

Ugrashravas, who spoke well, gave them a proper answer.

"I have come from the snake sacrifice of King Janamejaya, the royal seer,
the son of Parikshit. There, in front of the king, Vaishampayana told the
many holy stories that Krishna Dvaipayana made. He told them fully and in
the proper way. I heard those wonderful stories, all of them belonging to
the Mahabharata.

"After that I wandered through many holy bathing places and shrines. I went
to the sacred country called Samantapanchaka, where the brahmins go. It is
the place where the war was fought long ago, between the Pandavas and the
Kurus and all the kings of the earth. From there I came here, because I
wanted to see you. Long life to you all. To me, you are holy men.

"You have bathed and made yourselves pure. You have said your prayers and
fed the sacred fires. You shine like the sun, and like fire, and you sit
here at ease at your sacrifice. What shall I tell you, brahmins? Holy tales
from the ancient lore? Tales of dharma, the right way to live? Or the deeds
of the great kings and the great seers?"

The seers answered him.

"Tell us the ancient story that the supreme seer Dvaipayana made. The gods
heard it, and the brahmin seers, and they honoured it. It is the finest of
all tales, rich in its words and its parts, subtle in meaning, sound in
reason, and adorned with the meaning of the Vedas. It is the history of the
Bharatas: holy, finely made, filled out with learning of every kind. It is
the collection made by Vyasa, whose deeds were wonderful, and it stands
equal to the four Vedas. It teaches dharma and it takes away the fear of
wrongdoing. Vaishampayana told it to King Janamejaya at the sacrifice,
gladly and faithfully, because Dvaipayana told him to. That is the story we
want to hear."

---

Before he began, the bard bowed to God.

"I bow to the first Person, the Lord, on whom many call and whom many
praise. He is truth. He is the single sacred syllable. He is Brahman, seen
and unseen, eternal. He is what is and what is not, and the whole world,
and he is beyond both what is and what is not. He made the high and the low.
He is ancient, supreme, unchanging. He is Vishnu, blessed and the source of
blessing, the most worthy, pure and without fault. He is Hrishikesha,
and Hari, the teacher of all that moves and all that stands still.

"Having bowed to him, I will tell you the whole thought of Vyasa, the great
seer whom every world honours, whose power has no measure.

"Some poets have told this history before. Some are telling it now. And
others will tell it on this earth in times to come. It is great knowledge,
and it is established in all three worlds. The twice-born, the learned
classes, keep it both in full and in brief. It is adorned with fine words,
with the ways of gods and of men, and with every kind of metre, and the
learned love it."

Then he told them how the world began.

In the beginning there was no light. Nothing could be seen, and darkness
covered everything on every side. Then one great egg came into being, the
seed of all creatures, which never fails. At the start of the age, so they
say, this was the great and divine cause of things. In it, it is said, was
the true light, the eternal Brahman: wonderful, beyond thought, the same
everywhere, the subtle and unseen cause of what is and what is not.

From it was born the Grandsire, the one lord of creatures: Brahma, teacher
of the gods, who is also called Sthanu, and Manu, and Ka, and Parameshthin.
After him came Prachetas, and Daksha, and the seven sons of Daksha, and then
the twenty-one lords of creatures. Then came the Person whose self is beyond
measure, whom all the seers know. Then came the Vishvedevas, the Adityas,
the Vasus and the two Ashvins, the yakshas and the sadhyas, the pishachas
and the guhyakas, and the spirits of the ancestors. Then were born the
brahmin seers, wise and pure, and many royal seers endowed with every
virtue. Then came the waters, the sky, the earth, the wind, the air between,
and the directions; the year and the seasons, the months and the
fortnights, and day and night in their turn. Everything else, too, came
into being, and the world was its witness.

All that is seen here, whatever moves and whatever stands still, is drawn
back in again when the age comes to its end. When a season comes round,
its signs appear again, in all their different forms. In the same way,
when a new age begins, all things appear again. So this wheel turns in the
world, without beginning and without end, making creatures and unmaking
them.

Thirty-three thousand, thirty-three hundred and thirty-three gods were
created. That is creation, told in brief.

The sons of Vivasvat, the sun, were Divasputra, Brihadbhanu, Chakshus,
Atman, Vibhavasu, Savitri, Richika, Arka, Bhanu, Ashavaha and Ravi. The
youngest of them was Mahya. His son was Devabhrat, and Devabhrat's son was
called Subhrat. Subhrat had three sons, learned and blessed with children:
Dashajyoti, Shatajyoti and Sahasrajyoti. Dashajyoti had ten thousand sons,
Shatajyoti ten times as many, and Sahasrajyoti ten times as many again.
From them came the line of the Kurus, and the lines of the Yadus and of
Bharata, of Yayati and of Ikshvaku, and the lines of all the royal seers.
Many lines came from them, and many kinds of creatures, in all their
variety.

The seer saw it all come into being: the dwelling places of all creatures,
the threefold mystery, the Veda and its discipline and the knowledge that
goes with them, dharma and wealth and pleasure, the teachings on each of
these and many other sciences, and the rules by which the world goes on.
The histories with their explanations, and the sacred texts of every kind:
all of them are gone through here. That is the mark of this book. The seer
set out this great knowledge at length, and then he told it in brief as
well, for the learned of the world like to hold a thing both ways.

Some brahmins learn the Bharata starting from Manu. Some start from the
story of Astika, and some from the tale of Uparichara. Wise men bring to
light the many kinds of knowledge in the collection. Some are skilled at
explaining it, and others at keeping it by heart.

---

The maker of this holy history was the son of Satyavati. By austerity and
by a celibate life he divided the eternal Veda, and then he made this story.
He was Krishna Dvaipayana, the learned son of Parashara, a brahmin seer,
strict in his vows, whose heart was set on dharma.

Long ago, at the command of his mother and of the wise Bhishma, he fathered
three sons for the line of Vichitravirya, and they were like three sacred
fires: Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Vidura. Then the wise seer went back to his
hermitage to live in austerity. When his sons had been born, and had grown
old, and had gone to the highest goal, the great seer told the Bharata in
this world of men.

King Janamejaya asked him for it, and thousands of brahmins asked with him.
So Vyasa told his pupil Vaishampayana, who sat beside him, to tell it.
Vaishampayana sat with the priests of the sacrifice, and in the pauses
between the rites he told the Bharata, and they urged him on again and
again.

In it Dvaipayana told the whole story of the Kuru line. He told of
Gandhari's devotion to dharma, of Vidura's wisdom and Kunti's strength of
mind. He told of Krishna's greatness, of the truthfulness of the Pandavas,
and of the wickedness of Dhritarashtra's sons.

He made the Bharata as a collection of twenty-four thousand verses.
Without its side stories, that much is what the learned call the Bharata.
Then the seer made a summary of a hundred and fifty verses more: the first
chapter, which lists the events and the books.

Dvaipayana taught it first to his son Shuka. After that he gave it to other
pupils who were worthy of it. Narada told it to the gods. Asita Devala told
it to the spirits of the ancestors. And Shuka told it to the gandharvas, the
singers of heaven, to the yakshas, the spirits of the wild places, and to
the rakshasas, the night-walkers.

---

This is the story in brief, as the bard told it.

Duryodhana is a great tree made of anger. Karna is its trunk and Shakuni
its branches. Duhshasana is its rich flower and fruit. Its root is the
foolish king Dhritarashtra.

Yudhishthira is a great tree made of dharma. Arjuna is its trunk and
Bhimasena its branches. The two sons of Madri are its rich flower and fruit.
Its root is Krishna, and the Veda, and the brahmins.

Pandu conquered many lands by his courage in battle. Then he went to live in
the forest with his people, for he loved to hunt. There he killed a deer
while it was mating, and that brought a terrible misfortune on him.

His two wives, following a secret teaching of dharma, went to the gods:
to Dharma, to Vayu the wind, to Indra, and to the twin Ashvins. So the sons
of Pritha were born, and from their birth they were brought up there in the
proper way. The boys grew up among ascetics, guarded by their two mothers,
in holy forests and in the hermitages of great men.

Then the seers themselves brought them to the sons of Dhritarashtra.
They were handsome children with matted hair, living the life of young
students of the Veda.

"These are your sons and your brothers, your pupils and your friends,"
the sages said. "These are the Pandavas." And then the sages vanished.

The Kauravas saw the Pandavas presented to them. So did the good citizens of
every class. They all shouted aloud for joy.

Some said, "They are not his."

Others said, "They are his."

Others said, "Pandu has been dead a long time. How can they be his?"

And on every side voices were heard: "Welcome, all of you! What good fortune
that we see Pandu's children! Say welcome to them!"

When that noise died down, a great roar rose from beings no one could see,
and it filled every direction. Flowers rained down, there were sweet scents,
and conches and drums sounded as the Pandavas entered the city. It was a
wonder. The citizens were delighted, and their joy rose in a great sound
that reached the sky and spread the Pandavas' fame.

The Pandavas learned all the Vedas and many other sciences, and they lived
there honoured, afraid of nothing. The people loved Yudhishthira for his
purity, Bhimasena for his steadiness and Arjuna for his courage. They loved
Kunti for the care she gave to her elders, and the twins for their modesty.
The whole world was glad of the brothers, for their courage and their
goodness.

In time the kings gathered for a princess who would choose her own
husband, and Arjuna did a thing that was very hard to do and won Draupadi.
From then on every archer in the world honoured him, and in battle he was
like the sun: too bright to look at. He conquered all the kings of the
earth, and all the great peoples, and so he brought his brother the king
the great sacrifice of kings, the Rajasuya.

So Yudhishthira held the Rajasuya, rich in food and in gifts, perfect in
every part. He held it through Krishna's good counsel and the strength of
Bhima and Arjuna, once they had killed Jarasandha and the proud king of the
Chedis.

Gifts came in from every side, and it was Duryodhana who received them:
gems and gold and jewels, cattle, elephants, horses and riches. When he saw
how the Pandavas prospered, a great anger rose in him, born of envy. He saw
the assembly hall that Maya, the builder of the asuras, had made for them,
as fine as the flying palaces of the gods, and the sight tormented him.
In that hall he stumbled, confused, and Bhima laughed at him like a boor, in
front of Krishna.

Duryodhana had pleasures of every kind and jewels of every kind. Yet word
came to Dhritarashtra that his son had grown pale and sallow and thin. So
Dhritarashtra, who loved his son, allowed the game of dice.

When Krishna heard of it, a great anger rose in him. He was far from
pleased. Yet he let the quarrels take their course, and he looked on as the
dice and the other terrible wrongs grew. Then, setting aside Vidura and
Drona, Bhishma and Kripa, he made the warriors of the earth kill one another
in that fearful war.

---

When the sons of Pandu were winning, and all the news was bitter, King
Dhritarashtra knew what Duryodhana intended, and what Karna and Shakuni
intended. He thought for a long time. Then he spoke to Sanjaya.

"Listen to all of it, Sanjaya, and do not think ill of me. You are learned,
and clever, and wise, and the wise respect you.

"I never wanted war. The ruin of the Kurus gave me no joy. I made no
difference between my own sons and the sons of Pandu. But my sons are full
of anger, and they resent me because I am old. And I am blind. I am weak,
and I love my son, and so I put up with it. Duryodhana loses his senses, and
I lose mine with him.

"At the Rajasuya he saw the glory of Pandu's mighty son. When he climbed up
into that hall to look at it, they laughed at him. He could not bear it. He
could not beat the Pandavas in battle by himself, and he had no heart to
win fortune for himself, the way a kshatriya, a man born to fight, should
win it. So he and Shakuni, the king of Gandhara, planned a crooked game of
dice.

"Now hear from me, Sanjaya, everything I came to know, as it came to me, one
thing after another. When you have heard my words and seen that they are
reasoned and true, then you will know, son of a suta, that I am a man who
sees with his mind."

**Divergences.**

* Many retellings say Vyasa dictated the poem to **Ganesha**, who wrote it
  down. That episode is not in the Critical Edition, and neither are the
  "8,800 riddling verses" that go with it. Both are in the vulgate (Ganguli
  1.1). See ledger L-01 and L-02. Brahma's visit to Vyasa, which in the
  vulgate leads into the Ganesha story, is also a rejected passage (CE
  Appendix I No. 1).
* The epigraph's *Victory* translates *jaya*. The epic calls itself "the
  history named Jaya" (CE 1.56.19).

**Choices.**

* 1.1.50 *manvādi*: "starting from Manu", kept literally. Ganguli, following
  the vulgate commentators, reads "from the opening invocation".
* 1.1.90 *bhīmenānabhijātavat*: the "like a boor" can describe Bhima's
  laughter or the way Duryodhana was treated. The English keeps the same
  ambiguity.
* 1.1.92–94: the subject of *anvamodata* ("let the quarrels run"),
  *upaikṣata* ("looked on") and *ahan kṣatraṃ parasparam* ("made the
  warriors kill one another") is Krishna (Vasudeva), continuing from 1.1.92.
  Ganguli reads it the same way. A possible alternative for 1.1.94 is "the
  warriors killed one another". The novel follows the natural grammar.
* 1.1.84 *rājñe*, "for the king": Yudhishthira, made explicit as "his brother
  the king".
* "Maya, the builder of the asuras" names Maya's role (told in full in Book 2);
  1.1.89 says only "made by Maya".
