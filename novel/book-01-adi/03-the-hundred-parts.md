# The Hundred Parts

"You spoke of Samantapanchaka, son of the suta," said the seers. "We would like
to hear all about it, as it should be told."

"Listen, then, good brahmins," said Ugrashravas, "if you wish to hear good
stories, and hear about the place called Samantapanchaka.

"Between the second age of the world and the third, Rama, the son of Jamadagni,
the greatest of all who bear arms, was driven by his rage to kill the kshatriyas
of the earth, the whole warrior class, again and again. He blazed like fire. By
his own strength he wiped out all the kshatriyas, and at Samantapanchaka he made
five lakes of their blood. There, beside himself with anger, he offered their
blood to his ancestors. So we have heard.

"Then Richika and his other ancestors came to him, that bull among brahmins, and
held him back. 'Forgive,' they said. And he stopped.

"The land around those lakes of blood is called Samantapanchaka, the Place of
the Five, and it is holy. The wise say that a place should be called by the mark
it bears. And when the time came between the third age and the last, the Kali
age, the war between the armies of the Kurus and the Pandavas was fought at
Samantapanchaka. Eighteen full armies came together in that land, eager to
fight. It is a land of the highest dharma, and nothing on it is flawed. That is
how the place got its name, brahmins. I have told you all of it now: how that
holy and lovely land became famous in the three worlds."

The seers spoke again. "You spoke of the full army, the *akshauhini*, son of the
suta. We would like to hear exactly what it is: how many chariots and horses,
men and elephants. Tell us, for you know it all."

"One chariot, one elephant, five men on foot and three horses make a *patti*,"
said Ugrashravas. "So say those who know. Three pattis make an army's head.
Three heads make a troop, three troops a company, three companies a column.
Three columns make a division, three divisions a host, three hosts a force. And
ten forces, the learned say, make a full army, an akshauhini.

"Those who know how to count have counted the chariots in a full army:
twenty-one thousand, eight hundred and seventy. The elephants are the same.
There are a hundred and nine thousand, three hundred and fifty men on foot, and
sixty-five thousand, six hundred and ten horses. That is the full army, the
akshauhini, as I have described it to you.

"Counted this way, the armies of the Kurus and the Pandavas together came to
eighteen full armies. They came together in that land and they died there, for
the Kauravas' sake, by the working of Time, whose deeds are wonderful.

"Bhishma, who knew the highest weapons, fought for ten days. Drona guarded the
Kuru army for five. Karna, who crushed enemy armies, fought for two. Shalya
fought for half a day, and after that came the fight with maces. And at the end
of that same day, Kritavarma, Drona's son and Kripa killed Yudhishthira's army
at night, while it slept, trusting them.

"Now, at this sacrifice of yours, Shaunaka, I will tell the Bharata in full,
beginning with the story of Pauloma. It is a story of many meanings and many
words, full of the customs of the world. The wise take to it the way men who
seek release take to giving up the world. Among all that can be known, it is
like the self. Among all that is dear, it is like life. Among all the sacred
traditions, this history is the best, and it has the highest purpose. The best
of minds is set in this best of histories, the way all speech, in the world and
in the Veda, rests on vowels and consonants.

"Listen now to the parts of this Bharata history, so full of wisdom, so rich in
its words and its parts."

---

So the bard went through the whole of it for them, book by book, naming each
part and telling what it held. There were a hundred parts in all, gathered into
eighteen books.

The **first book** is the **Beginnings**. Its parts are these: the Table of
Contents; the Summary of the Parts; Paushya; Pauloma; Astika; the Descent of the
Lines; the wonderful part of the Births, which the gods themselves made; the
Burning of the House of Lac; Hidimba; the Killing of Baka; Chaitraratha; the
Bride-choice of the Panchala princess; the Wedding, after the princess was won
by a warrior's rules; the Coming of Vidura; the Gaining of the Kingdom; Arjuna's
Stay in the Forest; the Carrying Off of Subhadra; the Bringing of the Wedding
Gifts; and the Burning of the Khandava Forest, where Maya is seen.

In Paushya, the greatness of Uttanka is told. In Pauloma, the family of Bhrigu
is described at length. In Astika come the births of all the snakes and of
Garuda, the churning of the ocean of milk, and the birth of the horse
Uchchaihshravas; and it tells how this story of the great Bharatas first came to
be told, at the snake sacrifice of King Janamejaya, son of Parikshit.

The part of the Births tells how many kings came to be born, and many brahmins,
and the seer Dvaipayana. It tells how the gods came down to earth in portions of
themselves, and so did the daityas and danavas, the mighty yakshas, the nagas,
the snakes, the gandharvas, the birds and all kinds of other beings. It tells
how the Vasus were born again of the river Ganga, in Shantanu's house, and went
back up to heaven. It tells how Bhishma was born from the joined portions of
their power; how he gave up the kingdom and held to his vow of celibacy, and
kept his promise; how he protected Chitrangada, and when Chitrangada was killed,
protected his younger brother, and put Vichitravirya on the throne. It tells how
Dharma was born among men because of the curse of Animandavya; how Dhritarashtra
and Pandu were born of Krishna Dvaipayana, by a boon; and how the Pandavas were
born.

Then comes the journey to Varanavata, and Duryodhana's plot, and the tunnel dug
on Vidura's advice. The Pandavas, in a terrible forest, meet Hidimba, and here
the birth of Ghatotkacha is told. The Pandavas live unknown in a brahmin's
house, and Baka is killed, and the townspeople marvel. Arjuna defeats the
gandharva Angaraparna on the bank of the Ganges, and goes with all his brothers
towards the Panchalas. Here are the fine tales of Tapati, of Vasishtha and of
Aurva, and the wonderful tale of the five Indras. Drupada wonders whether one
woman may be the wife of five men, and Draupadi's marriage is made by the gods,
not by men. Vidura comes; the Pandavas see Krishna; they live in Khandavaprastha
and rule half the kingdom. At Narada's bidding, they make their agreement about
Draupadi, and the tale of Sunda and Upasunda is told. Arjuna goes to live in the
forest and on the way meets Ulupi; he visits holy places, and Babhruvahana is
born. In Dvaraka, Arjuna of the diadem wins Subhadra, who loves him, with
Krishna's blessing. When Krishna, Devaki's son, comes with the wedding gifts,
the discus and the bow are won and the Khandava forest is burned. Abhimanyu,
full of fire, is born to Subhadra. Maya is saved from the fire, and so is a
snake, and the sons of the great seer Mandapala are born of the bird Sharngi.

That is the first book, and it is very long. The supreme seer Vyasa counted two
hundred and eighteen chapters in it, and seven thousand, nine hundred and
eighty-four verses.

The **second book** is the **Assembly Hall**, and much happens in it. Its parts
are: the Assembly Hall; the Counsel; the Killing of Jarasandha; the Conquest of
the Directions; the Royal Consecration; the Taking of the Guest Gift; the
Killing of Shishupala; the Dice; and the Second Dice Game.

The Pandavas' hall is built, and the servants seen. Narada, who has seen the
gods, tells of the halls of the guardians of the world. The Rajasuya begins,
Jarasandha is killed, and Krishna sets free the kings locked up in Girivraja. At
the Rajasuya, in the quarrel over the guest gift, Shishupala is killed.
Duryodhana sees the splendour of the sacrifice and is full of grief and
resentment, and Bhima laughs at him on the floor of the hall. From that his
anger grows, and it makes him set up the game of dice. In the dice game, Shakuni
the gambler beats the son of Dharma. The Pandavas are drowning in an ocean of
dice, and Draupadi carries them across it, as a boat carries men across the sea.
When King Duryodhana learns that they have got across, he challenges the
Pandavas to dice again. The great seer counted seventy-two chapters in this
book, and two thousand, five hundred and eleven verses.

The **third book** is the great book of the **Forest**. Its parts are: the
Forest; the Killing of Kirmira; the Mountain Hunter, the fight between the Lord
and Arjuna; the Journey to Indra's World; the Pilgrimage of the wise king of the
Kurus; the Killing of Jatasura; the Battle with the Yakshas; the Python; the
Session with Markandeya; the Talk between Draupadi and Satyabhama; the Cattle
Expedition; the Dream of the Deer; the Story of the Measure of Rice; the
Carrying Off of Draupadi; the Taking of the Earrings; and the Fire-Sticks.

The citizens follow the wise son of Dharma, and the Vrishnis and all the
Panchalas come. The story of the killing of Saubha is told, and Kirmira is
killed. Arjuna, whose power has no measure, goes away to win weapons. He fights
Mahadeva, the great god, who takes the form of a mountain hunter, and sees the
guardians of the world, and goes up to heaven. The great seer Brihadashva comes,
and Yudhishthira pours out his grief in his troubles. Here is the tale of Nala,
full of dharma and full of pity, where Damayanti stands firm when disaster falls
on Nala. Lomasha tells the Pandavas, in their exile, how Arjuna is faring in
heaven. The Pandavas go on pilgrimage, and Jatasura is killed. Draupadi sends
Bhimasena to Gandhamadana for the flowers of the mandara tree, and he breaks
into the lotus pool; there he fights a great battle with rakshasas, and with
mighty yakshas led by Manimat. Here is the tale of Agastya, who ate the asura
Vatapi, and who went to Lopamudra so that he might have a son. Then comes the
tale of the hawk and the dove, in which Indra, Agni and Dharma test King Shibi.
Here are the life of Rishyashringa, a celibate from boyhood, and the life of
Rama, the mighty son of Jamadagni, with the killing of Kartavirya and the
Haihayas. Here is Sukanya's story, in which Chyavana of the Bhrigus gives the
two Ashvins a share of soma at King Sharyati's sacrifice, and they give the sage
his youth again. Here is the story of Jantu, in which King Somaka sacrifices his
son to have sons, and gets a hundred of them. Here is the story of Ashtavakra,
who beats Bandin in debate and gets back his father from the sea.

Arjuna, the left-handed archer, having won the divine weapons, fights for his
teacher's sake with the Nivatakavachas who live in the city of gold. He meets
his brothers again on Gandhamadana. Then comes the cattle expedition, where
Arjuna fights the gandharvas; the return to the lake of Dvaitavana; and
Jayadratha's carrying off of Draupadi from the hermitage, when Bhima, swift as
the wind, runs after him. In the session with Markandeya, many tales are told,
one after another. Krishna visits them, and Draupadi talks with Satyabhama. Here
are the story of the measure of rice, the story of Indradyumna, the stories of
Savitri, of Auddalaki and of Vainya, and the long story of Rama. Here Indra robs
Karna of his earrings. And here is the story of the fire-sticks, in which Dharma
teaches his son, and after which the Pandavas, rewarded with boons, go away to
the west. That is the third book. The supreme seer counted two hundred and
sixty-nine chapters in it, and eleven thousand, six hundred and sixty-four
verses.

The **fourth book** is **Virata**, and it is broad and full. Its parts are:
Virata; the Killing of the Kichakas; the Cattle Raid; and the Wedding of
Abhimanyu and Virata's daughter.

The Pandavas go to Virata's city. In the cremation ground they see a great shami
tree and hide their weapons in it. They go into the city and live there in
disguise. Bhima kills the wicked Kichaka. In the cattle raid, Arjuna defeats the
Kurus in battle, and the Pandavas win back Virata's herds. Virata gives Uttara
to Arjuna as a daughter-in-law, a wife for Abhimanyu, Subhadra's son, the slayer
of enemies. The great seer counted sixty-seven full chapters in this book, and
two thousand and fifty verses.

The **fifth book** is the **Effort**. Its parts are: the Effort, which is very
wonderful; Sanjaya's Journey; the Sleepless Night of Dhritarashtra's worry; the
Teaching of Sanatsujata, which shows the secret of the self; the Terms of the
Treaty; the Lord's Journey; the Quarrel of Karna; the March Out of the Kuru and
Pandava Armies; the Counting of Chariot-fighters and Great Chariot-fighters; the
Coming of Uluka the Envoy, which feeds the anger; and the Story of Amba.

When the Pandavas are camped at Upaplavya, eager to win, Duryodhana and Arjuna
both come to Krishna. "You must help us in this war," they say. And Krishna,
whose wisdom is great, answers, "I will be one man who does not fight, a
counsellor. Or else I will give a full army. Which shall I give to which of you,
best of men?" The foolish, wicked Duryodhana chooses the army. Arjuna chooses
Krishna, who will not fight, as his counsellor. The mighty king Dhritarashtra
sends Sanjaya to the Pandavas as his envoy, to make peace. When he hears that
the Pandavas are led by Krishna, Dhritarashtra lies awake all night with worry.
Vidura tells the wise king many good and varied things. And Sanatsujata teaches
the king the highest knowledge of the self, while he is sick at heart and sunk
in sorrow. In the morning, in the assembly of kings, Sanjaya tells the king that
Krishna and Arjuna are one. Krishna, full of pity and wanting peace, goes
himself to Hastinapura to make it. King Duryodhana refuses him, though Krishna
begs for a peace that is good for both sides. Krishna learns of the wicked plot
of Karna, Duryodhana and the others, and shows the kings that he is the lord of
yoga. Krishna takes Karna up into his chariot and gives him counsel, and Karna,
out of pride, refuses him. Then the army marches out of Hastinapura, chariots
and horses, men and elephants, and its strength is counted. The king sends
Uluka, a cruel-tongued envoy, to the Pandavas on the eve of the great battle.
The chariot-fighters and the great chariot-fighters are counted, and the story
of Amba is told. That is the fifth book, full of events, about peace and war.
The great-minded Vyasa counted one hundred and eighty-six chapters in it, and
six thousand, six hundred and ninety-eight verses.

The **sixth book** is **Bhishma**, full of wonders. Its parts are: the
Consecration of Bhishma, whose cause is wonderful; the Making of the Land of
Jambu; the Earth, which tells of the continents at length; the Song of the Lord;
and the Killing of Bhishma.

Sanjaya describes the making of the land of Jambu. A terrible war is fought for
ten days, cruel beyond words, and Yudhishthira's army falls into the deepest
despair. Wise Krishna drives out Arjuna's despair, which comes of confusion,
with reasons that show the way to release. And the great archer Arjuna puts
Shikhandi in front, strikes Bhishma again and again with sharp arrows, and
brings him down from his chariot. That is the sixth great book of the Bharata.
Vyasa, who knew the Veda, counted a hundred and seventeen chapters in it, and
five thousand, eight hundred and eighty-four verses.

The **seventh book** is **Drona**, full of wonders and events. Its parts are:
the Consecration of Drona; the Killing of the Samshaptakas; the Killing of
Abhimanyu; the Vow; the Killing of Jayadratha; the Killing of Ghatotkacha; the
Killing of Drona, which makes the hair stand up; and the Release of the Narayana
Weapon.

The Samshaptakas draw Arjuna away from the battlefield. King Bhagadatta, Indra's
equal in battle, is killed by Arjuna of the diadem, together with his elephant
Supratika. Many great chariot-fighters, famous in the world, with Jayadratha at
their head, kill Abhimanyu, a brave boy not yet grown to manhood. When Abhimanyu
is dead, Arjuna in his anger kills seven full armies in the battle, and King
Jayadratha, and destroys what is left of the Samshaptakas. Alambusa and
Shrutayus, mighty Jalasandha, Somadatta's son, Virata, the great chariot-fighter
Drupada, Ghatotkacha and others are killed in the book of Drona. And here
Ashvatthama, when Drona has fallen in the fighting, in his fury calls up the
terrible Narayana weapon. That is the seventh great book of the Bharata. In it
most of the kings of the earth, the heroes named in it, meet their deaths. The
seer, the son of Parashara, after careful thought, counted one hundred and
seventy chapters in it, and eight thousand, nine hundred and nine verses.

The **eighth book** is **Karna**, most wonderful. The wise king of Madra is made
Karna's charioteer, and the ancient tale of the fall of the Triple City is told.
As they drive out to battle, Karna and Shalya trade harsh words, and here is the
story of the swan and the crow, told as an insult. Yudhishthira and Arjuna turn
on each other in anger. And Arjuna kills the great chariot-fighter Karna in
single combat. Those who study the Bharata call it the eighth book. It has
sixty-nine chapters, and four thousand, nine hundred verses.

The **ninth book** is **Shalya**, with its many meanings. Its parts are: Shalya;
the Entering of the Lake; the Mace Fight; and the Sarasvati, which tells of the
holy bathing places one after another. When the Kuru army's great heroes have
been killed, the king of Madra becomes its leader. The chariot fights are told
one by one, and the destruction of the chief Kurus. King Yudhishthira, the great
chariot-fighter, kills Shalya. The terrible fight with maces is told, and the
holiness of the bathing places of the river Sarasvati. That is the ninth book,
wonderful and full of meaning. Those who know how to count have counted
fifty-nine chapters in it, full of events, and three thousand, two hundred and
twenty verses, which the sage made about the Kauravas and their fame.

The **tenth book** is the terrible book of the **Night Raid**. Its parts are the
Night Raid, which is dreadful, and the Reed Weapon, which is more dreadful
still. When the Pandavas have gone away, three chariot-fighters, Kritavarma,
Kripa and Drona's son, come in the evening, spattered with blood, to King
Duryodhana, who lies raging with his thighs broken. Drona's son, the great
chariot-fighter, holding fast to his anger, makes a vow: "I will not take off my
armour until I have killed all the Panchalas, with Dhrishtadyumna at their head,
and the Pandavas with their counsellors." And those bulls among men, led by
Drona's son, kill the Panchalas and all their followers at night, as they sleep,
trusting. The five Pandavas escape, because they shelter under Krishna's
strength, and so does the great archer Satyaki; all the rest are killed.
Draupadi, grieving for her sons and stricken by the killing of her father and
brothers, makes up her mind to fast to death, and sits down in front of her
husbands. At her word, Bhima of the fearful courage runs in his fury after the
son of their teacher. Afraid of Bhima, and driven by fate, Drona's son looses
his weapon in rage: "Let there be no Pandavas!" "Not so!" says Krishna, to quiet
that word, and Arjuna quiets that weapon with a weapon of his own. Drona's son,
Dvaipayana and the others lay curses on one another. Water is offered for all
the dead kings, and the story is told of Karna, the son whom Pritha bore in
secret. That is the tenth book, the Night Raid. The great-souled seer set out
eighteen chapters in it, and eight hundred and seventy verses, in the book that
joins the Night Raid and the Reed Weapon.

The **eleventh book** is the **Women**, and it moves to pity. Its parts are the
Offering of Water, the Women, and the Rites for the Dead of the Kurus. Here the
wives of the heroes weep, and it is the most pitiful lament of all. Here
Gandhari and Dhritarashtra are seized by anger, and grow calm again. They see
the brave kshatriyas who never turned back and who met their fated end: sons,
brothers and fathers, killed in battle. And the wise king, best of all who keep
dharma, has the bodies of the kings burned according to the scriptures. That is
the eleventh book, very great and very pitiful. It has twenty-seven chapters and
seven hundred and seventy-five verses. The great-souled maker of the Bharata
made it to melt the hearts of good people and bring tears to their eyes.

The **twelfth book** is **Peace**, and it makes the mind grow. Its parts are:
the Consecration of the wise king of dharma; the Crushing of Charvaka, a
rakshasa disguised as a brahmin; the Sharing of the Houses; and then Peace,
which tells of the duties of kings, the duties in times of distress, and the
duties that lead to release.

Here King Yudhishthira, the king of dharma, is sick of the world, because he has
caused the deaths of fathers, brothers, sons, kinsmen and friends. Here the
duties spoken from the bed of arrows are explained, which kings who want to know
good government should learn. Here are the duties for times of distress, which
show the times and the reasons, and by learning them a man may come to know
everything. And the many and wonderful duties that lead to release are told at
length. That is the twelfth book, dear to the wise. There are three hundred and
thirty-nine chapters in it, and fourteen thousand, five hundred and twenty-five
verses.

The **thirteenth book** is the best of all, the book of **Instruction**. Its
parts are the Instructions and the Going to Heaven of the wise Bhishma. Here
Yudhishthira, king of the Kurus, hears from Bhishma, son of the river Ganga, the
final word on dharma, and comes back to himself. The whole practice of dharma
and of wealth is shown. The fruits of every kind of gift are set out: who is
worthy to receive, and the highest rule of giving; the rules of good conduct;
and the highest end of truth. Here too Bhishma's going to heaven is told. It has
a hundred and forty-six chapters and six thousand, seven hundred verses, and it
settles what dharma is.

The **fourteenth book** is the **Horse Sacrifice**, which destroys all sins. Its
parts are the Horse Sacrifice and the Song After, which speaks of the self. Here
is the fine story of Samvarta and Marutta. The treasure of gold is found.
Parikshit is born, burned by the fire of the weapon, and brought back to life by
Krishna. The horse is let loose for the sacrifice, and the Pandava follows it,
fighting resentful princes wherever it goes. Arjuna comes close to death in
battle with Babhruvahana, the son of Chitrangada, whose father had made her his
heir. And here is the story of the mongoose at the great horse sacrifice. That
is the fourteenth book, very wonderful. It has a hundred and thirty-three
chapters, and three thousand, three hundred and twenty verses.

The **fifteenth book** is the **Hermitage**. Its parts are: Living in the
Hermitage; Seeing the Sons; and the Coming of Narada. Here the king,
Dhritarashtra, gives up the kingdom and goes with Gandhari and Vidura to the
hermitage. When she sees him set out, the good Pritha follows him, giving up her
sons' kingdom, for she delights in serving her elders. Here the king sees his
dead sons and grandsons and the other kings, the heroes who had gone to the
other world, come back again, by the grace of the seer Krishna Dvaipayana. He
sees that highest wonder and lets go of his grief, and with his wife he reaches
the highest perfection. Here Vidura, holding to dharma, goes the good way, and
so does Sanjaya, the wise and self-controlled son of Gavalgana. And here
Yudhishthira, the king of dharma, sees Narada, and hears from him of the great
slaughter of the Vrishnis. It has forty-two chapters, and one thousand, five
hundred and six verses.

The **sixteenth book** is the terrible book of the **Iron Club**. Here the
Vrishnis, tigers among men who could bear the touch of weapons in battle, are
crushed by the curse of the brahmins near the salt sea. At a drinking party,
drunk and driven by fate, they kill one another with blades of eraka grass that
turn into thunderbolts. Having brought about the end of everything, Balarama and
Krishna do not pass beyond Time when it comes, for Time takes all things
equally. Arjuna comes to Dvaravati, empty of the Vrishnis, and falls into
despair and the deepest pain. He honours his mother's brother, Vasudeva, the
best of the Yadus, and sees the great slaughter of the Yadu heroes at their
drinking place. He has the bodies of Krishna and of great Balarama cremated, and
of the chief Vrishnis. He takes the old men and the children away from
Dvaravati, and in that bitter disaster he sees the Gandiva bow defeated, all the
divine weapons deaf to his call, the Vrishni women lost, and how nothing that is
powerful lasts. Seeing this, he is sick of the world, and, urged by Vyasa's
words, he goes to the king of dharma and chooses to renounce it all. It has
eight chapters and three hundred verses.

The **seventeenth book** is the **Great Journey**. Here the Pandavas, bulls
among men, give up the kingdom and, with the lady Draupadi, reach the highest
perfection. It has three chapters and a hundred and twenty verses.

The **eighteenth book** is **Heaven**, divine and more than human. It has five
chapters and two hundred verses.

Those are the eighteen books, told in full. After them come the supplements: the
Harivamsha, the Lineage of Hari, which is called an old story, and the Future, a
great and wonderful book among the supplements. So the hundred parts are
complete, as the great Vyasa spoke them, and as the son of the suta,
Lomaharshana's son, told them again, rightly, in the Naimisha forest.

---

"That is the whole Bharata," said Ugrashravas, "told in brief, by its parts.
Eighteen full armies came together, eager to fight, and that great and terrible
war lasted eighteen days.

"A twice-born man who knows the four Vedas, with their limbs and their
Upanishads, but does not know this story, cannot be called learned.

"Once you have heard this tale, nothing else is worth hearing. It is like
hearing the cuckoo's song, after which the crow's voice is harsh. The minds of
poets are born from this best of histories, as the three worlds are made from
the five elements. The old stories live inside this one, brahmins, as the four
kinds of creatures live inside the sky. Every deed and every virtue rests on it,
as all the senses rest on the mind's many workings. There is no tale on earth
that does not lean on this one, as the body cannot live without food. All the
best poets live on it, as servants who want to rise depend on a noble master.

"It came from the lips of Dvaipayana. It has no measure. It is holy and
purifying, it takes away sin, and it brings good. Whoever takes in the Bharata
as it is recited, what need has he to bathe in the waters of the holy lake
Pushkara?

"This is the greatest of all stories, and its meaning is great. It has been laid
out here, in its parts. Once men have heard it first like this, it is easy to
enter, the way a boat makes the wide salt sea easy to cross."

**Choices.**

* **Order.** The CE first names all hundred parts (1.2.34–69), then summarises
  the eighteen books (1.2.72–232). Here each book's part names are given
  just before its summary, so the reader has one map of the story instead
  of two. No part or event is dropped.
* **Part names** are translated, e.g. *Sambhava* as "the Births" and
  *Vaivāhika* as "the Wedding". Names that are also people or places stay
  as they are (Paushya, Pauloma, Astika, Hidimba, Chaitraratha).
* **Counts.** The chapter and verse numbers are the text's own. They do not
  match the Critical Edition's actual size (for example, the CE's Book 1 has
  225 chapters, while 1.2.95 says 218). The novel reports what the text
  says.
* **The ages.** 1.2.3 "the junction of Treta and Dvapara" and 1.2.9 "the
  interval of Kali and Dvapara" are given as "between the second age and the
  third" and "between the third age and the last, the Kali age". The four
  ages, in order, are Krita, Treta, Dvapara and Kali.
* **The supplements.** 1.2.69 and 1.2.233 name the *Harivaṃśa* and the
  *Bhaviṣya* as *khila*, supplements beyond the eighteen books. Following the
  Canon Policy, the novel tells the eighteen books only.
* **Book boundaries in the list of parts.** The list (1.2.34–69) does not mark
  where one book ends and the next begins, so each part is placed by where
  the CE narrates it. The Rites for the Dead (*śrāddha*) go with Book 11,
  where the bodies are burned (11.26; 1.2.193). The Consecration of
  Yudhishthira and the Crushing of Charvaka go with Book 12 (12.39–40). The
  Consecration of Bhishma comes in the list between Amba (the end of Book 5)
  and the Making of the Land of Jambu (the start of Book 6), and is placed at
  the head of Book 6 as the list orders it. The CE narrates Bhishma's
  appointment as commander at 5.153.31.
* 1.2.185 *apāṇḍavāya*, rendered "Let there be no Pandavas!", is the curse
  word spoken with the weapon.
* 1.2.209 *putrikā*: Chitrangada is an "appointed daughter", whose son counts
  as her father's heir. Rendered "whose father had made her his heir".
