# The Five Indras

"Long ago," said Vyasa, "the gods held a long sacrifice in the Naimisha forest.
There, king, Yama, Vivasvat's son, served as the slaughterer. So Yama, while he
was consecrated there, king, killed no one among living creatures. Then those
creatures grew very many, for time passed them by and they were freed from
death.

"Then Shakra, Varuna and Kubera, the Sadhyas, the Rudras, the Vasus and the two
Ashvins, and other gods too, came to Prajapati, the maker of the world. They
gathered and said to the teacher of the worlds, 'A terrible fear has come on us
from the growing number of men. Troubled by that fear, and wanting to be at
ease, we have all come to you for shelter.'

"'Why should you fear men,' said Brahma, 'when you are all immortal? Let no fear
ever come to you from mortals.'

"'Mortals have become immortal,' said the gods. 'There is no difference between
us any more. That is what troubles us, and we have come here so that there may
be a difference.'

"'Vivasvat's son is busy with the sacrifice,' said Brahma, 'and that is why
these men do not die. When he is free again, and all his work is done, then
their time to end will come. Vivasvat's son's body will be made mighty by your
strength and set to work. It will be their end when the time of their ending
comes. For that body will be a power among men.'

---

"When they had heard the first-born god's words, the gods went back to where the
gods were sacrificing. As those mighty ones sat there together, they saw a lotus
in the Bhagirathi. They were amazed when they saw it, and Indra, the hero among
them, set out. Where the Ganga springs out for ever, he saw a woman as bright as
fire. The woman was weeping there. She had come for water, and she stood in the
goddess Ganga. A drop of her tears fell into the water, and there it was a
golden lotus.

"When he saw that wonder, the wielder of the thunderbolt went close to the woman
and asked her, 'Who are you? Why are you weeping? For whose sake? Tell me the
truth, if you will.'

"'You will know who I am, Shakra,' said the woman, 'and why I weep, unhappy as I
am. Come, king. I will go ahead. You will see why it is that I weep.'

"He followed her as she went. Close by he saw a handsome young man seated on a
lion throne with a young woman beside him, playing at dice on the peak of the
king of mountains.

"The king of the gods said to him, 'Know that this whole world is mine, under my
power. I am its lord.' He said this in anger, when he saw the young man wholly
absorbed in the dice.

"When he saw Shakra angry, the god laughed and looked up slowly at Shakra. Then
the king of the gods was frozen. At that look he stood still like a post.

"When he had had enough of his game, the god said to the weeping woman, 'Bring
him here, near me, so that pride never enters him again.'

"As soon as she touched him, Shakra's limbs went slack, and he fell to the
ground. The blessed god of fierce power said to him, 'Never do such a thing
again, Shakra. Roll aside this great king of mountains, for your strength and
courage are beyond measure, and go into its heart, where others like you sit,
bright as the sun.'

"He opened up the peak of the great mountain, and he saw four others of the same
splendour. When he saw them he was grieved. *Surely I will not become like
these?*

"Then the mountain god opened his eyes wide and said in anger to the wielder of
the thunderbolt, 'Go into this cave, god of a hundred sacrifices, since in your
childishness you scorned me just now.'

"When the lord said this to him, the king of the gods shook violently at the
blow, his limbs slack, like a pipal leaf blown by the wind on the peak of the
king of mountains. Told this so suddenly, he joined his hands, bowed his head,
and, trembling, said to the fierce god of many forms, 'Blessed one, look on me
with favour today.'

"The god of the fierce bow laughed at him. 'Those with such a nature do not win
favour here. These others were like this before. So go into this cave and lie
there. Your favour will come in this way, have no doubt. All of you must enter a
human womb. There you will do deeds that no one can withstand, and bring many
others to their death. Then you will come back to the world of Indra, the
precious world you won before by your own deeds. All that I have said must be
done, and much else, for many purposes.'

"'We will go from the world of the gods to the world of men,' said the earlier
Indras, 'where release is ordained, hard to win. But let the gods place us in
our mother: Dharma, Vayu, Maghavan and the Ashvins.'

"When he heard this, the wielder of the thunderbolt spoke again to the best of
the gods. 'By my own power I will give a man for the task, born of me, to be the
fifth of them.'

"The blessed god of the fierce bow, in his kindness, granted their wish as they
had asked. And he appointed that woman, lovely to all the world, who was Shri,
to be their wife among men.

"Then the god went with them to Narayana, who is beyond measure, and he too
ordained it all. Then they were all born on earth. And Hari pulled out two
hairs, one white and one black. Those two hairs entered two women of the Yadu
family, Rohini and Devaki. Of the two, one became Baladeva, and the second, the
black one, became Keshava.

"Those who were once shut up in the form of Indras in that cave of the northern
mountain are these mighty Pandavas here. The Pandava Savyasachin is a portion of
Shakra. That is how these Pandavas were born, king, who were Indras before. And
Lakshmi was appointed long ago to be their wife. She is this Draupadi of divine
form. For how else, except by the design of the gods, could a woman rise from
the ground at the end of a rite, with beauty like the moon and the sun, and a
sweet scent that carries for two miles?

"And here is another thing I give you gladly, lord of men, a wonderful boon:
divine sight. See Kunti's sons in their holy, divine former bodies."

---

Then Vyasa, the pure brahmin of the most generous deeds, by his austerity gave
the king divine sight. And the king saw them all, just as they were, in their
former bodies. He saw them divine, wearing golden crowns and garlands, like
Shakra, the colour of fire and of the sun. They wore bands in their hair. They
were handsome and young, broad-chested, as tall as palm trees. They shone
brightly in heavenly clothes without a speck of dust, golden, with the finest
garlands. They were like the three-eyed gods themselves, or the Vasus, or the
heavenly Adityas, with every good quality. When he saw the former Indras so
handsome, King Drupada was pleased and amazed.

Having gained that boundless divine vision, the lord of kings saw his radiant
daughter too. She was like Shri herself in bodily form, a wife worthy of them in
beauty, splendour and fame.

When he saw that great wonder, he clasped the feet of Satyavati's son. "In you,
great seer," he said, with a glad heart, "this is no wonder."

"In a grove of austerity," said Vyasa, "there was the daughter of a great seer.
She was beautiful and virtuous, but she did not find a husband. She pleased
Shankara with fierce austerity, so they say, and the lord, pleased, said to her
himself, 'Choose what you wish.'

"The girl said to the lord, the god who gives boons, 'I want a husband with
every good quality,' again and again.

"Then the lord of the gods, pleased, gave her that boon. 'You will have five
excellent husbands,' said Shankara.

"Trying to win the god's favour, she spoke again. 'I deserve from you one
husband with good qualities.'

"The god of gods, pleased with her, spoke again, kindly. 'Five times you said to
me, "Give me a husband." So it will be, good lady. Bless you. When you have gone
into another body, it will be as you said.'

"Drupada, she has been born as your godlike daughter. Blameless Krishna, the
daughter of the Prishata line, is ordained to be the wife of five. She is the
Shri of heaven, born in the great sacrifice for the Pandavas' sake. She
practised terrible austerity here, and she has come to be your daughter. This
lovely goddess, whom the gods love, is the one wife of five by her own deeds.
She was created by the Self-born himself to be the wife of gods. Now that you
have heard this, King Drupada, do as you wish."

**The CE's own doublets.**

* **Two reasons for five husbands, and a third telling.** Here Vyasa gives two reasons: the five
  Indras with Shri as their wife (1.189.1–34) and the seer's daughter who asked five times
  (1.189.41–46). The second repeats, nearly word for word, what Vyasa told the Pandavas at
  Ekachakra (1.157.6–13). Both are kept. The name "Nalayani" and the story of her marriage to
  Maudgalya are only in the appendix (App. I No. 100, 102). See ledger L-93.

**Divergences.**

* **The names of the earlier Indras.** The vulgate names the four earlier Indras as
  Vishvabhuj, Ritadhaman, Shibi and Shanti, with Tejasvin as the fifth (\*1916). The CE does not
  name them.
* The vulgate adds that Indra "made Nara, the chief of the gods, the fifth" (\*1918). In the CE
  Indra offers "a man born of me" (1.189.28).
* The vulgate adds the story of King Nitantu's five sons (App. I No. 101). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.189.1 *śāmitram*: the office of the priest who slaughters the animal at a sacrifice.
* 1.189.8: Brahma's words about Yama's body are obscure. They are kept close to the text.
* 1.189.21 *giriśaḥ*: "the mountain god", Shiva. The text names him Shankara at 1.189.42. The
  young god at dice is not named until then.
* 1.189.23–25 *śeṣa*: taken as "favour", in the sense of the grace left over for a devotee.
  Ganguli renders 1.189.24 as "those … never obtain my grace".
* 1.189.23 *sahasā*: "so suddenly".
* 1.189.27 *jananyām*: "in our mother", in one mother. The four gods named are the fathers of
  Kunti's three sons and Madri's two.
* 1.189.31: Hari's two hairs, white and black, become Baladeva and Krishna. It is the CE's own
  text. See ledger L-97.
* 1.189.38 *tryakṣān*: "three-eyed ones", the Rudras.
* 1.189.47 *pārṣatī*: "the daughter of the Prishata line".
