# The Bird's Head

Arjuna took the greatest pains in honouring his teacher, and the greatest care
with his weapons, and he became Drona's favourite. Drona called the cook aside
and told him, "You must never serve Arjuna his food in the dark."

Then one day, while Arjuna was eating, a wind blew up, and it put out the lamp
that was burning there. Arjuna went on eating his food, and his hand did not
miss his mouth, for the hand of a strong man always knows its way to his food
from habit. The Pandava understood that it came from practice, and he began to
practise at night.

Drona heard the twang of his bowstring, Bharata. He got up, came to him,
embraced him, and said, "I will do all I can to see that there is no other
archer like you in the world. I tell you truly."

Then Drona taught Arjuna more, how to fight from chariots, on elephants and
horses, and on the ground. He taught the Pandava to fight with the mace, with
the sword, with the lance, the spear and the javelin, and to fight in the melee.
When they saw his skill, kings and princes came by the thousand, eager to learn
archery.

---

Then, great king, Ekalavya, the son of Hiranyadhanus, king of the Nishadas, came
to Drona. But Drona, who knew dharma, would not take him as a pupil in archery,
thinking, *He is a Nishada*, out of regard for the others. The tamer of enemies
touched Drona's feet with his head and went into the forest. There he made a
Drona out of clay. He treated it with the utmost respect, as his teacher, and he
set himself to practise with arrows and weapons under the strictest discipline.
With the greatest faith and the greatest effort, he became very quick in
drawing, aiming and loosing.

Then one day, with Drona's leave, the Kurus and the Pandavas all drove out in
their chariots to hunt, tamers of enemies. A man followed the Pandavas by
chance, king, alone, carrying their gear and leading a dog. While they wandered
about, each busy with his own affairs, the dog strayed off in the forest and
came upon the Nishada. When it saw the Nishada in the forest, dark, his body
smeared with dirt, wearing a black deerskin, it stood near him and barked. Then,
as it barked, he loosed seven arrows into its mouth, all as if at once, showing
how quick he was with his weapon. The dog came back to the Pandavas with its
mouth full of arrows. When the heroic Pandavas saw it, they were amazed. They
saw how quick he was, and how he could shoot by sound, and they were ashamed.
They praised him in every way.

Then the Pandavas searched for the forest dweller, king, and found him in the
forest, shooting arrows without stopping. He looked so strange that they did not
know him. So they asked him, "Who are you? Whose son are you?"

"Heroes," said Ekalavya, "know that I am the son of Hiranyadhanus, king of the
Nishadas, and a pupil of Drona. I have worked hard at archery."

When they had learned the truth about him, the Pandavas went back and told Drona
everything that had happened, and the wonder of it. But Arjuna, Kunti's son,
kept thinking of Ekalavya, king. He went to Drona in private and said to him,
with the freedom of affection, "You embraced me, when I was alone, and told me
lovingly, 'No pupil of mine will be better than you.' Then how is it that you
have another pupil, better than me, better than anyone in the world, a mighty
man, the son of the king of the Nishadas?"

Drona thought for a moment and made up his mind. He took Savyasachin with him
and went to the Nishada. He saw Ekalavya, his body smeared with dirt, his hair
matted, wearing rags, bow in hand, shooting arrows without stopping. When
Ekalavya saw Drona coming near, he went to meet him, clasped his feet, and
touched the ground with his head. The Nishada honoured Drona as the rules
require. He told him he was his pupil, and stood before him with joined hands.

Then Drona said to Ekalavya, king, "If you are my pupil, then give me my fee at
once."

When he heard this, Ekalavya was delighted. "What shall I give, lord? Let my
teacher command me. There is nothing I would not give to my teacher, best of
those who know Brahman."

"Give me your right thumb," said Drona.

Ekalavya heard Drona's cruel words. But he kept his promise, for he was always
devoted to truth. His face was just as happy, and his heart just as cheerful.
Without a moment's thought, he cut off his thumb and gave it to Drona.

After that the Nishada drew the bowstring with his fingers. But he was never as
quick as he had been before, lord of men. Then Arjuna was glad, and the fever
left his heart. And Drona's word came true: no one else surpassed Arjuna.

---

Two of Drona's pupils among the Kurus, Duryodhana and Bhima, were outstanding at
the mace. Ashvatthama excelled in all the secret arts. The twins were better
with the sword than any other men. Yudhishthira was the best chariot fighter.
But Dhananjaya was the best in everything. He was famous to the ocean's edge as
the leader of the leaders of chariot bands. In intelligence, in concentration,
in strength and in energy, and in all the weapons, the Pandava excelled. In his
weapons and in his love for his teacher, Arjuna stood out. The teaching was the
same for all, but in skill and in courage Arjuna alone was a great warrior
beyond all the princes. The wicked sons of Dhritarashtra could not bear
Bhimasena's strength or Dhananjaya's learning, lord of men.

When they had all mastered every branch of learning, Drona brought them together
to test their knowledge of arms, bull among men. He had craftsmen make an
artificial bird, a vulture, and set it on top of a tree without the princes
knowing, and he pointed it out as the target.

"All of you, take up your bows quickly," said Drona, "and stand with your arrows
fitted, aiming at this bird. As soon as I speak, cut off its head. I will call
on you one at a time, boys. Do as I say."

Then the best of the Angirasas spoke first to Yudhishthira. "Fit your arrow,
unconquerable one, and let it go when I have finished speaking."

So Yudhishthira took up his loud bow first, and stood aiming at the bird, as his
teacher had told him. When the joy of the Kurus had drawn his bow, after a
moment Drona said to him, "Do you see the bird on top of the tree, prince?"

"I see it," Yudhishthira answered his teacher.

After another moment Drona spoke to him again. "Now do you see this tree, or me,
or your brothers?"

"I see this tree," said Kunti's son, "and you, and my brothers, and the bird,"
again and again.

"Step back," said Drona, as if displeased. "You cannot hit this target," he said
scornfully.

Then, in the same way, the famous teacher tested Duryodhana and the rest of
Dhritarashtra's sons, and his other pupils, Bhima and the others, and the kings
from other lands. "We see everything," they all said, and he scorned them all.

Then Drona smiled and spoke to Dhananjaya. "Now you must shoot at this target.
Listen. You must let go your arrow as soon as I speak. Draw your bow, son, and
wait a moment."

Savyasachin bent his bow into a circle and stood aiming at the target, as his
teacher had told him. After a moment Drona spoke to him in the same way. "Do you
see the bird sitting there, and the tree, and me?"

"I see the bird," Partha said to Drona. "I do not see the tree, or you,
Bharata."

Then Drona was pleased. After a moment the unconquerable one spoke again to the
best of the Pandava chariot fighters. "If you see the bird, then tell me again."

"I see the bird's head," he said, "not its body."

When Arjuna said this, the hair on Drona's body stood on end with joy. "Shoot,"
he said to Partha, and Partha shot without a thought. With a sharp, razor-headed
arrow the Pandava cut the head off the bird on the tree, and brought it down
with force. When that was done, Drona embraced Phalguna. And he thought Drupada
already beaten in battle, with all his followers.

---

Some time later, bull of the Bharatas, the best of the Angirasas went with his
pupils to bathe in the Ganga. When Drona had waded into the water, a strong
crocodile that lived there, driven by Time, seized him by the calf. Though he
could have freed himself, he urged all his pupils on, as if in haste. "Kill the
crocodile and save me!"

At the very moment he spoke, Bibhatsu struck the crocodile under the water with
five sharp arrows. The others stood there bewildered. When Drona saw the Pandava
act, he thought him the best of all his pupils, and he was pleased. Cut to
pieces by Partha's arrows, the crocodile let go of the great man's leg and died.

Then Bharadvaja's son said to the great warrior, "Take this weapon, strong-armed
one. It is the best of weapons, very hard to withstand, the weapon called
Brahmashiras, with the art of using it and of calling it back. You must never
use it against men. If it is loosed at anyone of little power, it could burn up
the world. This weapon, son, is said to be without equal in the worlds. Keep it
with care, and hear what I say. If ever an enemy who is not human attacks you,
hero, then use this weapon in battle to kill him."

"So be it," Bibhatsu promised, with joined hands, and he received that supreme
weapon. And his teacher said to him again, "No other man in the world will be an
archer equal to you."

**Divergences.**

* **The head, not the eye.** In the popular telling Arjuna says he sees only the
  bird's eye. In the CE he says, "I see the bird's head, not its body" (*śiraḥ paśyāmi
  bhāsasya na gātram*, 1.123.64), and he cuts off its head (1.123.66). See ledger
  L-84.
* **Ekalavya.** The thumb is in the CE (ledger L-08). The vulgate has Drona ask for
  "one thumb of the right hand" and Ekalavya cut off "both thumbs" (\*1394–\*1395), and
  has Drona praise his truth (\*1397–\*1399). The CE has none of this. It says only that
  Drona's words were "cruel" (*dāruṇam*, 1.123.36).

**Choices.**

* 1.123.4: the reason Arjuna's hand finds his mouth, "from long habit", is the text's.
* 1.123.11 *teṣām evānvavekṣayā*: "out of regard for them", i.e. for the Kuru
  princes. The CE gives Drona's reason in those words only.
* 1.123.19 *yugapad yathā*: "as if at once".
* 1.123.21 *śabdavedhitva*: shooting by sound.
* 1.123.33 *vetanam*: "payment", "fee"; 1.123.35 *dakṣiṇa*: "right", with a play on
  *dakṣiṇā*, "a teacher's fee".
* 1.123.39 *vigatajvaraḥ*: "his fever gone", i.e. his anxiety.
* 1.123.41 *rahasyeṣu*: "in the secret arts" of weapons.
* 1.123.46 *bhāsa*: a bird of prey, perhaps a vulture. Rendered "a vulture" once,
  then "the bird".
* 1.123.49 *aṅgirasāṃ varaḥ*: Drona, "best of the Angirasas", his lineage.
* 1.123.62: Arjuna's "Bharata" addresses Drona. Kept.
* 1.123.66 *kṣureṇa*: a razor-headed arrow.
