# Drupada's Test

When he was asked this, Prince Dhrishtadyumna, the best of the Somakas, was
delighted, and he told his father what had happened, and who had taken Krishna.

"That young man with long red eyes," he said, "wearing a black deerskin, as
handsome as a god, who strung the finest bow and brought the target down to the
ground, went away unhindered, swift, surrounded and honoured by the chief
brahmins. He strode like the wielder of the thunderbolt among the sons of Diti,
attended by all the gods and seers. And Krishna took hold of his deerskin and
followed him gladly, like a cow elephant after her mate. The kings could not
bear it, and they rushed at him in anger. Then another man, in the middle of the
kings, tore up a great tree and drove the crowds of kings back, like angry Death
driving living things before him.

"While the kings looked on, lord of men, those two best of men took Krishna and
went, shining like the moon and the sun, to a potter's workshop outside the
city. A woman sat there like a flame of fire. I think she is their mother. And
round her sat three other fine men like fires. The two of them bowed at her
feet, and told Krishna, 'Greet her.' They handed Krishna over to her and stayed
there, and then those best of men went out to beg for alms.

"Krishna took their alms, made the offering to the gods, and gave a share to a
brahmin. She served the old woman and those fine men, and then she ate herself.
Then they all slept, king. Krishna was the pillow for their feet. Their bed was
on the ground, spread with darbha grass and fine deerskins. Like dark clouds
thundering, they told one another wonderful stories. The heroes did not tell the
kind of stories vaishyas or shudras would tell, or brahmins either. There is no
doubt they are bulls among kshatriyas, king, from the way they talk of war. Our
hope has plainly come true, for we have heard that the Parthas escaped the fire.
From the way he strung the bow by force and brought down the target, and from
the way they talk to each other, the Parthas are surely going about in
disguise."

---

Then King Drupada, delighted, sent his family priest to them. "Say to them, 'We
would know you. Are you the great sons of Pandu?'"

The king's priest took the message, went, and praised them. Then, knowing the
proper order, he gave them the king's whole message properly, point by point.

"Drupada, king of the Panchalas, lord of the earth, wants to know who you are,
you who deserve the best. For since he saw this man who pierced the target, he
can see no end to his joy. So tell us, in order, your kin, your family and your
line. Set your foot on the heads of your enemies. Delight this heart of mine,
the heart of the Panchala king and his people. King Pandu was King Drupada's
dear friend, as dear as himself. This was his wish: 'If only this daughter of
mine could be the Kaurava's daughter-in-law.' And this wish has always been in
King Drupada's heart, you of blameless form: that long-armed Arjuna might win my
daughter by dharma."

When the priest had said this, and stood there modestly, the king looked at him
and said to Bhima, who was near, "Give him water for his feet and the guest
offering. He is King Drupada's respected priest, and he should be shown more
honour than most."

Bhima did so, lord of men, and the priest accepted the honour.

When the priest was comfortably seated, Yudhishthira said to the brahmin, "The
Panchala king gave his daughter as he wished, looking to his own dharma. King
Drupada set the price for her, and this hero met it. No question of class was
raised there, nor of livelihood, nor of family or line. She was given for the
bow strung and the target pierced. That is how this great man won Krishna here,
in the middle of the crowd of kings.

"Since this is so, King Drupada of the Somakas has no reason to grieve today and
make himself unhappy. And King Drupada's wish will come true as well. For I
think, brahmin, that this princess, who could not be had by others, has been
rightly won. That bow could not be strung by a man of little strength. Nor could
a man untrained in weapons, or of low birth, bring down the target like that. So
the Panchala king has no reason to grieve for his daughter today. No man on
earth could have brought the target down in any other way."

While Yudhishthira was still speaking, another man came quickly from the
Panchala king to announce that the food was ready.

"King Drupada has had food prepared for the wedding party," said the messenger,
"for the wedding. Come to it when you have finished everything, and Krishna too.
Do not be long. Here are chariots fit for kings, painted with golden lotuses and
yoked with fine horses. Mount them, all of you, and come to the Panchala king's
palace."

---

So the bulls of the Kurus set out. They sent the priest ahead and mounted those
great carriages, and Kunti and Krishna rode together.

Now the king, Bharata, had heard what the Dharma King had said to the priest.
So, simply to test the best of the Kurus, he gathered things of many kinds. He
had fruit brought, and fine garlands, shields, armour and seats, and cows, king,
and ropes, and other things for farming. All the things that belong to the other
crafts were set out there, complete, and everything used for sport. The king
brought all these there. There was gleaming armour for chariots and horses,
great swords, and fine horse chariots, excellent bows and the best arrows,
spears and lances decked with gold. There were javelins and bhushundis and axes,
and all the gear of war, and beds and seats finely made, of every kind.

Kunti took the good Krishna and went into Drupada's women's quarters, and the
women, in good spirits, honoured her, the queen of the Kuru king.

Then the king, and all the king's ministers, the king's sons and his friends,
and all his servants, king, looked at those heroes. They had the gait of lions
and the eyes of great bulls. They wore deerskins as their upper garments. Their
shoulders were broad, and their arms were long, like the coils of lords of
snakes. And they were all overjoyed.

Without hesitation the heroes sat down in order of age on the finest seats, with
footstools, and they showed no wonder at the costly things. Men and women
servants, finely dressed, and the cooks brought them food of every kind, fit for
kings, on plates of gold and silver. When the heroes had eaten there as much as
they wanted, well content, they passed by all the other goods and went to the
gear of war.

When Drupada's son and the king, with all his chief ministers, saw this, they
came up gladly and honoured Kunti's sons, the sons and grandsons of kings.

**Divergences.**

* The vulgate adds a bathing and adorning of the Pandavas by the women before they set out
  (\*1902). Not in the CE.
* The vulgate has Dhrishtadyumna guess that the archer was the man who fought "the suta's son"
  and the tree-wielder the man who threw Shalya (\*1898, \*1899). The CE's report does not
  name Karna or Shalya.

**Choices.**

* 1.185.4 *nāgaṃ yathā nāgavadhūḥ*: "like a cow elephant after her mate".
* 1.185.5 *mahīpraroham*: "a great tree", literally "that which grows from the earth".
* 1.185.7 *mama pratarkaḥ*: "I think", Dhrishtadyumna's guess that the woman is their mother.
* 1.185.14–19: the priest speaks in Drupada's own voice ("this heart of mine"). The wish that
  Draupadi might be the Kaurava's daughter-in-law is Drupada's.
* 1.185.20: "the king" who tells Bhima to honour the priest is Yudhishthira.
* 1.185.23 *na tatra varṇeṣu kṛtā vivakṣā*: "no question of class was raised". Yudhishthira's
  answer to the kings' complaint at 1.180.6.
* 1.185.25 *saumakiḥ*: Drupada, "of the Somakas".
* 1.186.1 *janyārtham*: "for the wedding party".
* 1.186.4 *jijñāsayā*: "to test". The goods are laid out to see which the guests would choose.
* 1.186.8 *bhuśuṇḍyaḥ*: a weapon of uncertain form. The novel keeps the name.
* 1.186.9 *kauravarājapatnīm*: "the queen of the Kuru king", that is Kunti, Pandu's widow, as in Ganguli.
* 1.186.10 *gūḍhottarāṃsān*: "broad-shouldered", literally "with the collarbones hidden".
