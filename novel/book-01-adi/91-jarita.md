# Jarita

"Why did Agni not burn the Sharngakas," said Janamejaya, "when things were as
they were, while that forest was burning? Tell me quickly, brahmin. You have
told the reason why Ashvasena and the danava Maya were not burned, brahmin, but
you have not told it for the Sharngakas. The sparing of the Sharngas is a
wonder, brahmin. Tell me how they were not destroyed in that onslaught of fire."

"I will tell you all of it, Bharata," said Vaishampayana, "just as it happened,
why Agni did not burn the Sharngakas when things were as they were."

There was a great seer named Mandapala, the most eminent of those who know
dharma, an ascetic strict in his vows, and learned. He followed the path of the
seers who keep their seed drawn up, king. He studied the Veda, delighted in
dharma, practised austerity and mastered his senses. When he had reached the far
shore of austerity, he gave up his body, Bharata, and went to the world of the
ancestors. But he did not find the fruit of his austerity there. When he saw
that the worlds he had won by his austerity bore no fruit, he asked the gods who
sat near the Dharma King, "Why are these worlds of mine, won by austerity, shut
to me? What have I left undone there, that this is the fruit of my deeds? I will
do there whatever it is for which this is shut. Tell me the fruit of this
austerity, gods."

"Men are born debtors, brahmin," said the gods. "Hear to what. To the rites, to
celibate study, and to offspring, without a doubt. All of this is paid off by
sacrifice, by austerity, and by sons. You are an ascetic and you have
sacrificed, but you have no offspring. These worlds of yours are shut for the
sake of offspring. Have children, and then you will enjoy the eternal worlds. A
son saves his father from the hell called Put, sage. So strive to have children,
best of the twice-born."

When he heard the gods, Mandapala thought, *Where can I have offspring quickly,
and many of them?* As he thought, he came to the birds that have many young.
Becoming a male Sharngaka, he went to a female Sharngika named Jarita. On her he
fathered four sons who could speak of Brahman. He left them there, in the
forest, the little sons still in their eggs, with their mother, and the sage
went off to Lapita.

When that fortunate one had gone to Lapita, Bharata, Jarita, anxious with love
for her young, thought a great deal. Though he had abandoned them, she did not
abandon her little sons, the seers in their eggs, whom no one should abandon, in
the Khandava, king. In her distress, and faint with love, she reared them when
they hatched, by her own labour.

Then the seer Mandapala, as he wandered in that forest with Lapita, saw Agni
coming to burn the Khandava. Knowing what the fire meant to do, and knowing that
his sons were small, the brahmin seer, in fear, praised Jatavedas, the mighty
guardian of the world, and entrusted his sons to him.

"You, Agni," said Mandapala, "are the mouth of all the gods. You are the carrier
of offerings. You move hidden within all creatures, purifier. The poets call you
one, and call you threefold. Dividing you eightfold, they made you the carrier
of the sacrifice. The great seers say that this universe was made by you.
Without you, eater of offerings, the whole world would cease at once. Bowing to
you, brahmins go with their wives and sons to the eternal place they have won by
their own deeds. They say, Agni, that you are the clouds that hang in the sky
with the lightning, and that the flames that come out of you burn all creatures.
Jatavedas, of great splendour, this making of the universe is yours. All that
is, moving and still, is your work. By you the waters were made first, and in
you is this whole world. In you the offerings to the gods and to the ancestors
are rightly set. Agni, you are the blaze, you are the Ordainer, you are
Brihaspati. You are the two Ashvins, the twins, Mitra and Soma, and you are the
wind."

Praised like this by Mandapala, the sage of boundless power, Agni was pleased
with him, king, and said to him with a glad heart, "What would you have me do
for you?"

With joined hands Mandapala said to the carrier of offerings, "As you burn the
Khandava forest, spare my sons."

"So be it," the blessed carrier of offerings promised him. And at that time he
blazed up in the Khandava, wanting to burn it.

---

When the fire had blazed up, the Sharngakas were in great distress. Frightened
and terrified, they could find no refuge. Seeing her little sons, their mother,
poor Jarita, burning with sorrow, lamented, lord of men.

"This terrible fire is coming this way, burning the undergrowth, setting the
world alight, fierce, making my sorrow grow. And these little ones pull at me,
with their little sense, without feathers, unable to walk, the refuge of our
ancestors. And the fire comes on, frightening them, licking at the trees. My
sons have no strength, and cannot get away. And I cannot carry my sons away with
me. Nor can I leave them. My heart seems to burn. Which son shall I leave, and
which shall I take with me? What would be right for me to do? What do you think,
my sons? I think about how to save you, and I can find nothing. I will cover you
with my body, and die with you.

"'This family rests on Jaritari, the eldest,' your father said. 'Sarisrikva will
have children and make the ancestors' family grow. Stambamitra will practise
austerity, and Drona will be the best of those who know Brahman.' With that,
your pitiless father went away, long ago. Which of you can I take and go with?
Which of you faces the greatest danger? What can I do, so that I do what is
right?"

So she was distraught, and by her own wits she could find no way to save her
sons from the fire. As she spoke like this, the Sharngas answered their mother.

"Give up your love for us, mother, and fly where there is no fire. If we die,
you will have other sons. But if you die, mother, our family will not go on.
Think of both, and do what is best for our family. Now is the time for you to do
it, mother. Do not let your love for us, your sons, destroy our family. For our
father, who wanted the heavenly worlds, must not have acted in vain."

"There is a rat's hole in the ground, here, near this tree," said Jarita. "Go
into it quickly. The fire will not harm you there. Then I will cover the hole
with dust, my sons. That is how I think we can stand against the blazing,
black-tracked fire. Then, when the fire has passed, I will come and clear away
the dust. Choose this way to escape from the fire."

"We have no feathers," said the Sharngakas. "We are only lumps of flesh, and the
flesh-eating rat would kill us. We see this danger, and we cannot go in there.
How can the fire not burn us? How can the rat not eat us? How can our father's
work not be in vain? How can our mother live? In the hole, the rat would destroy
us. For creatures of the sky, it is the fire. Think of both. Burning is better
than being eaten. Death in a hole, eaten by a rat, would be a shameful death.
Giving up the body to the fire is approved by the wise."

"A hawk carried off the rat that came out of this hole," said Jarita. "It took
the little creature in its claws. So there is no danger from that."

"We do not know that the rat was carried off by the hawk," said the Sharngakas.
"And there may be others here, and they are a danger to us too. It is not
certain that the fire will come. The wind has been known to turn back. But death
from the creatures that live in the hole would be certain, mother. An uncertain
death is better than a certain one, mother. Fly in the sky, as is right. You
will find fine sons."

"I saw the hawk coming near the hole," said Jarita. "The strong bird seized the
rat as it moved about and carried it off from the hole. I followed the hawk
quickly as it flew, and I blessed it as it carried off the mouse from the hole:
'King of hawks, who fly off with our enemy, may you live in heaven, golden,
without enemies.' When the hungry bird had eaten it, I took leave of it and came
back home. Go into the hole, my sons, without fear. There is no danger for you.
The hawk took the rat while I watched. There is no doubt of it."

"We do not know that the rat was taken from here before, mother," said the
Sharngakas. "We cannot go into the hole without knowing."

"But I know that the hawk took the mouse," said Jarita. "So there is nothing to
fear. Do as I say."

"Do not try to save us from this great danger by a pretence," said the
Sharngakas. "When minds are confused, such a thing is not done with good
judgement. We have done nothing for you, and you do not even know who we are.
You are suffering to rear us. Who are you to us, and what are we to you? You are
young and lovely, and able to look for a husband. Go after your husband. You
will have fine sons. And we, going into the fire, will gain good worlds. And if
the fire does not burn us, you can come back to us."

When they said this, the Sharngi left her sons in the Khandava and went quickly
to a safe place where the fire could not reach. Then the blazing carrier of
offerings, sharp of flame, came to where Mandapala's little sons were. When the
Sharngas saw the fire blazing with its own splendour, Jaritari spoke, so that
Agni could hear.

* 1.220.11: the three debts, to the rites, to celibate study and to offspring.

  given.

  text.
