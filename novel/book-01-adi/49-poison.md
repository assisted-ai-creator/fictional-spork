# Poison

Then the steward, the king and Bhishma, with their kinsmen, gave the funeral
feast for Pandu, with the offerings to the dead that are like nectar. They fed
the Kurus and the leading brahmins by the thousand, and gave heaps of jewels and
fine villages to the best of the brahmins. When the Pandavas, bulls of the
Bharatas, had been purified, the townsfolk took them and entered the city named
after the elephant. All the people of the city and the country grieved for that
bull of the Bharatas without end, as if one of their own kin had died.

When the funeral feast was over, Vyasa saw that the people were in sorrow. His
mother was dazed and stricken with grief and pain, and he spoke to her.

"The times of happiness are past, and cruel times are at hand. Each day will be
worse than the last. The earth's youth is gone. A terrible time is coming, full
of deceit and thick with every kind of wrong, when the ways of dharma and its
rites are lost. Go. Give up the world, and live in the forest of austerity,
disciplined. Do not stay to see the terrible destruction of your own family."

"So be it," said Satyavati. She went in to her daughter-in-law and said to her,
"Ambika, we have heard that through your son's bad policy the Bharatas will
perish, with all who follow them, and their grandsons too. So, if you agree,
bless you, let us take Kausalya, who is stricken with grief for her son, and go
to the forest."

"So be it," said Ambika. And Satyavati, who kept good vows, said farewell to
Bhishma and went to the forest with her two daughters-in-law, Bharata. There the
queens practised very fierce austerity, best of the Bharatas. Then they gave up
their bodies, great king, and went the way they longed for.

---

The Pandavas received the rites of passage laid down in the Veda. They grew up
in their father's house, enjoying its pleasures. Playing with Dhritarashtra's
sons in their father's house, the Pandavas excelled in every children's game.

In racing, in hitting the mark, in eating and in scattering the dust, Bhimasena
trounced all Dhritarashtra's sons. When they were playing gleefully, he would
catch them at hide-and-seek, grab them by the head and make them fight one
another. Wolf-belly alone crushed all those hundred and one mighty boys without
much trouble. The strong boy would seize them by the feet, throw them down with
force, and drag them along the ground, screaming, till their knees, heads and
eyes were scraped raw. When he played in the water, he would grab ten boys in
his arms, stay under the water, and let them go only when they were half dead.
When they climbed a tree to pick fruit, Bhima would shake the tree with a kick.
Struck by the force of the blow, the tree swayed, and the little boys came
tumbling down fast, fruit and all.

In wrestling, in racing, in the exercises, the boys never got the better of
Wolf-belly, though they strove with him. So Wolf-belly, rivalling
Dhritarashtra's sons, made himself deeply hated by them. But he did it out of
childishness, not out of malice.

Then Dhritarashtra's mighty son, knowing Bhimasena's famous strength, showed his
evil heart. He had turned from dharma and he had his eyes on wicked things. Out
of folly and greed for power, a wicked thought came to him.

*This Wolf-belly, Kunti's son, the middle one of Pandu's sons, is the strongest
of the strong. He must be killed by treachery. Then I will take his younger
brother and the eldest, Yudhishthira, by force and put them in chains, and I
will rule the earth.*

When the wicked Duryodhana had made up his mind to this, he was always looking
for a weak spot in great-hearted Bhima.

He had large, splendid tents built of cloth and blankets, Bharata, for playing
in the water, and he chose a spot of ground at Pramanakoti. When the games were
over, they all put on clean clothes and ornaments, and slowly ate the food, rich
in everything they could want. At the end of the day, tired from their games,
the heroes, the leaders of the Kurus, chose to spend the night in the pleasure
tents.

The mighty Bhima was worn out. He had exercised more than the others, carrying
the boys who had come to play in the water on his back. Wanting a place to
sleep, he climbed up on to the ground at Pramanakoti and fell asleep. He had
found a cool place to rest, and he was tired and dazed with drink. The Pandava
slept without moving, king, like a dead man.

Then Duryodhana quietly bound Bhima with ropes of creeper, and threw him from
the bank into the deep water, where the current was terrible. But Kunti's son
woke. He broke all his bonds, and Bhima, the best of fighters, rose up out of
the water again.

Another time, while he slept, Duryodhana had angry snakes with sharp fangs and
deadly poison bite him all over his body, in all his vital places. But though
the fangs of those fanged creatures struck his vital places, they could not
break the skin of the broad-chested boy, so hard was he. Bhima woke, and he
crushed all the snakes. And with a backhand blow he killed Duryodhana's
favourite charioteer.

Again Duryodhana had poison thrown into Bhimasena's food, fresh kalakuta, sharp
and strong, enough to make the hair stand on end. The son of the vaishya woman
told the Parthas, for he wished them well. But Wolf-belly ate it and digested it
without any change in him. That very sharp poison did not affect him at all.
Bhima, whose frame was terrible, digested even that.

So Duryodhana, Karna and Shakuni, Subala's son, tried to kill the Pandavas by
many means. And the Pandavas, tamers of enemies, knew all about it. But they
kept to Vidura's advice, and did not bring it out into the open.

**Divergences.**

* **No visit to the Nagas.** In the popular story Bhima sinks to the Naga world,
  is bitten by the snakes until their venom cures the poison, and is given the
  Nagas' elixir by Vasuki, gaining the strength of ten thousand elephants
  (\*1312, App. I No. 72; G 1.128). In the CE he simply wakes, breaks his bonds and
  climbs out (1.119.35). The same short version is told again at 3.13.74–77. See
  ledger L-81.
* **The charioteer.** The CE says Bhima killed "his dear charioteer" (1.119.38). At
  3.13.77 the same line appears; Ganguli translates it there as "the favourite
  charioteer of Duryodhana" (G 3.12). The novel names Duryodhana to make the
  pronoun clear.
* **Karna appears early.** Karna is named here among the plotters (1.119.42), though
  his coming to the Kuru court is not told until later (1.126). Kept.

**Choices.**

* 1.119.6 *pṛthivī gatayauvanā*: "the earth has lost her youth".
* 1.119.9 *durnayāt*: "through bad policy", Dhritarashtra's.
* 1.119.15 *pāṃsuvikarṣaṇa*: a game of scattering or dragging dust; the exact game is
  uncertain.
* 1.119.16 *kākanilīyana*: literally "crow-hiding", a hide-and-seek game.
* 1.119.23 *bālyān na drohacetasā*: "out of childishness, not with a hostile mind".
* 1.119.30, 32 *pramāṇakoṭi*: the place on the Ganga where the games were held.
* 1.119.33 *madavimohitaḥ*: "dazed with intoxication" (from drink).
* 1.119.40 *vaiśyāputraḥ*: Yuyutsu, the vaishya woman's son.
