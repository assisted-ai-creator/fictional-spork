# Sunda and Upasunda

"When my great-grandfathers had gained the kingdom in Indraprastha, rich in
austerity," said Janamejaya, "what did those great Pandavas do next? How did
Draupadi, their lawful wife, behave towards all of them, those great men, my
forefathers? And how did those fortunate kings, living with one woman, Krishna,
not fall out with one another? I want to hear it all in full, rich in austerity,
how they behaved to one another, joined as they were with Krishna."

With Dhritarashtra's leave, the Pandavas, the tigers among men, the tamers of
enemies, enjoyed themselves with Krishna now that they had their kingdom. When
he had gained the kingdom, Yudhishthira, of great splendour and true to his
word, ruled the earth by dharma with his brothers. The sons of Pandu lived there
in the greatest happiness. Their enemies were beaten. They were very wise, and
devoted to truth and dharma. Those bulls among men did all the business of the
city, and sat on costly royal seats.

Once, when all those great men were seated, the divine seer Narada came there by
chance. Yudhishthira gave him his own fine seat. When the divine seer was
seated, wise Yudhishthira himself gave him the guest offering, as the rules
require, and told him about the kingdom. The seer accepted the honour and was
pleased. He blessed him, and said, "Sit down." Given leave, King Yudhishthira
sat down. Then he sent word to Krishna that the blessed one had come. As soon as
she heard, Draupadi purified herself and, with her mind collected, went to where
Narada sat with the Pandavas. Drupada's daughter, who kept to dharma, bowed at
the divine seer's feet and stood with her hands joined, modestly covered. The
righteous seer, the best of seers, whose word is true, gave the princess many
blessings. Then the blessed Narada said to that blameless woman, "You may go."

When Krishna had gone, the blessed seer spoke in private to all the Pandavas,
with Yudhishthira at their head.

"The glorious Panchala princess is the lawful wife of you all. Make a rule so
that there is no quarrel among you over her. There were once two asura brothers,
Sunda and Upasunda. They were always together, no one else could kill them, and
they were famous in the three worlds. They had one kingdom and one house. They
shared one bed, one seat, one meal. And they killed each other for the sake of
Tilottama. So guard your friendship, which keeps each of you well disposed to
the others. Do something, Yudhishthira, so that there is no quarrel among you
here."

"Great sage," said Yudhishthira, "whose sons were the asuras Sunda and Upasunda?
How did the quarrel arise, and how did they kill each other? Whose was this
Tilottama? Was she an apsaras, or a daughter of the gods, whom they longed for
so madly that they killed each other? We want to hear all of it in full, rich in
austerity, just as it happened, brahmin. We are very curious."

---

"Listen, Partha," said Narada, "with your brothers, Yudhishthira, while I tell
you this ancient tale in full, just as it happened.

"Long ago, in the line of the great asura Hiranyakashipu, there was a lord of
the daityas named Nikumbha, splendid and strong. He had two sons of great
courage and fearsome might. They ate together, and neither went anywhere without
the other. Each did what pleased the other, and each spoke kindly to the other.
They had one character and one way of life, like one person made into two.

"When they had grown up, the mighty pair were of one mind in all they did. So,
of one mind, they set out to conquer the three worlds. They took the vow, went
to the Vindhya mountains, and practised fierce austerity there. After a long
time they were full of austerity's power. Worn out with hunger and thirst,
wearing matted hair and bark, their whole bodies caked with dirt, they lived on
air. They offered their own flesh into the fire. They stood on the tips of their
big toes, their arms raised and their eyes unblinking, keeping their vows for a
long time. Heated for a long time by the power of their austerity, the Vindhya
gave off smoke. It was a wonder.

"The gods were afraid when they saw the fierceness of their austerity, and to
break it, they put obstacles in their way. Again and again they tempted the two
with jewels and with women. But those two, strict in their great vows, did not
break their vow.

"Then the gods worked a trick of illusion on those great-spirited two. Their
sisters, mothers and wives, and their household, seemed to be pursued and struck
down by a rakshasa with a spear in his hand. In terror, their ornaments and hair
falling loose, their clothes slipping from them, they all ran to the two and
cried out, 'Save us!' But those two, strict in their great vows, did not break
their vow. When neither of them was shaken or distressed, the women and the
creature all vanished.

"Then the Grandfather himself, the grandfather of all the worlds, came to the
two great asuras and offered them a boon. When the brothers Sunda and Upasunda,
firm in their courage, saw the god, the Grandfather, they stood before him with
joined hands. Then together they said to the lord god, 'If the Grandfather is
pleased with this austerity of ours, and if the lord is gracious to us, let us
both know magic, know weapons, be strong, take any form we wish, and be
immortal.'

"'Apart from immortality,' said the Grandfather, 'you shall have all you ask.
Choose some other way of death, to make you equal to the immortals. You took up
this great austerity saying, "We will do this." Because of that, immortality is
not granted to you. You undertook your austerity to conquer the three worlds.
For that reason, lords of the daityas, I will not grant your wish.'

"'Let us have no fear,' said Sunda and Upasunda, 'from anything that exists in
the three worlds, moving or still, Grandfather, except from each other.'

"'I grant you your wish,' said the Grandfather, 'what you have asked, as you
have said it. And this will be the way of your death, just so.'

"Then, when he had given them this boon, the Grandfather turned them back from
their austerity and went to the world of Brahma. And the two lords of the
daityas, having gained all their boons and being beyond the reach of all the
world, went home.

"When all their friends saw that the two great asuras had won their boon and had
their wish, they were overjoyed. Then the two cut off their matted hair and wore
crowns. They put on costly ornaments and clean clothes. They held a festival of
moonlight out of season, with every pleasure, those two lords of the daityas,
overjoyed, and their friends too. 'Eat! Enjoy! Make merry! Sing! Drink! Give!'
was heard in every house. The whole city of the daityas was delighted and happy,
ringing everywhere with great drinking bouts and loud clapping. As the daityas,
who could take any form they wished, amused themselves with all kinds of
pleasures, years passed like a single day.

---

"As soon as the festival was over, the two, who longed for the three worlds,
took counsel and gave orders to their army. With the leave of their friends, of
the elders of the daityas and of their ministers, they performed the rite for
setting out, and they left at night, under the constellation Magha. They set out
with a great army of daityas, who shared their way of life, carrying maces and
spears, lances and hammers. Praised by bards with blessings and hymns that
promised victory, they went on with the greatest joy.

"The two daityas, who could go wherever they wished, flew up into the sky, mad
for war, and went straight to the home of the gods. When the gods knew they were
coming, and knew of the lord's boon, they left heaven and went to the world of
Brahma. The two, of fierce courage, conquered the world of Indra, and the hosts
of yakshas and rakshasas, and the creatures that move in the sky. The great
asuras conquered the snakes that live under the earth, and all the barbarian
peoples who live by the sea.

"Then the two, fierce in their rule, set about conquering the whole earth. They
called their soldiers together and spoke harsh words to them. 'The royal seers
with their great sacrifices, and the brahmins with their offerings to the gods
and the ancestors, make the power, strength and glory of the gods grow. All of
them, grown great in this way, are enemies of the asuras. We must all join
together and kill them, with all our might.'

"When they had given all their soldiers this order, on the eastern shore of the
great ocean, the two set their minds on cruelty and went out in every direction.
Whenever the mighty pair saw people who offered sacrifices, or brahmins who
performed sacrifices for others, they killed them all by force. In the
hermitages their soldiers boldly seized the fire offerings of the seers, who had
made their souls pure, and threw them into the water. The curses that the great
ascetics spoke in their anger did not touch the two, who were swollen with their
boon. When the curses did not touch them, like arrows shot at stone, the
brahmins gave up their vows and fled. The men on earth who had won perfection by
austerity, self-controlled and devoted to peace, fled in fear of the two, like
snakes from Garuda. With the hermitages churned up and broken, and the water
pots and ladles scattered, the whole world was empty, as if Time had struck it
down.

"When the royal seers and seers could not be seen, the two great asuras, set on
killing, made up their minds and changed their shapes. They became elephants in
rut, their temples burst open, and they sent to Yama's house even those who hid
in strongholds. They became lions, and then tigers, and then they vanished from
sight. By all these means the cruel pair found the seers and killed them.

"Sacrifice and Veda study stopped on earth. Kings and brahmins were destroyed.
Festivals and sacrifices ceased. The earth was full of cries, stricken with
fear. Trade and markets stopped. The rites of the gods stopped, and holy
marriages were no more. Farming and herding stopped. Cities and hermitages were
laid waste. The earth was strewn with bones and skeletons, and terrible to see.
The rites for the ancestors stopped. There was no call to the gods and no
blessing. The world looked frightful, and hard to look at. The moon and the sun,
the planets, the stars, the constellations and the gods of heaven all fell into
despair when they saw what Sunda and Upasunda had done.

"So by their cruel deeds the two daityas conquered every quarter, and, with no
rival left, they made their home in Kurukshetra."
