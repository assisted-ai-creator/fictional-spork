# Ghatotkacha

When they woke and saw Hidimba's more than human beauty, the tigers among men
were amazed, and so was Pritha. Kunti looked at her, marvelling at her beauty,
and spoke to her gently, sweetly and kindly. "Whose are you, you who shine like
a child of the gods, and who are you, lovely one? Why have you come, and from
where? If you are the goddess of this forest, or an apsaras, tell me all of it,
and why you are standing here."

"This great forest you see, dark as a blue cloud," said Hidimba, "is the home of
the rakshasa Hidimba, and my home. Know that I am the sister of that lord of the
rakshasas, noble lady. My brother sent me here, for he wanted to kill you and
your sons. I came here at the word of that cruel-minded one, and I saw your
mighty son, who shines like gold. Then love, which moves within all beings,
drove me, good lady, and I came under the power of your son. So I chose your
mighty son for my husband. I tried to carry him off, but I could not. When he
saw that I was taking a long time, the man-eater came himself to kill all your
sons. And your son, my beloved, the wise, great-hearted one, crushed him by
force and dragged him away from here. Look at them, man and rakshasa, dragging
each other, roaring at each other, showing their courage in battle."

As soon as they heard her words, Yudhishthira leaped up, and Arjuna, Nakula and
mighty Sahadeva. They saw the two locked together, dragging each other, each
longing for victory, like two lions mad for battle. Hugging each other and
dragging each other, they raised a cloud of dust from the earth like the smoke
of a forest fire. Covered in the dust of the earth, like mountains, they shone
like two peaks wrapped in mist.

When Partha saw Bhima being hard pressed by the rakshasa, he said to him
quietly, as if smiling, "Bhima, do not be afraid, strong-armed one. We did not
know you were fighting this terrible creature. We were asleep, worn out. I am
here to help you, Partha. I will fight the rakshasa. Nakula and Sahadeva will
guard our mother."

"Stand aside and watch," said Bhima. "There is no need for you to rush in. Now
that he has come between my arms, he will not live."

"Why let this wicked rakshasa live so long, Bhima?" said Arjuna. "We must go. We
cannot stay here long, tamer of enemies. Kill him before the east grows red,
before the twilight comes. In that cruel hour the rakshasas grow strong. Hurry,
Bhima. Stop playing. Kill this terrible rakshasa before he works his magic. Put
all the strength of your arms into it."

At Arjuna's words, Bhima lifted up the body of the terrible rakshasa and quickly
whirled it round a hundred times. "You were fattened for nothing on flesh got
for nothing," said Bhima. "You grew for nothing, and your mind was for nothing.
You deserve a death for nothing, and today you will be nothing."

"Or, if you find this rakshasa a burden in battle," said Arjuna, "I will help
you. Let him be killed quickly. Or let me kill him myself, Wolf-belly. You have
done your work and you are tired. Rest a while."

When Bhimasena heard this, he flew into a rage. He crushed the rakshasa to the
ground by force and killed him as one kills a beast for sacrifice. As Bhima
killed him, he roared a huge roar that filled the whole forest, like a drum wet
with water. The mighty son of Pandu locked him in his arms, broke him in the
middle, and gladdened the Pandavas.

When they saw Hidimba dead, the fierce brothers were delighted, and they
honoured Bhimasena, the tiger among men, the tamer of enemies. When they had
honoured great Bhima of the terrible courage, Arjuna spoke to Wolf-belly again.
"I think there is a town not far from this forest, lord. Let us go quickly,
bless you, so that Suyodhana does not find us."

"So be it," they all said. And the tigers among men, the tamers of enemies, set
off with their mother, and Hidimba the rakshasi went too.

---

"Rakshasas remember a quarrel," said Bhima, "and use their bewildering magic.
Hidimba, go the way your brother went."

"Even in anger, tiger among men," said Yudhishthira, "do not kill a woman,
Bhima. Guard dharma, Pandava, above guarding your life. You killed the mighty
one who came meaning to kill. What can his sister do to us, even if she is
angry?"

Then Hidimba bowed to Kunti with joined hands, and said to her and to
Yudhishthira, Kunti's son, "Noble lady, you know the pain that love brings to
women in this world. That pain has come to me, good lady, because of Bhimasena.
I have borne that terrible pain, waiting for my time. Now my time has come, and
it will bring me happiness. I have left my friends, my own dharma and my own
people, and chosen your son, this tiger among men, as my husband, good lady. Yet
when I spoke as I did, I was refused by this fine man, and by you too, famous
lady. Think me a fool, or think me devoted and faithful, great lady, but join me
with this husband, your son. Let me take him, who looks like a god, and go where
I will. And I will come back. Trust me, good lady. When you think of me, I will
come and carry you all wherever you want. I will bring you, bulls among men,
through dangers and hard places. I will carry you on my back when you want to go
fast. Be kind to me, and let Bhimasena love me.

"To come through a danger, a man should keep himself alive by every means.
Whoever follows dharma must do all he can to that end. He who holds to dharma in
times of danger is the best of those who know dharma. For those who keep dharma,
a danger is a trial of their dharma. Merit keeps a man alive. Merit, they say,
is the giver of life. There is no blame in the means by which a man practises
dharma."

"It is as you say, Hidimba," said Yudhishthira. "There is no doubt of it. But
you must keep to dharma as I tell you, slender-waisted one. Every day, good
lady, when he has bathed, said his prayers and been blessed, you may have
Bhimasena, until the sun sets. Enjoy yourself with him by day as you like, swift
as thought. But you must always bring Bhimasena back at night."

"So be it," promised Hidimba the rakshasi. And she took Bhimasena and rose into
the sky.

On lovely mountain peaks and in the shrines of the gods, ringing with the calls
of beasts and birds, always delightful, she took on the most beautiful form,
decked with every ornament. Talking sweetly, she gave the Pandava pleasure. So
too in forest thickets, on slopes of flowering trees, by lovely lakes with
lotuses and water lilies, on river islands and beaches of beryl sand, by forest
pools with fine bathing places and by mountain streams, on the shores of the
ocean strewn with gems and gold, in lovely towns and great forests of sal, in
the holy groves of the gods and on the mountain slopes, in the homes of the
guhyakas and the retreats of ascetics, by the Manasa lakes, with the fruits and
flowers of every season, she wore her most beautiful form and gave the Pandava
pleasure.

Giving Bhima pleasure here and there, swift as thought, the rakshasi bore a
mighty son to Bhimasena. He had strange eyes, a huge mouth and ears like
arrowheads. He was terrible to look at, with coppery lips and sharp fangs, very
strong. He was a great archer, very brave, of great spirit and great arms, very
swift, huge in body, and a master of great magic, a tamer of enemies. He was not
human, though born of a human, of terrible speed and great strength. He
surpassed the pishachas and all other beings, and men too. Though a child, he
reached his youth among men at once, lord of the people. The mighty hero became
supreme in all weapons. For rakshasa women conceive and give birth on the same
day, and they take any shape they like, and many shapes.

The great archer, bald, bowed and clasped his father's feet and his mother's,
and they named him. He said to his mother, "My pot is bare," and so his name
became Ghatotkacha, Pot-bare. And Ghatotkacha was devoted to the Pandavas, and
they always loved him, and he became one of them.

"Our time together is over," Hidimba said to Bhima. And, having made an
agreement, she went her own way. "When there is work to be done, I will come to
my fathers," said Ghatotkacha, the best of rakshasas. Then he took his leave and
set out for the north. He had been created by great Indra for the sake of the
spear, to destroy Karna, whose courage was matchless.

---

From forest to forest the heroes went, killing many herds of deer, hurrying
away, king, the great warriors. Passing through the lands of the Matsyas, the
Trigartas, the Panchalas and the Kichakas, looking at lovely stretches of forest
and lakes, they all wore their hair matted and dressed in bark and deerskin.
With Kunti, the great men took on the look of ascetics. Sometimes they carried
their mother, hurrying, the great warriors, and sometimes they walked at their
ease, and then pressed on again. Reciting the Veda of Brahman, all the limbs of
the Veda and the books of statecraft, those men who knew dharma saw their
grandfather.

They bowed to great Krishna Dvaipayana, and stood before him with joined hands,
the tamers of enemies, with their mother.

"I knew of this beforehand in my mind, bulls of the Bharatas," said Vyasa, "how
you were driven out, wrongly, by Dhritarashtra's sons. Knowing it, I have come
to do you the greatest good. Do not despair at this. All of it is for your
happiness. They and you are all the same to me, without doubt. But kinsmen show
more love to those who are in need and to those who are young. So my love for
you is greater now. Out of love I want to do you good. Listen. Here, near by, is
a lovely, healthy town. Live here in hiding, and wait for me to come."

When he had comforted the Parthas, the tamers of enemies, like this, Vyasa went
with them to Ekachakra, and the lord comforted Kunti. "Mother of living sons,
this son of yours, Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, the king of dharma, will
rule all the kings of the earth. He knows dharma and is master of himself. He
will win the whole earth by dharma, and enjoy it through the strength of
Bhimasena and Arjuna, without doubt. Your sons and Madri's, all great warriors,
will enjoy themselves in their own kingdom, happy at heart. When they have
conquered this earth, the tigers among men will offer the royal consecration,
the horse sacrifice and other rites, with rich gifts. Favouring their friends
with wealth and comfort, your sons will enjoy their fathers' and grandfathers'
kingdom here."

When he had said this and settled them in a brahmin's house, the seer Dvaipayana
said to the best of kings, "Wait for me here. I will come back. When you know
the time and place, you will find great joy."

"So be it," they all said to him, with joined hands, lord of men. And the
blessed seer Vyasa went where he wished.

**Divergences.**

* **Yudhishthira's terms, not Kunti's.** In the CE Hidimba pleads with Kunti and
  Yudhishthira, and it is Yudhishthira who sets the terms (1.143.16–18). The vulgate
  adds Kunti's speech, a set term "until a son is conceived" (App. I No. 88), and a stay
  of months at Shalihotra's hermitage (\*1565–\*1566, \*1571, \*1579). Not in the CE.
* **Why Ghatotkacha was made.** The CE says outright that Indra created him "for the
  sake of the spear, to destroy Karna" (1.143.38). Karna will spend Indra's spear on him
  in the war (Book 7).

**Choices.**

* 1.142.25: Bhima's taunt plays on *vṛthā*, "for nothing", five times. The novel keeps
  the repetition.
* 1.142.28 *paśumāram*: "as one kills a sacrificial beast".
* 1.143.8: the line is compressed. Hidimba seems to say that she has already been
  refused. The vulgate adds that she will die if refused (\*1523, \*1555); the CE does
  not.
* 1.142.22 *purā saṃrajyate prācī*: "before the east grows red"; the fight has lasted
  the night.
* 1.143.13–15: Hidimba's argument that dharma allows every means of survival in
  danger is hers.
* 1.143.24 *sagarasya pradeśeṣu*: "in the regions of Sagara", i.e. the ocean shores.
* 1.143.26 *mānaseṣu saraḥsu*: "the Manasa lakes".
* 1.143.34 *ghaṭabhāsotkaca*: "his pot (head) shone hairless". The CE's etymology of
  the name.
* 1.144.2 *kīcakān*: the Kichaka country.
* 1.144.13 *jīvaputri*: "you whose sons live", a blessing.
