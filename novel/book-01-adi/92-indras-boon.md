# Indra's Boon

"The wise man is awake before the time of trouble comes," said Jaritari, "and
when the time of trouble comes, he is never shaken. But the fool who does not
see trouble before it comes is shaken when it does, and knows nothing."

"You are steady and wise," said Sarisrikva, "and this is a danger to our lives.
Among many, there is one who is brave and wise. There is no doubt of it."

"The eldest is the protector," said Stambamitra. "The eldest saves us from
trouble. If the eldest does not know what to do, what can the younger do?"

"The fire with the golden seed is coming quickly, blazing, to destroy us," said
Drona. "The hungry fire with seven tongues is creeping up on us, licking."

When his brothers said this to him, Jaritari joined his hands and praised the
bright fire. Hear how, king.

"You are the self of the wind, the purifier," said Jaritari. "You are the body
of the plants. The waters are your source, bright one, and you are the source of
the waters. Your flames go up and down and spread out on every side, mighty one,
like the rays of the sun."

"Our mother has gone, and we do not know our father," said Sarisrikva. "Our
wings have not grown, god of the lotus banner. We have no protector but you,
Agni. So protect us, lone hero. Whatever gentle form you have, Agni, and your
seven flames, protect us with them today. We praise you, and we seek your
shelter. You alone give heat, Jatavedas. There is no other who gives heat among
the rays, god. Protect us, who are little seers. Pass us by, carrier of
offerings."

"You alone are everything, Agni," said Stambamitra. "In you is this whole world.
You sustain living things, and you bear up the world. You are Agni, the carrier
of offerings, and you are the highest offering. The wise worship you as many,
and as one. When you have made these three worlds, carrier of offerings, you
cook them again when the time comes, kindled. You are the birthplace of this
whole world, Agni, and again its resting place. You, lord of the world, living
within, cook the food that creatures eat. Growing for ever, you cook it, and all
things rest in you."

"Becoming the sun, Jatavedas," said Drona, "you take up with your rays the water
of the earth and all the juices born of the earth, and when the time comes to
release them, you give them back as rain and make things grow here, bright one.
From you, bright one, come these plants with their green leaves, and the lotus
pools, and the ocean, the great sea. This body of ours, sharp-rayed one, belongs
to Varuna. Be our gentle protector. Do not destroy us today. Tawny-eyed,
red-necked, black-tracked eater of offerings, pass us by. Spare us, as you spare
the houses of the ocean."

When Drona, whose deeds were unwearied, said this, Jatavedas, glad at heart,
spoke to Drona, mindful of his promise to Mandapala.

"You are the seer Drona. What you have spoken is Brahman. I will do what you
wish, and you have nothing to fear. For Mandapala told me of you before: 'As you
burn the forest, spare my sons.' His words and what you have said here both
weigh with me. Tell me, what shall I do for you? I am very pleased with your
hymn, brahmin. Bless you, lord."

"These cats are always frightening us, bright one," said Drona. "Put them
between your fangs, carrier of offerings, with all their kin."

The fire did so. Then, taking leave of the Sharngakas, he blazed up and burned
the Khandava, Janamejaya.

---

Mandapala too, joy of the Kurus, kept thinking of his sons. Though he had spoken
to the fire, he could not stop worrying. Tormented about his sons, he said to
Lapita, "How can my little sons, Lapita, who cannot fly, escape, when the fire
is growing and the wind is blowing hard? They will not be able to get away. How
will their mother, poor thing, bear her misery, unable to save them, seeing no
way to rescue her sons? How will she bear it, running about, crying out,
tormented, over my sons, who can neither run nor fly? How is my son Jaritari?
How is Sarisrikva? How are Stambamitra and Drona? And how is she, poor thing?"

As the seer Mandapala lamented like this in the forest, Lapita answered him, as
if in jealousy, Bharata.

"You have no real care for your sons. You told me yourself those seers were full
of power and strength. They have nothing to fear from the fire. And you
entrusted them to the fire in front of me, and the great fire promised, 'So be
it.' A guardian of the world never speaks a false word. And they are able to
speak for themselves. Your heart is not with them at all. It is my enemy you are
thinking of, and pining for. Surely you have no love for me, as you had for her
before. It is not right for one who has wings, and could help, to look on
without love while a friend is in torment. Go to Jarita, since it is for her you
are pining. I will go about alone, like a woman with a worthless man."

"I do not go about in the world as you think," said Mandapala. "I go about for
the sake of offspring, and that offspring is in danger. The fool who lets go of
what he has for the sake of what may come is despised by the world. Do as you
wish. This blazing fire, licking at the trees, raises a hateful, evil anguish in
my heart."

When the fire had passed from that place, Jarita, longing for her sons, hurried
back to them. She found them all safe, freed from Jatavedas, crying piteously in
the forest. Again and again she could hardly believe the sight of them, and she
went to each of her sons in turn, weeping.

Then Mandapala too arrived suddenly, Bharata. But none of his sons welcomed him.
Though he spoke to each of them again and again, and to Jarita, they said
nothing to the seer, good or bad.

"Which is your eldest son?" said Mandapala. "Which is the next, which the middle
one, and which the youngest? Why do you not answer me, when I speak to you in my
distress? I did entrust them to the eater of offerings. And still I find no
peace."

"What do you want with the eldest son?" said Jarita. "Or with the next? What do
you want with the middle one, or with the youngest, poor thing? You left me with
nothing at all and went away long ago. Go to that Lapita, young and sweetly
smiling."

"For women," said Mandapala, "nothing is so bad, except another man, as a rival
wife. That is how it must be in the world. Even the lovely Arundhati, true to
her vows and famous in all the worlds, suspected Vasishtha, the best of seers,
whose heart was wholly pure, who always cared for her good, the hero among the
seven seers. And she scorned that sage. For that ill thought she has become the
colour of smoke and dusk, now seen and now not seen, not beautiful, and she is
looked on as an ill omen. So you too, when I have come here for the sake of our
children, turn from the one you love and behave as she did. A man should never
trust that a woman is his wife. For a wife who has a son does not think of her
duty."

Then all his sons attended him properly, and he began to comfort them, king.

"I asked the fire to protect you," said Mandapala, "and Agni had already
promised, 'So be it.' I knew Agni's word, and your mother's knowledge of dharma,
and your own great power, and so I did not come here before. You need not grieve
over death, my sons. The eater of offerings knows that you are seers. And
Brahman is known to you."

When he had comforted his sons in this way, Bharata, Mandapala took his wife and
went from that place to another land.

---

The blessed sharp-rayed fire burned the kindled Khandava forest with the two
Krishnas, making the world safe. When he had drunk the streams of fat and marrow
there, the fire reached the greatest contentment, and showed himself to Arjuna.

Then the blessed lord of the gods came down from the sky, surrounded by the
Maruts, and said to Partha and Madhava, "You two have done a deed that even the
immortals would find hard. Choose boons. I am pleased with you, even boons that
are rare and more than human."

Partha asked Shakra for all his weapons, and Shakra set a time for him to
receive them. "When the blessed Mahadeva is pleased with you, Pandava," he said,
"then I will give you all my weapons. I myself will know when that time comes,
joy of the Kurus. And for your great austerity I will give them to you. All the
fire weapons and all the wind weapons, and all of mine, you will receive,
Dhananjaya."

And Vasudeva asked for eternal friendship with Partha, and the king of the gods,
pleased, gave him that boon.

When he had given them their boons, the lord of the Maruts, well pleased, took
leave of the fire and went back to heaven with the gods.

And Agni, having burned that forest, with its beasts and birds, for six days,
was well satisfied, and stopped. When he had eaten the flesh and drunk the fat
and blood, he was full of the greatest joy, and he spoke to the two of them,
lord of the people.

"You two, the best of men, have satisfied me as I wished. I give you leave,
heroes. Go wherever you wish."

When the great Agni had given them leave, Arjuna, Vasudeva and the danava Maya,
all three, bull of the Bharatas, walked about, and then sat down together on a
lovely river bank.

**The CE's own doublets.**

* **Agni sated twice.** Agni is "glad and full" when the gods withdraw (1.219.34), and again at
  the end of the burning (1.225.6, 15). Both are kept.

**Divergences.**

* **Pashupata.** The vulgate has Arjuna ask Indra for the Pashupata weapon itself (\*2159). In
  the CE he asks for "all the weapons", and Indra puts it off until Shiva is pleased with him
  (1.225.9–12). Shiva gives him the Pashupata weapon in Book 3 (3.41.13).
* The vulgate has Agni formally hand Gandiva, the quivers and the chariot over to Arjuna for
  good (\*2160), and has Maya speak to Arjuna here (\*2161). In the CE Maya's offer opens Book 2 (2.1.1–5).
* The vulgate adds Janamejaya asking again why Agni burned the forest. The answer tells of an
  Ikshvaku king, here named Shvetaketu, whose thousand-year sacrifice sated Agni (App. I No. 121).
  It is another version of the Shvetaki story (App. I No. 118; ledger L-107).
