# The Story in Brief

"When the wise seer Krishna Dvaipayana heard that Janamejaya had been
consecrated for the snake sacrifice," said the bard, "he came there.

"Kali bore him to Parashara, the son of Shakti, on an island in the Yamuna. She
was still a maiden then. He was the grandfather of the Pandavas. As soon as he
was born, he made his body grow by his own will, and that famous man learned the
Vedas with their limbs, and the histories. No one could surpass him, not by
great austerity, nor by study of the Veda, nor by vows, nor by fasting, nor by
birth, nor by anger. The best of those who know the Veda divided the one Veda
into four. He knew what was higher and what was lower, a brahmin seer, a poet,
true to his vows and pure. He fathered Pandu, Dhritarashtra and Vidura, carrying
on the line of Shantanu, a man of holy and great fame.

"With his pupils, who had mastered the Vedas and their limbs, he entered the
assembly at the sacrifice of the royal seer Janamejaya. There he saw King
Janamejaya seated, like Indra among the gods. Many members of the assembly
surrounded him, and consecrated lords of many countries, and priests like gods,
skilled in laying out the sacrifice.

"When the royal seer Janamejaya saw the seer arrive, that best of the Bharatas
went quickly to meet him with his people, gladly. With the assembly's consent,
the lord had a golden seat set for him, as Shakra does for Brihaspati. When he
was seated there, the giver of boons, honoured by the hosts of divine seers, the
king of kings honoured him with the rites the scriptures lay down. According to
the rules, he offered water for his feet, water to rinse his mouth, the guest
offering and a cow to his grandfather Krishna, who deserved them all.

"Vyasa accepted that honour from the Pandava Janamejaya, and told them to let
the cow go, and he was pleased. When the king had honoured his great-grandfather
with such care, he sat down near him, glad at heart, and asked after his health.
And the blessed one looked at him and told him that he was well. Honoured by all
the members of the assembly, he honoured them in turn.

"Then, when all the assembly had honoured him, Janamejaya joined his palms and
asked that best of brahmins, 'You saw the Kurus and the Pandavas with your own
eyes. I want to hear you tell their story, brahmin. How did the quarrel come
between them, whose deeds were never mean? All my grandfathers' minds were
possessed by fate. How did that great war come about among them, which made an
end of so many creatures? Tell me this fully, blessed one, for you know it all.'

"When he heard these words, Krishna Dvaipayana gave an order to his pupil
Vaishampayana, who was sitting near him. 'Tell him all of it, as you heard it
from me: how the quarrel of the Kurus and the Pandavas happened long ago.'

"The bull among brahmins took his teacher's word, and then he told the whole
ancient history to the king, and to the members of the assembly, and to all the
kshatriyas: the quarrel of the Kurus and the Pandavas, and the ruin of the
kingdom.

---

"First I bow to my teacher," said Vaishampayana, "with mind and understanding
and full attention. I honour all the brahmins, and the other learned men. Now I
will tell the whole of the thought of Vyasa, this wise great seer, famous in all
the worlds, whose splendour has no measure. You are a worthy listener for this
story of the Bharatas, king, and now that I have you, the stirring of my
teacher's words seems to urge me on with joy.

"Hear, king, how the quarrel came between the Kurus and the Pandavas, how it
grew out of the dice for the sake of the kingdom, and how the exile in the
forest followed. And I will tell you, bull of the Bharatas, since you ask, how
the war came about that destroyed the earth.

"When their father died, those heroes came from the forest to their own home.
Soon they were learned in the Veda and in the bow. The Pandavas were handsome,
brave and strong, honoured by the people of the city, and full of fortune and
fame. When the Kurus saw them like that, they could not bear it. Then cruel
Duryodhana, and Karna, with the son of Subala, tried all kinds of ways to hold
them down and drive them out.

"The wicked son of Dhritarashtra gave Bhima poison. Heroic Wolf-belly digested
it along with his food. Then he tied up Wolf-belly while he was asleep at
Pramanakoti, and threw Bhima into the waters of the Ganga, and went back to the
city. When the son of Kunti woke, great king, he broke his bonds, and Bhimasena
rose up, unharmed. And as he slept, he had him bitten on every part of his body
by black cobras, full of poison. But the slayer of enemies did not die.

"Through all those wrongs, the wise Vidura was watchful, to save them and to
foil the attempts. As Shakra in heaven brings happiness to the world of the
living, so Vidura always brought happiness to the Pandavas.

"Duryodhana tried all kinds of means, secret and open, and he could not kill
them, for fate and what was to be protected them. Then he took counsel with his
advisers, with Vrisha, Duhshasana and the rest. With Dhritarashtra's leave, he
ordered a house of lac to be built. He had the Pandavas, whose strength had no
measure, live there. And when they trusted him, he had it set on fire. But on
Vidura's word a tunnel had been dug, and by that means he set them free, and
when they were free, they ran in fear.

"Then, in a great and terrible forest, Bhimasena of the terrible courage killed
a rakshasa named Hidimba in his anger. Then the heroes joined together and went
to Ekachakra with their mother, the scorchers of enemies, in the guise of
brahmins. There they killed Baka, who was very strong, for the sake of a
brahmin, and then they went with brahmins to the city of the Panchalas. There
they won Draupadi. They lived there a full year, and when they became known, the
tamers of enemies came back to Hastinapura.

"King Dhritarashtra and the son of Shantanu said to them, 'How can we keep you
from quarrelling with your brothers, my sons? We have thought that you should
live in Khandavaprastha. So go and live in Khandavaprastha, which has its own
country and its broad roads well laid out, and let your anger go.'

"At their word, they went with all their friends to the city of Khandavaprastha,
taking every kind of treasure with them. They lived there, king, for many years,
and by the might of their weapons they brought other kings under their power.
So, with dharma first in their minds, devoted to their vows of truth, watchful,
active and patient, they scorched their enemies.

"Mighty Bhimasena conquered the east, heroic Arjuna the north, Nakula the west,
and Sahadeva, killer of enemy heroes, the south. So they brought the whole earth
under their power. With the five Pandavas, true in their valour, bright as suns,
and with the sun shining too, the earth seemed to shine with six suns.

"Then, for some reason, Yudhishthira, the king of dharma, sent his brother
Dhananjaya to the forest. He lived in the forest for a full year and a month.
Then one day he went to Hrishikesha in Dvaravati. There Bibhatsu won a wife, the
lotus-eyed Subhadra, sweet of speech, the younger sister of Vasudeva. As Shachi
was joined with great Indra, and Shri with Krishna, so Subhadra, glad at heart,
was joined with Arjuna the Pandava.

"And the son of Kunti, Bibhatsu, with Vasudeva, gave the fire its fill in the
Khandava forest, best of kings. With Keshava beside him it was no great burden
for Partha, just as it is none for Vishnu, with resolve as his helper, when he
kills his enemies. And Agni gave Partha the Gandiva, the finest of bows, and two
quivers of arrows that never run out, and a chariot with the ape on its banner.
There Bibhatsu set free the great asura Maya, and Maya built a heavenly hall,
covered with every kind of jewel.

"Foolish Duryodhana, in his wicked mind, grew greedy for that hall. Then, with
the son of Subala, he cheated Yudhishthira at dice, and sent him to the forest
for seven years and five, and the thirteenth year to be spent unknown in a
kingdom. Then, in the fourteenth year, they asked for their own wealth, and they
did not get it, great king. Then the war began. They destroyed everything, and
killed King Duryodhana, and took back the kingdom, most of it laid waste.

"This, then, is what happened long ago to those whose deeds were never mean: the
quarrel, the ruin of the kingdom, and the victory, best of victors."

---

"Best of brahmins," said Janamejaya, "you have told me all of it in brief, the
story of the Mahabharata, the great life of the Kurus. But as you tell this
story, sinless one, so full of wonders, a great longing has come over me to hear
it in full. So you should tell it again, in full. I never tire of hearing the
great life of those who came before me.

"The Pandavas knew dharma. They killed all those who should not have been
killed, and still men praise them. It can be no small reason that made them do
it. Why did those tigers among men, strong as they were, and without fault, bear
the torments the wicked put on them? How did Wolf-belly, with the strength of
ten thousand elephants, and arms like weapons, hold back his anger when he was
tormented, best of brahmins? How did Draupadi, dark Krishna, tormented by the
wicked, not burn up the sons of Dhritarashtra with her terrible eyes, when she
had the power? How did the two sons of Pritha, and the two sons of Madri, follow
that tiger among men when he went wrong at the dice, cheated by the wicked? How
did Yudhishthira, the best of those who uphold dharma, the son of Dharma, who
knew dharma, bear such great suffering, when he did not deserve it? And how did
the Pandava Dhananjaya, with Krishna as his charioteer, alone, shooting his
arrows, send so many armies to the world of the dead? Tell me all of this, as it
happened, rich in austerity: all that those great warriors did, at each place
and time."

"I will tell you," said Vaishampayana, "the whole of the thought of the great
seer Vyasa, great-souled, honoured in all the worlds, whose splendour has no
measure. The son of Satyavati, whose power has no measure, has set out here this
work of a hundred thousand verses of holy deeds. The wise man who recites it,
and the man who hears it, reach the world of Brahma and become equal to the
gods. For this is the equal of the Vedas, and holy, and the highest. It is the
best of all things to be heard, an ancient work praised by the seers. In this
history of great holiness, the business of life and dharma are taught in full,
and the understanding that leads to the final goal.

"The wise man who recites this Veda of Krishna to people who are not mean, who
are generous, true and believing, wins his reward. A man who hears this history,
even a very cruel man, will surely shed the sin even of killing an unborn child.
This history is called *Jaya*, Victory. Whoever wants to win should hear it. He
will win the whole earth and defeat his enemies. It is the best rite for the
birth of a son, a great blessing. The queen and the crown prince should hear it
often.

"Vyasa, whose understanding has no measure, has told it as a holy book of the
business of life, a highest book of dharma, and a book of release. People tell
it now, and others will tell it in time to come. Those who hear it have sons who
serve them, and servants who please them. A man who always hears this is quickly
freed of every sin he has done, by body, by speech and by mind. Those who hear
the great birth of the Bharatas without envy have no fear of sickness. How much
less do they fear the next world?

"Krishna Dvaipayana made this, wishing to do holy good. It brings wealth, fame,
long life and heaven, and it is holy. It spreads through the world the glory of
the great-souled Pandavas, and of the other kshatriyas, rich in wealth and
splendour. As the blessed ocean and Himalaya, the mountain, are both famous as
stores of jewels, so is the Bharata said to be.

"The wise man who recites it to brahmins at the holy days is cleansed of sin. He
wins heaven, and he becomes one with Brahman. And whoever recites even a quarter
of a verse of it to brahmins at a rite for the dead, that rite reaches his
forefathers and never runs out. Whatever wrong a man does unknowing by day, as
he goes about, is dissolved as soon as he hears the story of the Mahabharata.

"The great birth of the Bharatas is called the Mahabharata. Whoever knows the
meaning of its name is freed from all sins. The sage Krishna Dvaipayana, rising
early every day, made this finest story, the Mahabharata, in three years.

"On dharma and on wealth, on love and on release, bull of the Bharatas, what is
here is found elsewhere. What is not here is found nowhere."

**Choices.**

* 1.54.2 *kālī*: Satyavati is called Kali, "the dark one", here. The next
  chapter tells her birth.
* 1.54.3 *iṣṭyā deham avīvṛdhat*: "by his will he made his body grow".
* 1.55.10 *pramāṇakoṭyām*: Pramanakoti, a place on the Ganga. The text only names
  it.
* 1.55.15: the subject is not named in the verse. The context makes it
  Duryodhana, and the novel names him for clarity.
* 1.55.16 *vṛṣa*: Vrisha, a name of Karna.
* 1.55.18 *khanitrī*: literally "a digger". Here a tunnel dug on Vidura's advice.
* 1.55.36 *vyavasāyasahāyasya viṣṇoḥ*: "for Vishnu, whose helper is resolve".
* 1.55.37 *kapilakṣaṇam*: "marked with the ape", i.e. the ape banner.
* 1.55.42 *vidrutabhūyiṣṭham*: the kingdom "mostly laid waste" (or "mostly
  fled").
* 1.56.6 *nāgāyutaprāṇaḥ*: "with the strength of ten thousand elephants".
  *bāhuśālī*: "strong in the arms".
* 1.56.13 *śatasahasram*: "a hundred thousand" verses. This is the text's own
  claim about the whole work.
* 1.56.17 *kārṣṇaṃ vedam*: "the Veda of Krishna", meaning Krishna Dvaipayana.
* 1.56.19 *jayo nāmetihāsaḥ*: "this history is called Jaya". Compare the epigraph
  of chapter 1.
* 1.56.33: the famous closing verse, given whole.
