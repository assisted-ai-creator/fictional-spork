# The Seer on the Stake

"What had Dharma done, that he was cursed?" asked Janamejaya. "And by whose
curse, brahmin seer, was he born from a shudra woman?"

There was a brahmin called Mandavya, a famous man. He was steadfast and knew
every dharma, and he held to truth and austerity. At the gate of his hermitage,
at the foot of a tree, the great ascetic stood with his arms raised, a great
yogi keeping a vow of silence.

He had stood a long time in his austerity when some robbers came to the
hermitage carrying stolen goods, bull of the Bharatas. Many guards were on their
trail. The robbers hid the loot in his dwelling, best of the Kurus. Then, when
the troop came after them, they hid there too in their fear.

They had just hidden themselves when the troop of guards arrived in haste. The
men chasing the thieves saw the seer. The ascetic was standing there as he
always stood, and they asked him, "Which way did the robbers go, best of
brahmins? Tell us, brahmin, so that we can follow them as fast as we can."

But though the guards said this to him, the ascetic said nothing, king, good or
bad.

Then the king's men searched the hermitage, and they found the thieves hiding
there, and the goods as well. So the guards grew suspicious of the sage. They
bound him and brought him before the king with the robbers. The king ordered him
to be put to death along with the thieves. The executioners did not know who he
was, and they impaled the great ascetic on a stake. When they had set the sage
on the stake, the guards took the goods and went back to the king.

The righteous brahmin seer stayed on the stake for a long time. He ate nothing,
but he did not die. He kept himself alive, and he called the seers to him. The
great man went on with his austerity on the point of the stake, and the sages
were deeply troubled, tamer of enemies. They took the form of birds and came to
him at night from every side. They showed themselves as well as they could, and
asked the best of brahmins, "We want to hear, brahmin, what sin you have done."

The tiger among sages said to the ascetics, "Whom shall I blame? No one else has
wronged me."

When the king heard about the seer, he came out with his ministers and tried to
win over the best of seers, where he sat on the stake. "Whatever wrong I did
you, best of seers, I did in folly and ignorance. I beg your forgiveness for it.
You should not be angry with me."

When the king said this, the sage forgave him. And once he was forgiven, the
king had him taken down. When he had taken him down from the point of the stake,
he tried to pull the stake out of him. He could not pull it out, so he cut it
off at the base. So the sage went about with the stake still inside him. By that
austerity he won worlds that others can hardly win. And from then on he was
known in the worlds as Animandavya, Mandavya of the Point.

The brahmin, who knew the highest truth, went to the house of Dharma. There he
saw Dharma seated on his throne, and the lord reproached him.

"What wrong did I do without knowing it," he said, "that I should reap a fruit
like this? Tell me the truth at once. See the power of my austerity."

"You pushed a reed into the tails of little flying insects," said Dharma. "This
is the fruit of that deed, rich in austerity."

"For a small offence you have given me a huge punishment," said Animandavya.
"So, Dharma, you will be born as a man, from a shudra woman. Today I lay down a
limit in the world for the fruit of dharma. Up to the fourteenth year, nothing
done will be a sin. But for those who do such things after that, it will be a
fault."

So by the curse of that great man, for that offence, Dharma was born from a
shudra woman in the form of Vidura. He was skilled in dharma and in wealth, and
free of greed and anger. He saw far ahead, he was devoted to calm, and he
delighted in the good of the Kurus.

---

When those three princes were born, the three grew and flourished: Kurujangala,
the Kurus and Kurukshetra.

The earth gave tall crops, and the crops bore fruit. The rain god rained in
season, and the trees were full of flowers and fruit. The beasts of burden were
well and happy, and the beasts and birds rejoiced. The garlands were fragrant
and the fruits were full of juice. The cities were crowded with merchants and
craftsmen. Brave men, learned men and good men were happy. There were no
robbers, and no one delighted in wrongdoing. Even in the far corners of the
kingdom the golden age had come.

The people were given to gifts, to rites and to dharma, and they were devoted to
sacrifices and vows. They loved one another, and they prospered. They had no
pride or anger, and they were free of greed. They helped each other to prosper,
and dharma was the highest thing.

The city was full, like the great ocean, and it shone. It had gates, arches and
turrets like banks of clouds, and it was crowded with a hundred palaces, like
the city of great Indra. The people amused themselves happily by rivers and in
stretches of woodland, by pools, ponds and hillsides, and in lovely groves. The
southern Kurus roamed about with the northern Kurus, vying with them, and with
the siddhas, the seers and the charanas. No one was poor, and no woman was a
widow.

In that lovely country the Kurus made many wells, gardens, halls and pools, and
houses for brahmins. Bhishma protected it on every side according to the books
of law, king. It was lovely, marked with hundreds of shrines and sacrificial
posts. The land grew, taking in other kingdoms, and the wheel of dharma that
Bhishma set going turned in the kingdom.

As the rites of the noble princes were performed, the people of the city and the
country kept a festival without end. In the houses of the Kuru chiefs and the
townsmen, lord of men, the cry was heard everywhere: "Give! Eat!"

From their birth Bhishma looked after Dhritarashtra, Pandu and wise Vidura as if
they were his own sons. They went through the rites of passage, and they kept
their vows and studied. They grew skilled in hard work and exercise, and they
came to their youth. They mastered archery, riding, fighting with the mace and
with sword and shield, the training of elephants, and statecraft. They learned
the old stories and the Puranas, and many other kinds of learning, lord. They
knew the truth of the Vedas and their limbs, and they worked hard at everything.

Pandu was bold with the bow, and he outdid all men. King Dhritarashtra was
stronger than anyone else. And in the three worlds there was no one equal to
Vidura, king, who was always devoted to dharma and went furthest in it.

The line of Shantanu had been lost, and people saw it raised up again. Then a
saying went round the world, in every kingdom: "Of mothers of heroes, the Kashi
princesses. Of countries, Kurujangala. Of all who know dharma, Bhishma. Of
cities, the City of the Elephant."

Dhritarashtra did not come to the throne, because he was blind, and Vidura did
not, because of his mixed birth. So Pandu became king.

**Divergences and the CE's own doublets.**

* **Two versions of the sin.** At 1.57.78–80 Animandavya himself remembers that as
  a child he pierced "a little bird" with a reed. Here Dharma tells him that he put
  a reed into the tails of *pataṃgaka*s (1.101.24), and the CE does not say at what
  age. The novel keeps each version where it stands.
* **Fourteen, not twelve.** The CE's limit is "up to the fourteenth year"
  (1.101.26). The vulgate adds another line setting it at twelve (\*1101). See
  ledger L-73.
* **No "as a child" from Dharma.** The vulgate has Mandavya ask *when* he did it and
  Dharma answer "when you were a child" (\*1100). In the CE the childhood is implied
  only by his new rule.

**Choices.**

* 1.101.15 *śakunā bhūtvā*: the sages "became birds" to visit him at night.
* 1.101.16 *doṣataḥ kaṃ gamiṣyāmi*: "whom shall I go to as at fault?", that is,
  "whom shall I blame?"
* 1.101.21 *aṇīmāṇḍavya*: *aṇī* is the point of the stake left inside him. The name
  is explained, not invented.
* 1.101.24 *pataṃgaka*: small flying creatures, insects or birds. "Little flying
  insects" follows the diminutive.
* 1.102.1: the three that flourished are Kurujangala, the Kurus and Kurukshetra.
* 1.102.12 *dharmacakram*: "the wheel of dharma" Bhishma set turning.
* 1.102.22 *kāśisute*: "the two daughters of Kashi" as mothers of heroes. The popular
  version of the saying names Kausalya (\*1106).
* 1.102.23 *karaṇatvāt*: Vidura is barred "because he was a *karaṇa*", of mixed
  birth: a brahmin's son by a shudra woman (1.101.27). Rendered as "because of his
  mixed birth".
