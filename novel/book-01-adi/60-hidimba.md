# Hidimba

While they slept there, a rakshasa named Hidimba was sitting in a sal tree not
far from the forest. He was cruel, an eater of human flesh, of great courage and
great strength, ugly in shape, with yellow eyes, fanged and fearful to look at.
Hungry, and longing for meat, he saw them by chance. With his fingers raised,
scratching and shaking his rough hair, yawning with his huge mouth, he looked at
them again and again. The wicked man-eater, huge and mighty, smelled the smell
of men, and said to his sister:

"At last, after so long, food I love has come to me. My mouth is running with
juices, and my tongue is licking round it. I will sink my eight sharp fangs,
which nothing can withstand for long, into their bodies and their soft flesh. I
will spring on a man's throat and tear open the artery and drink his blood, hot,
fresh and frothing, in plenty. Go and find out who these are, sleeping here in
the forest. The strong smell of men is filling my nose. Kill all these men and
bring them to me. They are asleep in our country, and you have nothing to fear
from them. We will cook the flesh of these men as we like and eat it together.
Do as I say, quickly."

At her brother's words the rakshasi went quickly, bull of the Bharatas, to where
the Pandavas were. When she got there she saw the Pandavas asleep with Pritha,
and unconquerable Bhimasena awake. As soon as the rakshasi saw Bhimasena,
towering like a young sal tree, beautiful beyond anyone on earth, she desired
him.

*This dark, strong-armed man with shoulders like a lion's, shining, with a neck
like a conch and eyes like lotuses, would make me a fitting husband. I will
never do my brother's cruel bidding. A woman's love for her husband is far
stronger than her bond with her brother. If I killed these men, my brother and I
would be satisfied for a moment. If I do not, I will be happy for years without
end.*

She could take any shape she liked. So she took on the finest human form and
came slowly up to strong-armed Bhimasena. Like a shy creeper, decked in heavenly
ornaments, she smiled and said to Bhimasena:

"Where have you come from, bull among men, and who are you? Who are these men
sleeping here, who look like gods? And who is this tall, dark, delicate lady of
yours, sinless one, sleeping as safely in this forest as if she were in her own
house? She does not know that this deep forest is the haunt of rakshasas. A
wicked rakshasa named Hidimba lives here. My brother, the rakshasa, meaning you
harm, sent me, for he wants to eat your flesh, you who are like an immortal. But
when I saw you, shining like a child of the gods, I wanted no other husband. I
tell you the truth. Now that you know this, you who know dharma, do right by me.
My heart and body are struck with love. I love you. Love me. I will save you
from the man-eating rakshasa, strong-armed one. We will live in the mountain
strongholds. Be my husband, sinless one. I can travel through the sky and go
wherever I wish. Find joy without equal with me, here and there."

"Rakshasi," said Bhima, "who would leave his mother, his elder brother and these
younger ones here, even if he had the power? What man like me would go off, sick
with desire, and leave his sleeping brothers and his mother as food for a
rakshasa?"

"I will do whatever pleases you," said the rakshasi. "Wake them all. I will save
you all, if you like, from the man-eating rakshasa."

"I will not wake my brothers and my mother, rakshasi," said Bhima, "while they
sleep peacefully in the forest, for fear of your wicked brother. Timid one, no
rakshasas can stand up to my courage, nor men, nor gandharvas, nor yakshas,
lovely-eyed one. Go or stay, good lady, or do whatever you like. Or send your
brother the man-eater, slender one."

---

When Hidimba, lord of the rakshasas, saw that his sister had been gone a long
time, he came down from the tree and went to the Pandavas. His eyes were red,
his arms strong, his hair on end. He was mighty, his body like a mass of clouds,
his face blazing with sharp fangs.

As soon as she saw him coming, so hideous to look at, Hidimba said to Bhimasena
in fear, "Here comes the wicked man-eater, in a rage. Do what I tell you, with
your brothers. I can go where I will, hero, and I have a rakshasa's strength.
Climb up on my hips and I will carry you through the sky. Wake these sleepers
and your mother, tamer of enemies. I will take you all and go through the sky."

"Do not be afraid, you of the wide hips," said Bhima. "He is nothing while I am
here. I will kill him before your eyes, slender-waisted one. This low rakshasa
is no match for me in battle, timid one, nor are all the rakshasas together.
Look at my arms, well rounded, like elephants' trunks. Look at my thighs, like
iron bars, and my broad, solid chest. Today you will see my courage, which is
like Indra's, lovely one. Do not despise me, you of the wide hips, thinking I am
only a man."

"I do not despise you, tiger among men," said Hidimba. "You look like a god. But
I have seen what the rakshasa does to men."

As Bhimasena was talking like this, Bharata, the angry man-eating rakshasa heard
his words. And Hidimba looked at his sister's human body: her hair wreathed with
garlands, her face like the full moon, her fine brows, nose, eyes and hairline,
her delicate nails and skin, decked with every ornament, dressed in the finest
cloth. When he saw her wearing that lovely human form, the man-eater suspected
that she wanted a man, and he was furious. In his rage against his sister, best
of the Kurus, the rakshasa opened his wide eyes and said to her:

"What fool is standing in my way when I want to eat? Are you so bewildered,
Hidimba, that you are not afraid of my anger? Shame on you, you faithless,
man-hungry woman, doing me this harm, disgracing all the rakshasa lords before
us. You have taken the side of these men and done me a great wrong. I will kill
them all today, and you with them."

When the red-eyed rakshasa had said this to his sister, he sprang at her to kill
her, gnashing his teeth. As he rushed at her, Bhima, the best of fighters, saw
him. The splendid man shouted at him, "Stop! Stop!"

Bhimasena looked at the rakshasa, furious with his sister, and said, as if
laughing, "What will you gain, Hidimba, by waking these people who are sleeping
so sweetly? Come at me, fool, man-eater, as hard as you can. Strike at me. You
should not kill a woman, least of all when she has done no wrong, when it is
someone else who has wronged you. This girl is not in her own power, loving me
here. She is driven by the bodiless god of love, who moves inside the body, your
sister, fool, you disgrace to the rakshasas. She saw my looks when you sent her,
and now the timid girl loves me. She does not disgrace your family. Love is to
blame, rakshasa. While I stand here, wicked one, you should not kill a woman.
Come, man-eater, one against one. I will send you to the house of Yama today.
Today let your head be crushed by the force of my hand, rakshasa, as if trampled
by the foot of a mighty elephant. Today let the eaters of flesh, hawks and
jackals, drag your limbs gladly over the ground, when I have killed you in
battle. In a moment I will clear this forest of the thorn that has always
spoiled it, eating men. Today, wicked one, your sister will see you dragged
along the ground by me, big as a mountain, like a great elephant dragged by a
lion. When I have killed you, you disgrace to the rakshasas, the men who roam
the forest will go about this forest unharmed."

"What use is your roaring and your boasting, man?" said Hidimba. "Do it all,
then boast. Do not waste time. You think yourself strong and brave. When you
meet me today, you will know I am the stronger. I will not hurt these others
yet. Let them sleep in peace. I will kill you first, you fool with your hateful
tongue. I will drink the blood from your limbs, and after that I will kill them
too, and then this girl who has wronged me."

With that the man-eater raised his arm and ran at Bhimasena in a rage. As he
rushed in, Bhima, of terrible courage, caught the arm he struck with, laughing.
Holding him by force as he writhed, Bhima dragged him eight bow-lengths from
that place, as a lion drags a small deer. Then the rakshasa, caught by force by
the Pandava, flew into a rage. He threw his arms round Bhimasena and roared a
terrible roar. Again mighty Bhima dragged him away by force, so that the noise
would not reach his sleeping brothers. They grappled each other and dragged each
other with great force, the rakshasa and Bhimasena, and they showed the greatest
courage. They broke great trees and tore down creepers, like two sixty-year-old
elephants in rut, full of fury.

At the great noise they made, those bulls among men woke up with their mother.
And they saw Hidimba standing in front of them.

**Divergences.**

* **Her name.** The CE calls the sister Hidimba (*hiḍimbā*) here, the same name as
  her brother (*hiḍimba*). The novel keeps both, and says "the rakshasi" where it would
  be unclear. The form "Hidimbi" is the vulgate's (\*1521).
* The vulgate adds a longer description of Hidimba's ugliness (\*1516), and Bhima's
  refusal to be carried off alone (\*1525). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.139.3 *ūrdhvāṅguliḥ*: "with his fingers raised", scratching his head.
* 1.139.15: her reasoning, that love of a husband outweighs love of a brother, is her
  own.
* 1.139.26 *antarikṣacarā*: "able to move through the sky".
* 1.140.5 *āruhemāṃ mama śroṇīm*: "climb onto my hips".
* 1.141.4 *anaṅgena*: "by the bodiless one", Kama, the god of love.
* 1.141.19 *dhanūṃṣy aṣṭau*: "eight bow-lengths".
* 1.141.23 *ṣaṣṭihāyanau*: "sixty years old", elephants in their prime.
