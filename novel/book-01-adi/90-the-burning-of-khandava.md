# The Burning of Khandava

The two tigers among men, in their two chariots, one on each side of the forest,
made a great slaughter of living things in every direction. Wherever the
creatures that lived in Khandava were seen running away, the two heroes chased
after them. So fast did the chariots move that the creatures could see no gap
between them. The two chariot fighters, in their fine chariots, looked like a
single spinning wheel.

As the Khandava burned, creatures rose up in their thousands, crying out
terribly in all ten directions. Many were burned in one part of the body, others
were scorched all over. Their eyes burst, their bodies split, and they leapt
about out of their wits. Some clung to their children, others to their fathers
and mothers. They could not let them go, for love, and they died like that.
Others rose up in their thousands, their faces distorted, and whirling round
here and there they fell back into the fire. Here and there living creatures
were seen dying, writhing on the ground, their wings, eyes and feet burned. As
all the pools of water boiled, Bharata, turtles and fish were seen by the
thousand, lifeless. With their bodies ablaze, the living creatures in that
forest looked like fires with bodies, as their lives came to an end.

As they flew up, Partha cut them to pieces with his arrows, laughing, and threw
them into the blazing fire. Their whole bodies stuck with arrows, they rose up
with great cries, and fell back into the fire. The cries of the forest
creatures, struck by arrows and burning, sounded like the ocean being churned.
The great flames of the delighted fire rose into the sky, and they filled the
gods with great alarm.

Then all the great gods went for shelter to the king of the gods, thousand-eyed
Purandara. "Why are all these men being burned by the fire, lord of the
immortals?" they said. "Has the destruction of the worlds come?"

When he heard them, the slayer of Vritra looked for himself, and then the god of
the bay horses set out to save the Khandava. The wielder of the thunderbolt,
lord of the gods, spread a great mass of clouds of every kind across the sky and
rained. The thousand-eyed god rained on the fire, over the Khandava, pouring out
streams as thick as axles by the hundred thousand. But those streams never
reached it. They dried up in the sky itself, in the heat of Jatavedas, and not
one reached the fire. Then the slayer of Namuchi grew very angry with the
blazing fire, and he rained on it again, pouring out a great flood of water.
With flames tangled in the streams of rain, full of smoke and lightning, the
forest became terrible, and it rang with the roar of thunder.

---

As he rained water, the Pandava Bibhatsu held it back with a rain of arrows,
showing off his finest weapons. The Pandava covered the whole Khandava with
arrows on every side, and kept that rain off the forest. As Savyasachin shot,
the forest was so covered with arrows that not a single creature could get out.

Takshaka, the mighty king of the snakes, was not there. While the forest was
burning, he was at Kurukshetra. But his strong son Ashvasena was there, and he
struggled hard to escape from the fire. He could not get out, hard pressed by
Kaunteya's arrows. His mother, the snake woman, saved him by swallowing him. She
swallowed his head first, and then his tail, and, greedy for her son's life, the
snake woman rose up. As she went, the Pandava cut off her head with a sharp,
broad-bladed arrow. The lord of the gods saw it, and, wanting to free Ashvasena,
the wielder of the thunderbolt confused the Pandava for a moment with wind and
rain, and Ashvasena got free. When he saw that terrible trick, and knew that the
snake had deceived him, the Pandava cut the creatures in the sky in two and in
three, Bharata. Bibhatsu, in his anger, cursed the snake that moves crookedly,
and Agni and Vasudeva cursed him too. "May you never find a place to rest."

Then Jishnu, in his anger, remembering that trick, covered the sky with sharp
arrows and fought the thousand-eyed god. When the king of the gods saw Phalguna
so enraged, he let loose his own blazing weapon, which spread over the whole
sky. Then a wind with a great roar, stirring up all the oceans, rose in the sky
and made clouds full of streaming water. To defeat it, Arjuna, skilled in
countering, let loose the finest weapon, the weapon of the wind, with its
mantra. It destroyed the strength and force of Indra's thunderbolts and clouds.
The streams of water dried up and the lightning vanished. In a moment the sky
was clear of dust and darkness, with a cool, pleasant breeze, and the sun's disc
as it always is. And the eater of offerings, glad to have nothing in his way,
blazed up in many shapes, with flames beyond compare, filling the world with his
roar.

When they saw that the forest was guarded by the two Krishnas, the proud birds,
led by Suparna, flew up into the sky. The garudas, their wings, beaks and claws
like thunderbolts, swooped down from the sky, wanting to strike Krishna and the
Pandava. In the same way hosts of snakes came out near the Pandava, their mouths
blazing, spitting deadly poison. When he saw them flying in their fury, Partha
cut them down with his arrows, and they fell helpless into the blazing fire, to
lose their bodies.

Then the gods, with gandharvas, yakshas, rakshasas and snakes, rose up with a
great roar, eager for battle. With iron balls, discuses, rocks and bhushundis in
their raised hands, their strength swollen with anger, they wanted to kill
Krishna and Partha. As they shouted and let loose their showers of weapons,
Bibhatsu cut off their heads with his sharp arrows. And Krishna, of great power,
the slayer of enemies, made a great slaughter of the hosts of daityas and
danavas with his discus. Others, mighty as they were, pierced by the arrows and
driven back by the force of the discus, stopped, like the sea at the shore.

Then Shakra, the great lord of the thirty gods, in great anger, mounted his
white elephant and rushed at the two of them. He took up his thunderbolt and
swiftly let fly that weapon. "They are dead," the slayer of the asuras said to
the gods.

When they saw the great thunderbolt raised by the king of the gods, the gods all
took up their own weapons. King Yama took up his staff of death, and the lord of
wealth his club. Varuna took up his noose, and Shiva his strange discus. The two
Ashvins took up blazing herbs. Dhatri took up a bow, and Jaya a pestle. Mighty
Tvashtri in his anger took up a mountain. Amsha took up a spear, and the god
Death an axe. Aryaman went about with a terrible iron bar, and Mitra stood
holding a discus edged like a razor. Pushan and Bhaga and Savitri, in their
anger, lord of the people, rushed at Krishna and Partha with bows and swords in
their hands. The Rudras, the Vasus, the mighty Maruts, the Vishvedevas and the
Sadhyas, blazing with their own splendour, these and many other gods, with
weapons of every kind, came at those two best of men, Krishna and Partha,
wanting to kill them.

In that great battle wonderful portents were seen, like those at the end of an
age, foretelling the ruin of creatures, Bharata. But when the two unfallen ones
saw Shakra enraged, with the gods, they stood unafraid in battle, invincible,
their bows ready. As the gods came on, one by one, the two in their anger drove
them back with arrows like thunderbolts. Again and again the gods' plans were
broken, and in their fear they left the battle and took refuge with Shakra. When
the sages standing in the sky saw the gods held back by Madhava and Arjuna, they
were amazed. And Shakra too, seeing their courage again and again in battle, was
delighted, and fought them once more.

Then the chastiser of Paka let loose a great shower of rocks, wanting to test
Savyasachin's courage further. Arjuna, unable to bear it, struck down that rain
with his arrows. When Shatakratu saw it made useless, the king of the gods made
the shower heavier still. But the son of the chastiser of Paka, to his father's
delight, destroyed that shower of rocks with swift arrows. Then Shakra tore up
with both hands a great peak of Mount Mandara, with its trees, and threw it,
wanting to kill the Pandava. But Arjuna shattered the mountain peak into a
thousand pieces with swift, straight arrows, blazing at their tips. As the
mountain broke apart, it looked like the sky itself breaking up, with sun, moon
and planets. As that great rock fell headlong into the forest, it killed still
more of the creatures who lived in the Khandava.

---

Frightened by the falling mountain, the dwellers of the Khandava, the danavas,
rakshasas and snakes, hyenas, bears and forest creatures, elephants in rut,
tigers, maned lions, deer and buffaloes, birds by the hundred, and other kinds
of creatures, were thrown into panic and fled. Looking at the forest and at the
two Krishnas with their weapons raised, they seemed terrified by the terrible
noise. Janardana let loose his discus, shining with its own light, and those
lesser creatures, with the danavas and the night-walkers, were cut down by the
hundred and fell into the fire at once. There the rakshasas, torn apart by
Krishna's discus, smeared with fat and blood, looked like clouds at dusk. The
Varshneya went about like Death, killing pishachas, birds, snakes and beasts by
the thousand, Bharata. Every time Krishna, the slayer of enemies, threw that
discus, it killed many creatures and came back to his hand, again and again. As
he killed every creature like this, Bharata, the form of that soul of all beings
became very terrible.

Among all the gods and danavas gathered there, no one could defeat Krishna and
the Pandava in that battle. When the gods could not protect the forest from
their might, or put out the fire, they turned away. When Shatakratu saw the
hosts of the gods turn away, he was pleased, and he stood still, praising
Krishna and the Pandava.

When the gods had turned back, a voice without a body spoke to Shatakratu, in a
deep and solemn tone.

"Your friend Takshaka, the best of snakes, is not here. He went to Kurukshetra
before the burning of the Khandava. And you cannot defeat these two, Vasudeva
and Arjuna, Shakra, standing here in this battle. Hear what I say. These are the
gods Nara and Narayana, famous in heaven. You yourself know their strength and
their courage. These two cannot be defeated. They are hard to overcome,
unconquered in battle, even in all the worlds, those ancient seers, the best of
seers. They deserve the greatest honour from all the gods and asuras, and from
yakshas, rakshasas, gandharvas, men, kinnaras and snakes. So you should go from
here with the gods, Vasava. And see that the destruction of the Khandava is
fated."

When he heard this voice, the lord of the immortals said, "It is true." He let
go of his anger and indignation and set out for heaven. When they saw the great
one set out, the gods all followed Shatakratu together, quickly, king. When they
saw the king of the gods go with the gods, the heroes Vasudeva and Arjuna gave a
lion's roar. With the king of the gods gone, king, Krishna and the Pandava, full
of joy, went on burning the forest without fear.

As the wind drives away clouds, Arjuna drove off the gods, and with showers of
arrows he killed the creatures that lived in the Khandava. As Savyasachin shot,
not one creature could get out, cut down by his arrows. Even the great creatures
there could not look at Arjuna in battle, with his arrows that never missed, let
alone fight him. He pierced a hundred with one arrow, and one with a hundred.
They fell lifeless into the fire, as if struck down by Death himself. They found
no comfort on the river banks or in rough places. Even in the dwellings of the
ancestors and the gods, the heat was felt. Thousands of hosts of creatures cried
out in distress. Elephants, deer and birds cried out, and at that noise the fish
in the Ganga and the ocean shook with fear. No one could even look at
strong-armed Arjuna, or at mighty Krishna, let alone fight them. Those
rakshasas, danavas and snakes who rushed out all together, Hari killed with his
discus. Their heads and bodies split by the force of the discus, those huge
creatures fell dead into the mouth of the blazing fire.

Fed with floods of flesh and blood, and floods of fat, the fire rose above the
sky, and could be seen without smoke. His eyes blazing, his tongue blazing, his
huge mouth blazing and open wide, his hair blazing and standing on end, his eyes
tawny, he drank the fat of living creatures. Having won that nectar, given him
by Krishna and Arjuna, the eater of offerings was glad and full, and reached the
greatest contentment.

---

Then Madhusudana saw an asura named Maya suddenly running from Takshaka's house.
Agni, with the wind for his charioteer, wanted to burn him, and pursued him,
taking on a body with matted hair, roaring like a cloud. And Vasudeva stood with
his discus raised, wanting to kill him.

When he saw the discus raised and the fire wanting to burn him, Maya cried out,
"Run to me, Arjuna!"

When he heard that cry of fear, Dhananjaya Partha answered Maya, as if giving
him life, "Do not be afraid!"

When Partha had given Maya, Namuchi's brother, his promise of safety, the
Dasharha did not want to kill him, and the fire did not burn him.

As that forest burned, the fire did not burn six: Ashvasena, Maya, and the four
Sharngaka birds.

**Divergences.**

* **How long it burned.** The CE says at the end of the episode that Agni burned the forest
  "for six days" (*ahāni pañca caikaṃ ca*, 1.225.15). The popular "fifteen days" comes from a
  rejected line (\*2138).
* The vulgate has Skanda, Kubera's attendants and the wind join the gods' attack (\*2130,
  \*2131). Not in the CE.
* **Ashvasena.** The snake cursed here is Takshaka's son. The CE names him again at 9.60.35.

**Choices.**

* 1.217.3 *āviddhāv iva*: "like a single spinning wheel", literally "as if whirled round".
* 1.217.11 *kṛṣṇavartman*: "the black-tracked one", the fire.
* 1.217.19 *akṣamātrāḥ*: streams "as thick as axles".
* 1.217.21 *namucihā*: "the slayer of Namuchi", Indra.
* 1.218.3 *khagamaiḥ*: "arrows", literally "sky-goers".
* 1.218.11 *apratiṣṭhaḥ*: "may you never find a place to rest". The curse is spoken by
  Arjuna, Agni and Vasudeva.

  "club". The word usually means a palanquin, but here it must be a weapon.
