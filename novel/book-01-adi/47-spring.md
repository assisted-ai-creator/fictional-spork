# Spring

When Kunti's sons and Dhritarashtra's sons had been born, the daughter of the
king of Madra spoke to Pandu in private.

"I have no quarrel with you, tamer of enemies, even though you cannot beget, and
even though I, who deserve the best, am always placed below. I did not grieve so
much when I heard that Gandhari had a hundred sons, king, joy of the Kurus. But
this is my great grief: that I, Kunti's equal, have no son. By good fortune my
husband now has children by Kunti. If Kuntibhoja's daughter would give me
children too, it would be a kindness to me, and good for you as well. My pride
holds me back from speaking to Kunti, because she is my co-wife. But if you are
pleased with me, urge her yourself."

"This is always in my heart too, Madri," said Pandu, "but I did not dare say it
to you, for I did not know whether you would welcome it or not. Now that I know
your mind, I will try. I am sure that if I speak to her, she will do what I
say."

Then Pandu spoke to Kunti again, alone. "Give my family children, lovely one,
and do the world a kindness. So that the funeral offerings to me and my
forefathers do not come to an end, and for love of me, do this highest good,
lovely one. For the sake of fame, do a very hard thing. Indra won the rule of
the gods, and still, wanting fame, he offered sacrifices. So too brahmins who
know the mantras practise the hardest austerities and still go to their
teachers, for the sake of fame, spirited one. So too all the royal seers and the
brahmins rich in austerity have done hard deeds, high and low, for the sake of
fame. Carry Madri across, as if on a raft, blameless one. Share your children
with her, and win the highest glory."

When he said this, Kunti said to Madri, "Think of a god once. You will surely
have a child worthy of you by him."

Then Madri thought for a moment, and turned her mind to the two Ashvins. They
came, and they fathered twin sons on her, Nakula and Sahadeva, whose beauty had
no equal on earth. For the twins too a voice without a body spoke. "These two
will have beauty, strength of spirit and every good quality beyond all other
men. They will shine with splendour, rich in beauty and in wealth."

The dwellers on Shatashringa named them, lord of the people, with devotion, with
rites and with blessings. The eldest of Kunti's sons they called Yudhishthira,
the middle one Bhimasena, and the third Arjuna. The brahmins, glad at heart,
named Madri's sons: the first-born Nakula, the other Sahadeva. And those best of
the Kurus were born a year apart.

Then Pandu urged Kunti again, for Madri's sake. But Pritha, when he spoke to her
in private, king, said to him, "I told her to call once, and she won two. She
has cheated me. I am afraid she will get the better of me. That is the way of
women. I was a fool. I did not know that calling two gods at once would bear two
fruits. So do not ask me again. Let that be my boon from you."

So the five sons of Pandu were born, given by the gods, mighty and famous, to
make the Kuru line grow. They had every lucky mark, and they were as lovely to
look at as the moon. They were proud as lions, great archers, and they walked
with a lion's stride. With necks like lions, those lords of men grew up with the
courage of gods. As they grew there on the holy Himalayan mountain, they filled
the gathered great seers with wonder. And the five and the hundred, who made the
Kuru line grow, all grew up in a short time, like lotuses in water.

---

Pandu looked at his five handsome sons in the great forest, growing up under the
protection of his own strong arms, and he was happy on the mountain.

Once, in the season of Madhu and Madhava, when the forest was in full flower,
the time that bewilders all creatures, the king was roaming the forest with his
wife. The forest was full of palasha, tilaka, mango, champaka and paribhadraka
trees, and many others rich in fruit and flowers. It was lovely with ponds of
every kind and with lotus pools. When Pandu looked at the forest, love was born
in his heart.

He was wandering there glad at heart, like an immortal, and Madri followed him
alone, wearing a fine dress. He looked at her, young and thinly clad, and his
desire grew like a fire that springs up in a thicket. The lotus-eyed king saw
her alone, his equal, and he could not hold back that desire. He was overpowered
by it. Then the king seized her by force, alone as they were. The queen tried to
stop him, struggling as hard as she could. But his whole being was swept up in
desire, and he did not think of the curse. He went to Madri by force, as a man
goes to a woman. Fallen into the power of love, to the end of his life, the
Kaurava threw off his fear of the curse and took his beloved by force. Time
itself had clouded the mind of the man who was ruled by desire. It churned up
his senses and was lost with his reason.

When he had lain with his wife, joy of the Kurus, Pandu, the righteous man, met
with the law of Time.

Then Madri held the lifeless king in her arms and cried out in grief, again and
again. Kunti came with her sons and the two sons of Madri, the Pandavas, to
where the king lay in that state.

"Come here alone, Kunti," Madri cried out in her pain. "Let the children stay
there."

When she heard this, Kunti held the children back where they were. She cried
out, "I am lost!" and ran to her. When she saw Pandu and Madri lying on the
ground, Kunti's whole body was seized with grief, and she wept in her anguish.

"I always guarded this hero," she said, "and he was always master of himself.
How could he break through the curse, when he knew what the forest-dweller had
said? Surely you should have guarded the king, Madri. How could you tempt him,
here where no one was? He was always so wretched, brooding on the curse. How did
joy come to him when he found you alone? You are blessed, daughter of the
Bahlikas, luckier than I am, for you saw the king's face when he was happy."

"He was lured on," said Madri, "and I held him back again and again. But he
would not hold himself back. He wanted to make what was fated come true."

"I am the elder," said Kunti, "his first wife by dharma. The first fruit of
dharma is mine. Do not stop me from what must be, Madri. I will follow my
husband, who has gone into the power of the dead. Get up, let him go, and look
after these children."

"I will follow our husband," said Madri, "who never turned back. I have not had
my fill of love. Let my elder sister allow it. This best of the Bharatas came to
me and perished with his desire unspent. How could I cut off his desire in the
house of Yama? And if I lived, noble lady, I could not treat your sons just as I
treat my own. That sin would touch me. So, Kunti, treat my two sons as your own.
The king has gone into the power of the dead desiring me. Let my body be burned
with the king's body, well covered. Do this kindness for me, noble lady. Watch
over the children, and think of my good. I see nothing else I need to ask of
you."

When she had said this, the famous daughter of the king of Madra, his wife by
dharma, quickly followed that bull among men onto the fire of his pyre.

**Divergences.**

* **Madri did not seduce him.** The CE says Pandu "seized her by force" while "the
  queen tried to stop him, struggling as hard as she could" (1.116.8–10). Kunti
  blames her (1.116.19), but Madri answers that she held him back again and again (1.116.22). See ledger L-78.
* **No account of the funeral here.** The vulgate adds the ascetics' rites and a
  scene in which Madri shows Kunti the dressed body (\*1263–\*1265, App. I No. 69).
  The CE goes straight from Madri's charge to her mounting the pyre (1.116.31). The
  bodies are brought to Hastinapura in the next adhyaya.
* **"A year apart."** The CE says only that the five were born "a year apart"
  (1.115.21). The vulgate's time notes (\*1245, \*1255: "in the fourteenth year",
  "in the eighteenth year") are not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.115.2: Madri's opening is compressed and some of it is obscure. The novel gives
  its plain sense: she has no grievance, though she is always ranked second.
* 1.115.6 *stambhaḥ*: "stiffness", "pride". She cannot bring herself to ask a
  co-wife.
* 1.115.14 *plaveneva tāraya*: "carry her across as if with a raft".
* 1.115.23 *vañcitā*: "cheated". Kunti's word.
* 1.116.2 *madhumādhave*: in the months Madhu and Madhava, i.e. spring.
* 1.116.3: the trees are named as the text names them.
* 1.116.11: *sākṣāt kālena mohitā*: "clouded by Time itself".
* 1.116.12 *yuyuje kāladharmaṇā*: "was joined with the law of Time", i.e. died.
* 1.116.21 *bāhlīki*: Madri, of the Bahlika country (Madra).
* 1.116.29 *supraticchannam*: "well covered", her body to be burned veiled, with his.
* 1.116.31 *citāgnistham*: the king is already "on the pyre fire" when Madri mounts
  it.
