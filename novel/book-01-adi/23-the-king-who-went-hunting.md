# The King Who Went Hunting

"Brahmin," said Janamejaya, "I have heard from you, as it should be told, how
the gods, the danavas and the rakshasas came down to earth in portions, and the
gandharvas and apsarases too. Now I want to hear again the line of the Kurus,
from the beginning, told by you in the presence of these brahmins and seers."

There was a mighty king named Dushyanta, bull of the Bharatas, who founded the
line of the Pauravas and protected the earth to its four ends. That lord of men
ruled a full quarter of the earth, and the lands girt by the sea. The winner of
battles, the crusher of enemies, ruled all the lands as far as the barbarians
and the forest tribes. He ruled to the edges of the ocean, the mine of jewels,
where the four classes of people lived.

While that king ruled, no one caused the classes to be mixed, no one had to
plough or dig mines, and no one did evil. Men took their pleasure in dharma,
tiger among men, and so they gained both dharma and wealth, while that lord of
the land ruled. There was no fear of thieves, my friend, and not the least fear
of hunger. There was no fear of sickness while he ruled the land. The classes
delighted in their own dharma, and they did not perform rites to gain what they
desired. With that lord of the earth to shelter them, they had nothing to fear
from any side.

The rain god rained at the right time, and the crops were full of fruit. The
earth was rich in every jewel and full of wealth. The young king was of
wonderful courage, and his body was as hard as a thunderbolt. He could have
lifted Mandara in his two arms and carried it off, with its woods and its
forests. He was a master of the bow and of fighting with the mace, of the sword
and the other hand weapons, on elephant and on horseback. In strength he was
like Vishnu, in splendour like the sun. He was as unshakeable as the ocean, and
as patient as the earth. That lord of the earth was loved by all. His city and
his kingdom were content, and he lived among people known for the love of
dharma.

---

Once the strong-armed king went to a deep forest with a great army and many
beasts of burden, surrounded by hundreds of horses and elephants. He went with
hundreds of warriors around him, heroes carrying swords and spears, maces and
clubs, lances and javelins. As the king went, a joyful din rose up. There were
the lion roars of the warriors, the sound of conches and drums and the rumble of
chariot wheels. The great elephants trumpeted, the horses neighed, and the men
gave war cries and slapped their arms.

The women stood on the roofs of the finest palaces and watched the hero, who had
won his own fame, in all his royal splendour. The crowds of women watched him,
weapon in hand. He was like Shakra, the slayer of enemies, who could hold back
the elephants of his foes. *This is the tiger among men*, they thought, *of
wonderful valour in battle. Those who meet the strength of his arms are no
longer enemies.* As they said this, the women praised the king with love. They
let showers of flowers fall on his head.

On every side, the chief brahmins praised him. He set out gladly for the forest,
wanting to hunt deer. The people of the city and the country followed him a long
way, and then turned back when the king gave them leave.

The lord of the earth filled the earth and the heavens with the roar of his
chariot, which was like Garuda. As he went, the wise king saw a forest like
Indra's garden of Nandana, full of bilva, arka and khadira trees, and crowded
with kapittha and dhava trees. It was rough with mountain slopes and covered
with stones. It had no water and no people, and it stretched for many yojanas.
It was full of herds of deer and other fierce creatures of the forest.

Dushyanta, the tiger among men, with his servants, his army and his beasts of
burden, stirred that forest up, killing deer of many kinds. When packs of tigers
came within range of his arrows, Dushyanta brought them down and pierced them
with his shafts. Some, the bull among men struck from far off with arrows.
Others, which came close, he cut down with his sword. He killed some antelopes
with his spear, the best of spearmen. He knew the art of whirling the mace, and
he moved about with courage beyond measure. He went about killing the beasts and
birds of the forest with lances and swords, with maces, clubs and heavy staves.

As the king of wonderful courage and his warriors, who loved to fight, stirred
up the great forest, the great beasts fled it. Their herds were scattered and
their leaders killed, and the herds of deer cried out in fear here and there.
Some went to a dried-up river. Worn thin by the hope of water that failed them,
their hearts spent with running, they fell down senseless. Some, overcome with
hunger and thirst and weariness, fell to the ground, and hungry tigers among men
ate them. Some of the forest people made a fire, lit it, and cut up the meat
properly and ate it.

Some elephants there, mad with rut, strong, wounded by weapons, curled up the
tips of their trunks and ran off fast in fear. Dropping dung and urine, and
streaming with blood, the great wild elephants trampled many men there. The
forest, full of buffaloes, covered by the cloud of the army with its rain of
arrows, shone, its great beasts killed by the king.

---

When he had killed thousands of deer, the king, with his great train, went on
into another forest, still hunting deer. He was alone now, the man of great
strength, hungry and thirsty. He came to the end of that forest and reached a
great barren plain. He crossed that too, and came to another great forest, with
a fine hermitage in it. It gladdened the heart and was a delight to the eye, and
cool winds blew there.

It was full of flowering trees, with soft, pleasant grass, wide, and ringing
with the sweet songs of birds. Trees with spreading branches gave pleasant
shade, and the creepers stirred with the humming of the bees. It was full of the
greatest beauty. In that forest there was no tree without flowers or without
fruit, no tree with thorns, and none without bees swarming on it. It rang with
the songs of birds and was lovely with flowers, with trees that flowered in
every season and soft, pleasant grass. The great archer entered that lovely,
splendid forest.

Trees heavy with blossom, shaken by the wind, dropped showers of many-coloured
flowers again and again. Trees that touched the sky, ringing with the sweet
voices of birds, shone there, clothed in bright flowers. On their young shoots,
bent low under their flowers, the birds sang softly, with the bees. He saw many
spots adorned with heaps of flowers, ringed with bowers of creepers, that
gladdened the heart, and the man of great splendour was full of joy.

The forest was beautiful with trees whose branches, covered in flowers, twined
together, like the banners of great Indra. A cool, sweet-smelling breeze,
carrying the pollen of flowers, moved among the trees of the forest as if it
wanted to play with them. The king saw that forest, with all these virtues,
growing lovely on the bank of a river, like a raised banner.

As he looked at that forest, full of happy birds, he saw a fine hermitage,
lovely and charming, full of trees of every kind, where fires were burning. It
was full of ascetics and Valakhilyas and hosts of sages. It had many
fire-houses, and it was spread with carpets of flowers, and great, wide groves
made it shine.

It lay on both banks of the holy Malini river, king, a river with pleasant
water, crowded with birds of many kinds, lovely with its ascetics' groves. There
he saw beasts of prey that were gentle, and he was filled with joy.

The glorious warrior came to that hermitage, like the world of the gods, lovely
on every side. He saw the river that flowed round the hermitage, its water holy,
standing there like the mother of all living creatures. Its banks were full of
chakravaka birds, and it carried a foam of flowers along. Kinnaras lived there,
and monkeys and bears came to it. It rang with holy recitation, and its
sandbanks made it beautiful. Elephants in rut, tigers and great snakes came to
it.

When he saw the river that flowed round the hermitage, and the hermitage itself,
the king made up his mind to go in. It was adorned by the Malini, with its
islands and lovely banks, like the place of Nara and Narayana made beautiful by
the Ganga. He entered the great forest, which rang with the cries of peacocks
mad with joy.

The lord of men came to that place. It was like the garden of Chitraratha, full
of every virtue and too bright for words. He had come to see the great seer
Kanva, the Kashyapa, whose wealth was austerity. At the entrance to the forest
he halted his army, crowded with chariots and horses and foot soldiers. "I will
go to see the sage, the Kashyapa, who is free from passion and rich in
austerity," he told it. "Wait here until I come back."

When the lord of men reached that forest, like Nandana, he forgot his hunger and
thirst, and he was filled with joy. The lord of men put aside the signs of his
rank. He went to that fine hermitage with his ministers and his family priest.
He wanted to see the seer there, the heap of austerity who never passes away.

He looked at the hermitage, like the world of Brahma, ringing with the songs of
the bees and full of birds of many kinds. As the rites went on, the tiger among
men heard the chief of the Rig Veda priests chanting its verses, word by word
and step by step. The hermitage shone with men who knew the science of sacrifice
and its limbs, who chanted the steps, men of boundless spirit and strict
discipline. The best of the Atharva Veda priests, honoured by the priests of the
great sacrifices, recited the collections word by word and step by step. Other
brahmins spoke with correct sounds, and the glorious hermitage, ringing with
their voices, shone like the world of Brahma.

It rang on every side with learned men. Some knew how to prepare the sacrifice
and were skilled in the rules of recitation. Some had mastered the Vedas and
understood the truth of logic and its meaning. Some were skilled in joining many
statements together, and some knew the special rites. Some were devoted to the
dharma of release. Some knew how to set out a proposition, raise an objection,
and reach a conclusion and the highest truth. And among them was the chief of
the Lokayatikas, the materialists.

Here and there the slayer of enemy heroes saw lords among brahmins,
self-controlled, strict in their vows, given to prayer and offerings, perfected.
The lord of the earth was amazed to see the bright seats, spread with flowers,
set out with care. When the best of kings saw the worship the brahmins paid at
the shrines of the gods, he thought himself in the world of Brahma. He could not
look enough at that fine and lovely hermitage, guarded by the austerity of the
Kashyapa, and full of hosts of ascetics.

The slayer of enemies entered the Kashyapa's dwelling with his ministers and his
family priest. It was secluded, lovely beyond words and blessed, and seers of
great vows, rich in austerity, surrounded it on every side.

**Choices.**

* 1.62.6 *nākṛṣyakarakṛj janaḥ*: "no one had to plough or dig mines", following
  Ganguli's reading (the land yielded by itself).
* 1.62.9 *daive karmaṇi niḥspṛhāḥ*: "they did not perform rites to gain what they
  desired", again following Ganguli. It could also mean they had no desire for
  the fruits of rites.
* 1.63.22 *naravyāghraiḥ*: the text says "tigers among men" ate the fallen deer.
  The phrase is kept, though it is odd.
* 1.64.2: the king is "alone" (*eka eva*), but at 1.64.29 he enters the hermitage
  with his ministers and priest. The CE has both.
* 1.64.25 *maharṣiṃ kāśyapaṃ … kaṇvam*: Kanva is "the Kashyapa", of Kashyapa's
  line. The novel uses "the Kashyapa" where the text does.
* 1.64.31–37: the lists of learning in the hermitage are given in full. They
  include the *lokāyatika*, the materialists (1.64.37).
* 1.64.24 *naranārāyaṇasthānam*: the hermitage is compared to the place of Nara
  and Narayana on the Ganga (Badari).
* Bilva, arka, khadira, kapittha, dhava: forest trees, kept as names.
