# The Grandsons

"When you lived in Nandana, taking any form you wished, for a million years,"
said Ashtaka, "why, first among the men of the golden age, did you leave it and
come down to the earth?"

"As a kinsman, a friend or one of his own people is abandoned by men here when
his wealth is gone," said Yayati, "so there the hosts of the gods, with their
lords, at once abandon a man whose merit is spent."

"How do they come to have their merit spent there?" said Ashtaka. "My mind is
very much confused about this. Where do they go, with what marks, and to whose
home? Tell me, for I think you know the field."

"They all fall into this earthly hell, lord of men, crying out," said Yayati.
"They are spent, and in many ways they grow again, to be food for herons,
jackals and crows. So a man should avoid wicked deeds, blamed in the world. I
have told you all of it, king. Now tell me, what else shall I tell you?"

"When birds peck at them," said Ashtaka, "and vultures, and blue-throated birds
and winged things, how do they come to be, and how do they come into being
again? I have not heard of any other earthly hell."

"After the body," said Yayati, "as their deeds swell, they plainly wander over
the earth. They fall into this earthly hell, and they do not count the many
years. For sixty thousand years they fall through the sky, and for eighty
thousand, and as they fall, fierce earthly rakshasas with sharp teeth tear at
them."

"If the fierce earthly rakshasas with sharp teeth tear at them for their sins as
they fall," said Ashtaka, "how do they come to be? How do they come into being?
How do they become embryos?"

"Seed, joined with the essence of flowers and fruit, follows the blood," said
Yayati. "When the man lets it go, it goes into the woman's flow, and there it
becomes an embryo. They enter trees and plants, water, wind, earth and sky, and
all four-footed and two-footed beings. So they come to be, and so they become
embryos."

"Does the embryo make another body here," said Ashtaka, "or does it go by its
own desire as it comes into the human womb? Tell me, for I ask in doubt. How
does it get its body and its growth, its eyes and ears and its awareness? Tell
me the truth of it all, since I ask, for we all think you know the field, my
friend."

"In the season," said Yayati, "the wind carries the seed, joined with the juice
of flowers, up into the womb. There the subtle elements take charge, and little
by little they make the embryo grow. When he is born, with his limbs formed, a
man is the seat of the six ways of knowing. He knows sound with his ears. He
sees all forms with his eyes, smells with his nose, tastes with his tongue,
feels touch with his skin, and knows what he feels with his mind. So know,
Ashtaka, how the great self that bears the breath gathers in the body."

"When a man dies," said Ashtaka, "and is burned, or buried, or ground away, he
comes to nothing and is destroyed. How does he become aware of himself
afterwards?"

"He lets go of his breath, groaning like a sleeper, lion among kings," said
Yayati, "and puts his good and bad deeds in front of him. Following the lead of
the wind, he leaves the body and takes another womb. Those who do good go to a
good womb. Those who do evil go to an evil womb. The evil become worms and
insects. I do not wish to say more, great one. Four-footed, two-footed and
six-footed, so they come to be, so they become embryos. I have told you all of
it. What more do you ask, lion among kings?"

"What must a mortal do, my friend," said Ashtaka, "to win the best worlds, by
austerity or by learning? Since I ask, tell me all of it, as it is: by what he
may go, one step after another, to the fair worlds."

"Austerity and giving, calm and self-control, modesty, honesty and pity for all
beings," said Yayati, "are always destroyed by pride, when a man is overcome by
darkness. So the good say. The man who studies and thinks himself learned, and
with his learning destroys the fame of others, has worlds that come to an end,
and that sacred learning gives him no fruit.

"Four acts give safety, and they give danger when they are done wrongly: the
fire offering made in pride, silence kept in pride, study done in pride, and
sacrifice made in pride. A man should not be glad when he is honoured, nor
pained when he is scorned. The good honour the good in this world, and the bad
do not win a good mind. 'I will give this, I will sacrifice this, I will study
this: that is my vow.' They say such things bring danger, and they must always
be avoided. The wise who know the ancient refuge, which the mind worships, reach
the highest good, the shining form, and the highest peace, after death and
here."

---

"How does a householder reach the gods?" said Ashtaka. "How does a wandering
beggar, or one who does a teacher's work, or a forest-dweller set on the good
path? Many people say many things about this now."

"The student," said Yayati, "studies when he is called, and needs no urging for
his teacher's work. He rises first and sleeps last. He is gentle,
self-controlled, steady and careful, and given to study. Such a student
succeeds.

"The householder gains wealth by dharma, and sacrifices with it. He always
gives, and feeds his guests, and he never takes what others have not given. That
is the ancient secret teaching for the householder.

"The sage in the forest lives by his own strength, and he has turned from wrong.
He gives to others, and he harms no one. Living in the forest, sparing in his
food and his movements, such a sage reaches the highest success.

"The beggar does not live by a craft, and he has no home. He has always
conquered his senses, and he is free on every side. He stays in no house, he
carries little, he moves little, and he wanders the lands alone. He is the true
beggar.

"The night by which worlds are won, and desires become conquered and happy, the
wise man should strive for that very night, to stand in the forest, master of
himself. The forest-dweller who gives up the elements of his body in the forest
raises ten forefathers before him, ten after him, and himself the twenty-first,
to the worlds of merit."

"How many kinds of sage are there," said Ashtaka, "and how many kinds of
silence? Tell us. We want to hear."

"The man who lives in the forest, and the village is behind him," said Yayati,
"or who lives in the village, and the forest is behind him, he is a sage, lord
of men."

"How is the village behind a man who lives in the forest?" said Ashtaka. "And
how is the forest behind a man who lives in the village?"

"The sage who lives in the forest should use nothing from the village," said
Yayati. "Then, as he lives in the forest, the village is behind him. Without a
fire, without a home, without a family or a school, the sage should want only as
much cloth as covers him, and only as much food as keeps him alive. Then, as he
lives in the village, the forest is behind him.

"The sage who gives up desires, gives up works, conquers his senses and keeps
the sage's silence wins success in the world. His teeth are clean, his nails
cut, he is always bathed and cared for. Dark he may be, but he stands firm in
bright deeds. Who would not honour him? When the sage, worn thin by austerity,
wasted, his flesh and bones and blood all spent, has gone beyond the pairs of
opposites and keeps the sage's silence, he wins this world and then the next.
When the sage seeks his food with his mouth, like a cow, then the world he had
before is ready for immortality."

"Of these two, which reaches oneness with the gods first," said Ashtaka, "as
they both run, like the sun and the moon?"

"The homeless beggar," said Yayati, "who holds himself in check while he lives
in the village among householders who follow their desires, reaches it first. If
a man who has not lived a long life falls into wrong, and grieves when he has
done it, let him then take up another austerity. What is cruel, they call
unwholesome. The man who follows dharma without thought of gain, having nothing
and ruling nothing, that is honesty, king, that is concentration, that is
noble."

"Who sent you here today as a messenger, king," said Ashtaka, "young, garlanded,
handsome and shining? Where have you come from, and from which direction? Or is
there a place on earth for you?"

"My merit spent," said Yayati, "I am thrown down from the sky to enter this
earthly hell. When I have spoken to you, I will fall at once. The brahmins, the
guardians of the world, are hurrying me. I chose to fall near the good, and they
are all gathered here, full of virtue. I won this boon from Shakra as I was
about to fall to the surface of the earth, lord of men."

"I ask you: do not fall," said Ashtaka, "if I have worlds of my own here, lord
of the earth, whether in the air or in heaven. I think you know the field of
that dharma."

"As many cattle and horses as there are on the earth," said Yayati, "with the
wild beasts and the mountains, so many worlds you have in heaven. Know this,
lion among kings."

"I give them to you. Do not fall," said Ashtaka. "The worlds I have in heaven,
king of kings, in the air or in heaven, take them quickly, conqueror of
enemies."

"No one like me, not a brahmin but a knower of Brahman, lives by taking gifts,
chief of kings," said Yayati. "As one should always give to brahmins, so I gave
before, lord of men. No one who is not a brahmin should live like a beggar, nor
a brahmin woman who is a hero's wife. How could I do now what I have never done
before, seeking to know what is good?"

"I ask you, you whose form is so lovely," said Pratardana, "if I, Pratardana,
have worlds of my own, whether in the air or in heaven. I think you know the
field of that dharma."

"You have many worlds, lord of men," said Yayati, "each one of them seven times
seven days, flowing with honey, rich with ghee, free of sorrow. They are
endless, and they wait for you."

"I give them to you. Do not fall," said Pratardana. "The worlds that are mine,
let them be yours, whether in the air or in heaven. Take them quickly, and be
free of your confusion."

"A king of equal splendour should not want another's merit as his safety, king,
since he is a king," said Yayati. "A wise king, brought into trouble by the
order of fate, should never do anything base. A king should think of the path of
dharma that brings fame, and act with his eyes on dharma. A man like me, who
knows dharma, should not do the mean thing you say. How could I do what I have
never done, what others have not done, seeking to know what is good?"

As King Yayati said this, Vasumanas, the best of kings, spoke to him.

"I, Vasumanas, son of Rushadashva, ask you," said Vasumanas, "if I have a world
in heaven, lord of men, or a famous one in the air, great one. I think you know
the field of that dharma."

"As much as the air, the earth and the quarters of the sky," said Yayati, "and
all that the sun warms with his heat: so many worlds you have in heaven. They
are endless, and they wait for you."

"I give them to you. Do not fall," said Vasumanas. "The worlds that are mine,
let them be yours. Buy them, king, even for a blade of grass, if taking a gift
is wrong for you."

"I do not remember ever making a false sale," said Yayati, "or taking anything
for nothing, fearing it as I would from a child. I would not do what others have
never done, seeking to know what is good."

"Take those worlds, king, as my gift," said Vasumanas, "if a sale does not
please you. I will not take them back, lord of men. Let all those worlds be
yours."

"I am Shibi, son of Ushinara," said Shibi, "and I ask you if I too have worlds
here, my friend, whether in the air or in heaven. I think you know the field of
that dharma."

"You have never scorned, in word or in heart, learned king, those who came to
you wanting something," said Yayati. "So you have endless worlds in heaven, like
lightning, full of sound, and great."

"Take those worlds, king, as my gift," said Shibi, "if a sale does not please
you. I will not take them back once I have given them, the worlds you would go
to and enjoy."

"As you have power like Indra's, lord of men, and those worlds are endless,"
said Yayati, "so I take no joy in a world given by another. That is why, Shibi,
I do not welcome the gift."

"If you will not accept our worlds one by one, king," said Ashtaka, "we will
give them all to you and go to hell."

"Give to the worthy what is due to them, good men, in truth and kindness," said
Yayati. "But I will not dare to do what I have never done before."

"Whose are these five golden chariots that we see," said Ashtaka, "standing high
and shining, blazing like tongues of fire?"

"These five golden chariots, standing high and shining, blazing like tongues of
fire," said Yayati, "will carry you."

"Mount the chariot, king," said Ashtaka, "and stride through the sky. We will
follow when the time comes."

"We must all go now together," said Yayati, "for we have won heaven. There is
our path to the home of the gods, free of dust. We can see it."

Then all those best of kings mounted the chariots and set out, striding up to
heaven, filling heaven and earth with the light of their dharma.

"I thought that I would go first, alone," said Ashtaka, "for Indra, the great
one, is my friend in every way. How is it that Shibi, son of Ushinara, has gone
ahead of us all, driving his horses at full speed?"

"This son of Ushinara gave all the wealth he found for the path of the gods,"
said Yayati. "That is why Shibi is the best of us. Giving, austerity, truth and
dharma, modesty, fortune, forgiveness, gentleness and patience, king: these all
live in King Shibi, who has no equal, through his kind understanding. Because he
lives like this, and is held back by modesty, Shibi has gone ahead in his
chariot."

Then Ashtaka, out of curiosity, asked again his mother's father, who was like
Indra, "I ask you, lord of men, tell me truly: where are you from, and whose son
are you? What you have done, no other kshatriya or brahmin in the world has
done."

"I am Yayati, the son of Nahusha, the father of Puru," he said, "and I was an
emperor here. I tell a secret to my own people: I am your mother's father, and
now you know it. I conquered this whole earth. I tethered fine, one-hoofed
horses fit for sacrifice and gave them to the brahmins, and so the gods had
their share of merit. I gave this whole earth to the brahmins, full of beasts of
burden, with cattle and gold and the finest wealth. There were a hundred arbudas
of cows. By my truth heaven and earth stand, and fire blazes among men. I have
never spoken a word in vain, for the good honour truth. All the gods, the sages
and the worlds are to be honoured by truth. That is what is in my heart. Whoever
tells the story of all of us who have won heaven, as it happened, without envy,
to the best of brahmins, will reach the same world as we."

So that great king, the crusher of enemies, was saved by his daughter's sons.
His deeds were most generous, and he left the earth and went to heaven, filling
the earth with the fame of his deeds.

**Divergences.**

* **The daughter is not named.** Here the four kings are simply Yayati's
  *dauhitras*, "daughter's sons" (1.88.26), and he their *mātāmaha*, "mother's
  father" (1.88.20–21). Their mother, Madhavi, and her story belong to Book 5
  (5.113–121). The novel does not bring them in here.

**Choices, and places where the sense is uncertain.**

* 1.85.8 *ṣaṣṭiṃ sahasrāṇi … tathā aśītiṃ parivatsarāṇi*: read as "sixty thousand
  years, and eighty thousand". It may instead mean "sixty thousand and eighty
  years".
* 1.85.10–16: Yayati's account of conception and the senses is given as he states
  it.
* 1.85.27: a hard verse. The novel gives a plain reading ("the ancient refuge,
  which the mind worships").
* 1.86.6: the "night" by which worlds are won is left as the text's image.
* 1.86.15 *asitaṃ sitakarmastham*: "dark, yet standing in bright deeds". Kept.
* 1.87.3–4 and 1.87.12 (the hero's wife): obscure verses. The plainest reading is
  given.

  sense is uncertain.
