# The Wife Who Woke Him

"At this very time," said the bard, "the great ascetic Jaratkaru was wandering
over the whole earth, a sage who made his home wherever evening found him.
Mighty in his power, he kept a vow that is hard for those who have not mastered
themselves, and he wandered from one holy ford to another, bathing in each. He
lived on air and ate nothing, and day by day he wasted away.

"And he saw his forefathers hanging head down in a pit. They were clinging to a
clump of virana grass that had only one root left, and a rat that lived in a
hole was slowly gnawing at that root. They had nothing to eat. They were thin
and wretched, suffering in the pit, and they longed for someone to save them. He
went up to them, wretched himself, and spoke to those wretched ones.

"'Who are you, hanging here, clinging to this clump of virana grass? It is weak,
for the rat that lives in the hole has eaten its roots. The one root that is
left in this clump of virana, the rat is gnawing slowly away with his sharp
teeth. There is so little of it left that it will soon be cut through, and then
you will fall head first into this pit. It pains me to see you hanging head
down, in such terrible danger. What can I do for you that you would like? Tell
me quickly: can you be saved from this danger by a quarter of my austerity, or a
third, or a half? Or let all of you be saved by the whole of my austerity. Let
it be as you wish.'

"'You are rich in austerity, celibate one,' said the forefathers, 'since you
want to save us here. But this cannot be taken away by austerity, best of
brahmins. We have the fruit of austerity, my son, best of speakers. It is
because our line is dying out, brahmin, that we are falling into a foul hell.
Hanging here, my son, our minds are dim, and so we do not know you, though you
are famous in the world for your manhood. You are rich and blessed, since you
come and pity us, who are so wretched and deserve pity. Listen, brahmin, to who
we are.

"'We are seers of strict vows, called the Yayavaras. Because our line is dying
out, lord, we have fallen here from a holy world. Our austerity and our merit
are lost, for we have no thread of descendants. We have one thread left today,
but it is as good as none. They say he is a kinsman in our family, that unlucky
son of unlucky fathers. He is known as Jaratkaru. He knows the Vedas and their
limbs through and through. He is self-controlled and great-souled, of good vows
and great austerity. Because of his greed for austerity, we have been brought to
this misery. He has no wife, no son and no kin at all. So we hang in this pit,
our senses gone, as if we had no protector.

"'If you see him, speak to him, and be our protector. Tell him, "Your
forefathers are hanging head down in a pit, in misery. Take a wife, good sir,
and have children, lord. You are the only thread of our family left, you who are
rich in austerity."

"'This clump of virana grass that you see us clinging to, brahmin, was our
family's clump. It made our family grow. The roots of this plant that you see
here, brahmin, are our threads, my son, which Time has eaten away. This root
that you see half-eaten, brahmin, from which we all hang, is the one who has
taken up austerity. The rat you see, brahmin, is Time, of great strength. Slowly
it is gnawing away and wasting that foolish one who delights in austerity,
Jaratkaru, greedy for austerity, dull and thoughtless.

"'His austerity will not save us, best of the good. See us, our roots cut,
fallen, our minds struck by Time, bound for hell, as if we were wrongdoers. And
when we have fallen here, with our forefathers before us, he too will be cut off
by Time, and go to hell after us. Austerity or sacrifice, or any other great
means of purifying, none of it is equal to having descendants, my son. That is
the view of the good. So, my son, if you see the ascetic Jaratkaru, tell him.
Tell him all of this, just as you have seen it. So that he takes a wife and has
sons, you must speak to him, brahmin, as our protector.'

---

"When he heard this, Jaratkaru was overcome with pain and grief. He spoke to his
forefathers in his sorrow, his voice choked with tears.

"'I am Jaratkaru. I am your son, and the sinner. Punish me, for I have done
wrong and I have not mastered myself.'

"'Son,' said the forefathers, 'it is good fortune that chance has brought you to
this place. Why have you not taken a wife, brahmin?'

"'Forefathers,' said Jaratkaru, 'this purpose has always been turning in my
heart: that I would carry this body to the next world without ever spilling my
seed. But now I have seen you hanging like birds, grandfathers, I have turned my
mind from the celibate life. I will do what you wish, and I will marry. There is
no doubt of it. That is, if I ever find a girl with my own name, one who is
offered to me of her own accord, like alms, and one whom I do not have to
support. I will take her. If I can find a marriage like that, I will make it.
Otherwise I will not. That is the truth, grandfathers.'

"When he had said this to his forefathers, the sage wandered the earth. And he
found no wife, Shaunaka, for people thought, *He is old.*

"When he fell into despair, and his forefathers still urged him on, he went into
the forest and cried aloud in great sorrow.

"'Let all the creatures that are here, moving and unmoving, and those that are
hidden, hear what I say. My forefathers, in their pain and misery, are urging me
on, as I practise fierce austerity: "Marry!" To do what pleases them, I wander
the whole earth wanting a wife, begging for a girl as alms. I am poor, and my
life is hard, and my forefathers have commanded me. Whichever of the creatures I
have named has a daughter, let them give me the girl, as I wander in every
direction. A girl with my own name, offered like alms, whom I will not have to
support: give me that girl.'

"Then the snakes who had been set to watch Jaratkaru took the news and told
Vasuki. When he heard them, the king of the nagas took that girl, finely
adorned, and went into the forest, close to where he was. There the king of the
nagas, Vasuki, offered the girl to that great man as alms, brahmin. But he did
not accept her. He thought she did not have his name, and the question of her
keep was not settled. He was set on release, and he was of two minds about
taking a wife. So he asked the girl's name, joy of the Bhrigus, and he said,
'Vasuki, I will not support her.'

---

"Then Vasuki said to the seer Jaratkaru, 'This girl has your name. She is my
sister, and she is devoted to austerity. I will support your wife. Take her,
best of brahmins. And I will protect her with all my strength, rich in
austerity.'

"When the naga had promised, 'I will support my sister,' Jaratkaru went to the
serpent's house. There the best of those who know the mantras, grown great in
austerity, of great vows, righteous, took her hand, with the rites and the
mantras before him. Then, praised by the great seers, he took his wife to a
bright chamber, which the king of the snakes had approved. A bed had been made
ready there, covered with fine spreads, and there Jaratkaru lived with his wife.

"There the good man made an agreement with his wife. 'You must never do anything
that displeases me, or say anything that displeases me. If you do anything
displeasing, I will leave you, and I will leave my home in your house. Take
these words to heart, as I have said them.'

"Then the snake king's sister, deeply anxious and in great distress, said to
him, 'So be it.' And so the famous woman waited on her husband, whose ways were
so hard, wanting only to please him, with the watchful care of the white crow.

"Then one day, at her season, Vasuki's sister bathed and went to her husband,
the great sage, as was right. And a child was conceived in her, like a blazing
fire, full of austerity, as bright as the fire that belongs to all men. It grew
like the moon in the bright half of the month.

"A few days later, the great ascetic Jaratkaru laid his head in her lap and fell
asleep, as if he were worn out. While that lord of brahmins slept, the sun went
towards the western mountain, as the day came to an end, brahmin. Then Vasuki's
sister, a woman of spirit, grew afraid that her husband would fail in his
dharma, and she thought, *What should I do? Is it right to wake my husband, or
not? His ways are hard, and he is righteous. How can I keep from offending him?
It is either the anger of a righteous man, or his failure in dharma. Failure in
dharma is the heavier.* So she made up her mind. *If I wake him, he will surely
be angry. But if the time of twilight passes, he will surely fail in his
dharma.*

"When she had settled this in her mind, the snake woman Jaratkaru, who spoke
sweetly, said gently to the seer, who lay asleep, blazing with austerity, like
fire, 'Rise, blessed one. The sun is setting. Say your evening prayers, lord.
Touch water, you who keep your vows. This is the hour, lovely and dreadful, when
the fire offerings are lit, and the twilight is coming on in the west, my lord.'

"When she said this, the blessed Jaratkaru, the great ascetic, said to his wife,
his lips trembling, 'You have insulted me, snake woman. I will not live with
you. I will go the way I came. I am sure in my heart, fair one, that the sun has
no power to set at its proper time while I am asleep. No one would want to live
where he has been insulted, let alone a righteous man like me, or one like me.'

"When her husband said this, words that made her heart shake, Jaratkaru,
Vasuki's sister, said there in her home, 'I did not wake you to insult you. I
did it so that you would not fail in your dharma, brahmin.'

"When she said this, the great ascetic Jaratkaru, the seer, spoke to his wife,
full of anger and wanting to leave the snake woman. 'My words have never spoken
a lie. I will go, snake woman. This was the agreement you and I made between us
before. I have lived here happily, dear one. Tell your brother, lovely one, when
I have gone from here, timid one, "The blessed one has gone." And you must not
grieve when I have left.'

"When he said this, the faultless woman, Jaratkaru, answered her husband
Jaratkaru. She was lost in worry and sorrow. Her voice was choked with tears and
her mouth was dry. She joined her palms, the lovely woman, and her eyes were
full of tears. Her heart was shaking, but she held herself steady.

"'You know dharma. You should not leave me, for I have done no wrong. I keep to
dharma, as you do, and I have always delighted in pleasing you and doing you
good. The reason I was given to you, best of brahmins, has not yet come about.
What will Vasuki say to me, poor unlucky woman that I am? My kin are overcome by
their mother's curse, good sir. They wanted a child from you, and there is no
sign of it yet. If I could have a child by you, my kin would be safe. Let this
union of mine with you not be in vain, brahmin. I want only the good of my kin,
and I beg you, blessed one, be gracious. You have planted this child in me,
though its form cannot be seen yet. How can a great soul like you want to leave
me, when I have done no wrong?'

"When she said this, the sage, the ascetic Jaratkaru, said to his wife words
that were fitting. 'There is a child, fortunate one, in you, like the fire that
belongs to all men. He will be a seer, deeply righteous, who knows the Vedas and
their limbs through and through.'

"When he had said this, the righteous Jaratkaru, the great seer, went away
again, his mind made up, to practise fierce austerity.

---

"As soon as her husband had gone, Jaratkaru went quickly to her brother and told
him all that had happened, rich in austerity. Then the best of snakes heard this
very unwelcome news, and he spoke to his wretched sister, more wretched himself.

"'You know, good sister, what must be done, and why you were given. If a son
were born to you from him, for the good of the snakes, then that mighty son, so
they say, would free us from the snake sacrifice. The Grandfather told me so
long ago, with the gods. Is there a child in you, fortunate one, from that best
of sages? I do not want that wise man's marriage to be without fruit. It is not
right for me to ask you such a thing, I know. But the matter is so grave that I
have pressed you. I know how hard your husband is to live with, and how great
his austerity, so I will not go after him. He might curse me. Tell me everything
your husband did, good sister. Pull out this terrible thorn that has been in my
heart so long.'

"When he said this, Jaratkaru answered, comforting Vasuki, the lord of the
snakes, in his distress.

"'I asked that great-souled, great ascetic for a child. He pointed to my womb
and said, "There is," and then he went. I do not remember him ever saying
anything false before, even in jest. How could he now, king, at such a time? He
said, "Do not worry about what has to be done, snake woman. A son will be born
to you, bright as fire and the sun." When he had said this to me, brother, my
husband went to the ascetics' forest. So let this great sorrow that sits in your
heart go away.'

"When he heard this, Vasuki, the king of the nagas, was filled with great joy,
and he accepted his sister's words, saying, 'So be it.' And the best of snakes
honoured his own sister, born of the same mother, with kind words and respect
and gifts of wealth, and with the honour that was due to her.

"Then the child grew, with great power, shining like the sun, best of brahmins,
like the moon rising in the sky in the bright half of the month. In time, the
snake's sister gave birth, brahmin, to a boy who shone like a child of the gods,
and he took away the fear of both his father's family and his mother's. He grew
up there, in the house of the king of the nagas, and he learned the Vedas and
their limbs from the Bhargava, Chyavana's son. He kept his vows while he was
still a boy, and he was gifted with wisdom and goodness and every virtue. His
name became famous in the worlds: Astika. His father had said, 'There is,'
*asti*, and gone to the forest while he was still in the womb. So he was known
by the name Astika.

"Though he was only a boy, his wisdom had no measure. As he lived there in the
house of the king of the snakes, he was guarded with every care. Like the
blessed lord of the gods, who holds the trident and gives gold, he grew, and he
delighted all the snakes."

**Choices.**

* **The pit told twice.** 1.13 tells Jaratkaru's meeting with his forefathers
  in brief. Here (1.41) the CE tells it in full and differently: at first he does
  not name himself, the root is Jaratkaru, and the rat is Time. The novel keeps
  both tellings.
* **A new condition.** At 1.42.7 and 1.42.15 Jaratkaru adds that he will not
  support the bride, which 1.13 does not mention. Vasuki's promise to support her
  (1.43.2) answers it.
* 1.41.1 *yatrasāyaṃgṛhaḥ*: "whose home is wherever evening finds him".
* 1.42.19 *mokṣabhāve sthitaś cāpi dvandvībhūtaḥ parigrahe*: "set on release,
  and of two minds about taking a wife".
* 1.43.10 *upāyaiḥ śvetakākīyaiḥ*: literally "by the ways of the white crow". It
  is an idiom for sleepless watchfulness. Kept as "with the watchful care of the
  white crow".
* 1.43.12, 1.43.38 *vaiśvānara*: the fire "that belongs to all men".
* 1.43.20 *prāduṣkṛtāgnihotraḥ … muhūrto ramyadāruṇaḥ*: "the hour, lovely and
  dreadful, when the fire offerings are made manifest".
* 1.43.23: Jaratkaru claims the sun would not dare set while he slept. The text
  says it, and the novel keeps it.
* 1.44.11 *rājan*: Jaratkaru addresses her brother as "king".
* 1.44.18 *bhārgavāc cyavanātmajāt*: "from the Bhargava, Chyavana's son". The
  text gives no name, though Chyavana's son in chapter 6 is Pramati.
* 1.44.20: the name Astika is explained from *asti*, "there is".
* 1.44.22 *deveśaḥ śūlapāṇir hiraṇyadaḥ*: "the lord of the gods, trident in
  hand, giver of gold". The text does not name the god.
