# Bhishma

So wise Shantanu, honoured by gods, royal seers and seers, was famous in all the
worlds as a righteous man who spoke the truth. Self-control, generosity,
patience, understanding, modesty, steadiness and the highest splendour were
always in Shantanu, that great-spirited bull among men. Such was the king, full
of virtues, skilled in dharma and in wealth, the protector of the Bharata line
and of good people.

His neck was lined like a conch shell, his shoulders were broad, and he moved
like an elephant in rut. He was settled in his mind that dharma is higher than
desire and than wealth. These were in the great-spirited Shantanu, bull of the
Bharatas, and there was no kshatriya like him in dharma. He kept to his own
dharma, the best of all who know dharma, and the kings of the earth consecrated
him as king of kings. With the protector of the Bharatas over them, the kings
were free of grief, fear and harm, and they slept and woke in comfort.

While the world was guarded by kings with Shantanu at their head, all the
classes kept to their rules, and the brahmins stood first. The kshatriyas served
the brahmins, the vaishyas were loyal to the kshatriyas, and the shudras,
devoted to brahmins and kshatriyas, served the vaishyas.

He lived in lovely Hastinapura, the capital of the Kurus, and ruled the earth to
the ocean's edge. He was like the king of the gods, knowing dharma, truthful and
honest, and through his giving, his dharma and his austerity he had the highest
fortune. He was free of passion and hatred, as good to look at as the moon, as
bright as the sun, as swift as the wind, like Death in his anger, and like the
earth in his patience. While Shantanu ruled the earth, king, no cattle, boars,
deer or birds were ever killed for nothing. In a kingdom where dharma and the
brahmins came first, Shantanu, humble and self-disciplined, ruled all creatures
alike, free from desire and passion. Rites were begun then for the gods, the
seers and the forefathers, and no living creature was killed against dharma. For
the unhappy, the unprotected, and those born as animals, the king was a father
to all.

While that best of Kuru kings was king of kings, speech rested on truth, and the
mind rested on giving and dharma. For sixteen years, and eight, and four, and
eight more, he found no delight in women, and he lived in the forest.

His son, Gangeya, named Devavrata, a Vasu, was like him in form, in conduct, in
behaviour and in learning. He was master of all weapons, those of kings and all
the others, of great strength, great spirit and great courage, a great warrior.

---

Once, when he had shot a deer and was following the river Ganga, King Shantanu
saw that the Bhagirathi had only a little water in it. When he saw it, Shantanu,
the bull among men, wondered, *Why is this best of rivers not flowing today as
it did before?*

Then the great-minded king looked for the cause, and he saw a boy, handsome,
tall and lovely to look at, using a divine weapon like the god Indra himself. He
stood there, having dammed the whole Ganga with his sharp arrows. When the king
saw the river Ganga held back by arrows near the boy, he was amazed at the deed,
which was more than human.

Shantanu had seen his son only at birth, long ago, and the wise king did not
remember him well enough to know him as his own. But when the boy saw his
father, he bewildered him by magic. And when he had bewildered him, he quickly
vanished there and then.

When King Shantanu saw that wonder, he suspected that it was his son, and he
said to Ganga, "Show him to me."

Ganga, taking on a lovely form, showed him the boy, adorned, holding him by the
right hand. Shantanu did not know her, though he had seen her before, adorned
with jewels and wearing spotless clothes.

"This is the eighth son you fathered on me long ago, king," said Ganga. "Here he
is, tiger among men. Take him home. The mighty one learned the Vedas and their
limbs from Vasishtha himself. He is skilled in weapons, a great archer, like the
king of the gods in battle, always honoured by the gods and the asuras, Bharata.
Whatever science Ushanas knows, this one knows it all. And the whole science
that the son of Angiras knows, whom the gods and asuras bow to, is established
in him too, in your strong-armed, great-souled son, with all its limbs and
branches. And whatever weapon the mighty seer Rama Jamadagnya knows, whom others
cannot overcome, is established in him too. This great archer, king, skilled in
the dharma and the business of kings, your own son, I have given back to you.
Take the hero home, hero."

Given leave by her, Shantanu took his son, who shone like the sun, and went to
his city. When the Paurava came to his city, which was like the city of Indra,
he thought himself rich in everything he could desire. Then he consecrated his
son as crown prince among the Pauravas. Shantanu's famous son delighted the
Pauravas, his father and the kingdom by his conduct, bull of the Bharatas. So
the king of boundless courage lived happily with his son for four years.

---

Once the lord of the earth went into a forest by the river Yamuna, and he
smelled a wonderful fragrance, too fine for words. Looking for where it came
from, he wandered everywhere, and then he saw a girl, of the fisher people, as
lovely as a goddess. As soon as he saw the dark-eyed girl, he asked her, "Whose
are you, who are you, and what are you doing, timid one?"

"I am a fisherman's daughter," she said. "I ferry a boat, for the sake of
dharma, at the command of my father, the great king of the fishermen. Bless
you."

King Shantanu looked at the fisher girl, lovely as a goddess, with her beauty,
her sweetness and her fragrance, and he desired her. He went to her father and
asked for her, and asked her father to give her to him.

The king of the fishermen answered the lord of the earth, "As soon as she was
born, this lovely girl was to be given to a fine husband. But I have a wish in
my heart. Hear it, lord of men. If you ask me for her as your lawful wife,
sinless one, then make me a promise by your truth, for you speak the truth. If
you promise, I will give you this girl, king. For I will never find a husband
for her equal to you."

"When I have heard your condition, fisherman," said Shantanu, "I will decide. If
it can be given, I will give it. But I will never give what should not be
given."

"The son who is born of her," said the fisherman, "must be consecrated as king,
lord of the earth, after you, and no one else, king."

Shantanu did not want to grant the fisherman this, though he burned with a
fierce desire of the body, Bharata. The lord of the earth went back to
Hastinapura, still thinking of the fisher girl, his mind struck down by grief.

Then one day, when Shantanu sat grieving, deep in thought, his son Devavrata
came to his father and said, "All is well with you on every side. All the kings
obey you. Why, then, do you grieve here so often, in pain? Why are you so deep
in thought, king, and never speak?"

When his son said this, Shantanu answered, "It is true, as you say, that I am
deep in thought. You are my only child in our great family, Bharata, and mortals
do not last. That is why I grieve, my son. If some misfortune came to you,
Gangeya, we would have no family left. You alone are surely better than a
hundred sons. And I do not want to marry again for nothing. I want it only so
that our line will not be destroyed. Bless you. Those who speak of dharma say
that to have one son is to have no son. The daily fire offering, the three
Vedas, and sacrifices with their gifts are not worth a sixteenth part of a
child. It is so among men, and among all creatures too, great and wise one:
there is no doubt in my mind about children. This is the lasting teaching of the
three Vedas and of the best of the ancients. And you are a hero, always quick to
anger, always with your weapons, Bharata. Your death can come from nothing but a
weapon, sinless one. So I am in doubt. If you were gone, what would happen? That
is the whole reason for my sorrow, my son."

When he had understood the whole reason, the wise Devavrata went away to think
it over. He went at once to an old minister, who wished his father well, and
asked him the cause of his father's grief. And when the chief of the Kurus asked
him properly, bull of the Bharatas, the minister told him of the condition about
the girl.

Then Devavrata went with old kshatriyas to the king of the fishermen, and asked
for the girl himself, for his father. The fisherman received him properly and
honoured him, and when he was seated in the royal assembly, Bharata, he said to
him,

"You alone are protector enough for Shantanu, bull among men, the best son of
those who have sons. What can I say to you? Who, even Indra of the hundred
sacrifices himself, would not regret passing over a marriage alliance so worthy
and so much to be wished for? This girl is the child of a noble man equal to you
in virtues, from whose seed the famous Satyavati was born. He praised your
father to me many times, my son, as the one among all kings worthy to marry
Satyavati, Bharata. I even turned away the divine seer Asita before. That best
of seers wanted Satyavati very much. But as the girl's father, I will say one
thing, bull of the Bharatas. I see only one fault here: a mighty rival. Whoever
has you as his rival, gandharva or asura, would never live happily if you were
angry, scorcher of enemies. That is the only fault here, lord of the earth, no
other. Know this, bless you, in giving and in not giving, scorcher of enemies."

When he said this, Gangeya answered fittingly, in the hearing of the kings, for
his father's sake, Bharata. "Take this as my true resolve, best of those who
keep the truth. No one born, and no one yet unborn, would dare to say this. I
will do as you say. The son born of her will be our king."

When he said this, the fisherman spoke to him again, wishing to do something
very hard for the kingdom's sake, bull of the Bharatas. "You alone are protector
enough for Shantanu, whose splendour has no measure, and you are the lord who
can give the girl, righteous one. But hear this word of mine, my friend, and
what must be done. I speak as girls' fathers do, tamer of enemies. What you have
promised among the kings for Satyavati's sake, you who are devoted to truth and
dharma, is worthy of you. It will not be otherwise, strong-armed one. There is
no doubt of that. But about the children you may have, we have a great doubt."

When he understood what the fisherman meant, the prince, devoted to truth and
dharma, made a promise then, king, wishing to do what would please his father.

"King of the fishermen," said Devavrata, "best of kings, hear what I say for my
father's sake, in the hearing of these kings. I gave up the kingdom already,
lord of men. Now, for the sake of children, I make this resolve. From this day
on, fisherman, I will live a celibate life. Though I have no son, I will have
worlds in heaven that never pass away."

When he heard his words, the hair on the righteous fisherman's body stood up,
and he answered, "I give her."

Then in the air the apsarases, the gods and the hosts of seers rained down
flowers and said, "This is Bhishma, the terrible one."

Then, for his father's sake, he said to the famous woman, "Mount the chariot,
mother. Let us go home." When Bhishma had said this, he lifted the proud woman
onto the chariot, came to Hastinapura, and told Shantanu everything.

The kings praised his deed, which was so hard to do, together and one by one,
and they said, "This is Bhishma."

When Shantanu saw the hard deed that Bhishma had done, his father, well pleased,
himself gave him the boon of dying when he chose.

**Divergences.**

* **No vow to serve the throne.** In the CE, Devavrata gives up the kingdom (1.94.79,
  87) and vows celibacy (1.94.88). He makes no promise to protect whoever sits on the
  throne of Hastinapura. See ledger **L-70**. The name and the boon are verified in
  **L-04** and **L-05**.

**Choices.**

* 1.94.18 *samāḥ ṣoḍaśāṣṭau ca catasro 'ṣṭau tathāparāḥ*: "sixteen years, and eight,
  and four, and eight more", thirty-six in all. Kept as the text counts.
* 1.94.19 *vasuḥ*: Devavrata is "a Vasu" (Dyaus of chapter 34).
* 1.94.26: Shantanu "did not remember" his son, since he had seen him only at birth.

  of Ushanas (Shukra), of the son of Angiras (Brihaspati) and the weapons of Rama
  Jamadagnya. The CE does not say here where or how he learned from Rama.
* 1.94.44 *dharmārthaṃ vāhaye tarīm*: she ferries the boat "for dharma".

* 1.94.71: the fisherman says Satyavati was born "from the seed" of a noble man equal
  to Devavrata, which points back to Uparichara Vasu (chapter 19, 1.57).

  kings.
