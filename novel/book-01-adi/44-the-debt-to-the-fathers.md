# The Debt to the Fathers

There on Shatashringa, living in the best of austerities, the mighty Pandu
became dear to the companies of siddhas and charanas. He served others, did not
boast, held himself in check and mastered his senses. By his own strength,
Bharata, he set out to win heaven. To some of them he became a brother, and to
some a friend. Other seers looked after him as if he were their son. After a
long time, bull of the Bharatas, Pandu reached an austerity without stain, and
he became like a brahmin seer.

Longing to cross over to the far shore of heaven, he set out north from
Shatashringa with his two wives. But the ascetics there said to him, "As we have
gone higher and higher, northwards on the king of mountains, we have seen many
hard places on it. There are playgrounds of the gods, the gandharvas and the
apsarases, and Kubera's gardens, level and rough. There are the slopes of great
rivers, and hard mountain caves. There are places of everlasting snow, with no
trees, beasts or birds. Some places have great rains. Some are hard to cross,
and some cannot be reached at all. No bird could fly over them, let alone any
other creature. Only the wind goes there, and the siddhas and the great seers.
How could these two princesses travel on this king of mountains and not break
down? They have not deserved such hardship. Do not go, bull of the Bharatas."

"Great ones," said Pandu, "they say the door of heaven is not open to a man
without children. I have no children, and that is why I burn with grief. I tell
you this plainly. Men are born on the earth with four debts, owed by the hundred
thousand, to the ancestors, the gods, the seers and men. Those who know dharma
have laid it down that there are no worlds for the man who does not see to these
in their time. A man satisfies the gods with sacrifices, the sages with study
and austerity, the ancestors with sons and funeral offerings, and men with
kindness. I am freed by dharma from my debts to the seers, the gods and men. But
I am not freed from the debt to the ancestors, and that is why I burn, rich in
austerity. When a man's body dies, the ancestors surely die with it. That is
certain. So the best of men are born here to have children. That great man begot
me in my father's field. How can children be born in my field in the same way?"

"You have children, righteous king," said the ascetics. "We know it with the
eyes of the gods: fine, sinless offspring, like the gods. Tiger among men, bring
about by your own action what the gods have ordained. A wise man who does not
waver gets his fruit without pain. Now that you see the fruit, son, you should
make the effort. When you have children with good qualities, you will be happy."

When he heard the ascetics' words, Pandu was lost in thought. He knew that the
deer's curse had spoiled his own power to beget.

---

He spoke in private to Kunti, his famous wife by dharma, urging her towards a
way to get children in time of need.

"The wise know, Kunti, that offspring is the foundation of the worlds, bound up
with dharma. That is the eternal dharma, from the beginning. Sacrifices offered,
gifts given, austerities practised, rules well kept: none of it purifies a man
here who has no children, so it is said. I know this, you of the bright smile,
and I see it. *I have no children*, I think, *so I will not reach the fair
worlds.* Because of the deer's curse, my power to beget is destroyed, for I had
no mastery of myself and did a cruel thing, timid one.

"In the teaching of dharma, Pritha, there are six kinds of sons who are heirs
and kin, and six kinds who are not. Hear them from me. The son born to a man
himself; the son begotten on his wife by another; the son acquired; the son of a
remarried woman; the son of an unmarried girl; and the son born of an unchaste
wife. And the son given; the son bought; the son adopted; the son who comes of
his own accord; the son the bride brings with her to the wedding; the son of
another's seed; and the son born of a woman of low birth. When one kind fails, a
man should seek the next. In time of need men want a son from a better man than
themselves. Good men get offspring that bring the fruit of dharma, better even
than from their own seed, Pritha. So Manu, son of the Self-born, said.

"So, since I have lost the power to beget, I will send you today. Get children
from a man my equal or my better, famous one.

"Hear this story too, Kunti, about Sharadandayani. She was a hero's wife, and
her elders appointed her to bear children. In her season, pure and bathed,
Kunti, she went out at night to a crossroads. She chose a brahmin who had
reached perfection, and offered into the fire in the rite for a son. When that
rite was over, she lived with him. And there she bore three great warriors,
Durjaya and his brothers. You too, lovely one, at my bidding, lose no time in
getting children from a brahmin great in austerity."

When he said this to her, great king, Kunti answered Pandu, her husband, the
hero, the bull of the Kurus, the lord of the earth.

"You know dharma. You should never say such a thing to me, your wife by dharma,
who loves you, lotus-eyed one. You yourself, strong-armed Bharata, will beget
children on me by dharma, heroes full of courage. Tiger among men, I would go to
heaven with you. Come to me for children yourself, joy of the Kurus. I would not
go to any other man even in my mind, only to you. And what other man on earth is
better than you?

"But first hear from me this old story, in keeping with dharma, which I heard
and will tell you, wide-eyed one.

"There was once a king, so they say, named Vyushitashva. He was deeply devoted
to dharma, and he made the line of Puru grow. When that righteous, great-souled
king offered sacrifice, the gods came to him with Indra and the great seers.
Indra was drunk with soma, and the brahmins with their fees, at the sacrifice of
the great royal seer Vyushitashva. Then Vyushitashva shone out above all
mortals, king, as the sun outshines all beings at the end of winter. The best of
kings conquered and seized the kings of the east, the north, the middle and the
south, and drove them before him. At the great horse sacrifice the mighty
Vyushitashva became the lord of kings, with the strength of ten elephants. And
those who know the old stories sing a verse about it: *Vyushitashva conquered
this earth to the ocean's edge, and he protected all the classes as a father
protects the sons of his body.*

"He offered great sacrifices and gave wealth to the brahmins. He gathered jewels
without end and brought the great rites to pass. He pressed much soma and
performed the soma sacrifices. He had a wife he loved dearly, lord of men, named
Bhadra, the daughter of Kakshivat, whose beauty had no equal on earth. We have
heard that the two of them desired each other. Wild with desire for her, he fell
ill of consumption. Before long he went down like the setting sun.

"When that lord of men was dead, his wife was in deep grief. She had no son,
tiger among men, and she lamented, so we have heard. Hear it, lord of men.
Bhadra was in the utmost pain.

"'Any woman without a son, you who know dharma best, who lives without her
husband, does not live. She only grieves. It is better for a woman to die than
to be without her husband, bull of the kshatriyas. I want to go where you have
gone. Be kind, and take me. Without you I cannot bear to live a moment. Be kind
to me, king, and take me from here quickly. I will follow behind you, tiger
among men, on level ground and rough, as you go and never come back. I will be
like your shadow, king, never leaving you, always obedient, always delighting in
what is dear and good to you. From today, king, without you, lotus-eyed one,
cruel sorrows will overwhelm me and wither my heart. Surely I was unlucky. In my
former bodies I must have parted companions, or separated lovers who had come
together, king. So this grief of parting from you has come to me, stored up by
wicked deeds in former bodies. From today, king, I will lie on a bed of kusha
grass, lost in misery, longing only to see you. Show yourself to me, tiger among
men. I am wretched, helpless and pitiful, and I am weeping, lord of men.'

"As she lamented like this, again and again, with her arms around the dead body,
a voice spoke from the air, so they say. 'Rise, Bhadra, and go. I give you a
boon here. I will beget children on you, you of the lovely smile. When you have
bathed after your season, on the eighth or the fourteenth night of the moon, lie
down with me on your own bed, lovely one.'

"When she was told this, the queen, true to her husband, did exactly as the
voice had said, for she longed for sons. And from that dead body, lord of men,
the queen bore sons: three Shalvas and four Madras, best of the Bharatas.

"In the same way, bull of the Bharatas, you too can beget sons on me by the
power of your mind, by your austerity and your yoga."

**Divergences.**

* **No journey to Brahma's world.** The vulgate has the seers set out on the
  new-moon day to visit Brahma, with Pandu wanting to go too (\*1171–\*1172). In the
  CE Pandu simply sets out north "to cross to the far shore of heaven" (1.111.5).

**Choices.**

* 1.111.12 *ṛṇaiś caturbhiḥ*: the four debts, to "ancestors, gods, seers and men".
* 1.111.16: "when the body dies, the ancestors surely die", that is, their line of
  offerings ends.
* 1.111.27–29: the kinds of sons are listed tersely and some terms are obscure. The
  text announces six and six but names more. The novel gives the terms in order:
  *svayaṃjāta*, *praṇīta*, *parikrīta*, *paunarbhava*, *kānīna*, *svairiṇyāṃ jāta*;
  *datta*, *krīta*, *kṛtrima*, *upagata svayam*, *sahoḍha*, *jātaretas*,
  *hīnayonidhṛta*. "Son of another's seed" for *jātaretas* is a guess, and "acquired" for
  *parikrīta* keeps it apart from *krīta*, "bought".
* 1.111.31: the sentence is compressed. Read as Pandu's claim that a son from a better
  man is to be preferred even to one's own seed, on Manu's authority.
* 1.111.33–35: Sharadandayani's rite: *puṣpeṇa* (in her season), *catuṣpathe* (at a
  crossroads), *puṃsavana* (the rite for a son).
* 1.112.9: Indra "drunk with soma" and the brahmins "with their fees" is the text's
  own wit.
* 1.112.15 *kākṣīvatī*: "daughter of Kakshivat".
* 1.112.29 *vāk … antarhitā*: "a voice, unseen". The voice is the dead king's.
* 1.112.33: "three Shalvas and four Madras", the peoples descended from them.
