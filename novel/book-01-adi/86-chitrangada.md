# Chitrangada

When he had told the brahmins all that had happened, Bharata, the son of the
wielder of the thunderbolt went on to the flank of the Himalaya. At the
Agastyavata, at Vasishtha's mountain and at Bhrigutunga, Kunti's son purified
himself. The best of the Kurus gave thousands of cows at the bathing places and
shrines, and he gave dwellings to brahmins. The best of men bathed at the
bathing place of Hiranyabindu, and saw the finest of mountains and its holy
shrines.

Then the best of men came down with the brahmins, Bharata, and the bull of the
Bharatas went on, wanting to reach the east. One after another the best of the
Kurus saw the bathing places, the lovely river Utpalini on the way to the
Naimisha forest, the Nanda and the Aparananda, the glorious Kaushiki, the
Mahanadi, Gaya, and the Ganga, Bharata. So, seeing all the bathing places and
the hermitages, and purifying himself, he gave wealth to brahmins. He went to
every holy bathing place and shrine in Anga, Vanga and Kalinga, and when he had
seen them as the rules require, he gave wealth there too.

At the gates of the kingdom of Kalinga, the brahmins who had followed the
Pandava took their leave of Kunti's son and turned back, Bharata. With their
leave, the hero Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, went on with a few companions towards
the sea. The lord passed through the Kalingas and went on, looking at the lands
and the shrines, righteous and lovely. He saw Mount Mahendra, graced by
ascetics, and went slowly along the seashore to Manalura.

There the strong-armed man visited all the holy bathing places and shrines, and
then he went to the lord of the land, the king of Manalura, Chitravahana, who
knew dharma, king. The king had a lovely daughter named Chitrangada. Arjuna saw
her by chance as she walked about in the city. When he saw Chitravahana's
daughter, with her fine hips, he desired her. He went to the king and told him
what he wanted.

The king spoke to him gently. "There was a king in this family named
Prabhankara. He had no son, and wanting children, he practised the highest
austerity. By that fierce austerity and by bowing down to him, he pleased
Shankara, the lord, the great god, Uma's husband. The blessed god gave him one
child for each generation of the family. So one child is always born in this
family. All my forefathers had sons. But to me this daughter was born, and she
is surely the one who will carry on the family. *She is my son*: that is how I
think of her, best of men. By the rule made for such a case she has been named
an appointed daughter, bull of the Bharatas. Let this be her bride-price: let a
son be born here who will carry on the family. On this condition, Pandava, take
her."

He promised, "So be it," and took the girl, and Kunti's son lived in that city
for three years.

---

Then the bull of the Bharatas went to the bathing places in the southern sea,
very holy, and graced by ascetics. But there were five bathing places there that
the ascetics shunned, though ascetics had used them in the past: Agastya's
bathing place, Saubhadra's, the very pure Pauloma's, the bright Karandhama's,
which gives the fruit of a horse sacrifice, and Bharadvaja's great bathing
place, which takes away sin.

The Pandava noticed that those bathing places were deserted, and saw that the
sages, who had their minds on dharma, kept away from them. With joined hands the
joy of the Kurus asked the ascetics, "Why do those who speak of Brahman keep
away from these bathing places?"

"Five crocodiles live in them," said the ascetics, "and they carry off the
ascetics. That is why these bathing places are shunned, joy of the Kurus."

When he heard them, the strong-armed man, the best of men, went to see those
bathing places, though the ascetics tried to stop him. He came to Saubhadra, the
fine bathing place of the great seer, and the hero, the tamer of enemies,
plunged in quickly and bathed. Then a great creature that moved under the water,
a crocodile, seized that tiger among men, Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, in the water.
But strong-armed Kunti's son, the best of the strong, took hold of the creature
as it struggled, and by his strength he rose out of the water with it.

As soon as glorious Arjuna dragged the crocodile out, it became a lovely woman,
decked with every ornament, shining with beauty, king, heavenly in form and
delightful. When he saw that great wonder, Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, was very
pleased, and he said to the woman, "Who are you, lovely one? And why do you move
in the water? Why did you do this great evil in the past?"

"I am an apsaras, strong-armed one," said the woman, "who roams the forests of
the gods, always dear to the lord of wealth. My name is Varga, mighty one. I
have four other friends, all lovely, who can go wherever they wish. With them I
set out once for the home of one of the guardians of the world. On the way we
all saw a brahmin, strict in his vows, handsome, studying the Veda, alone,
living in solitude. The forest there was filled with the power of his austerity,
king. He lit up the whole place like the sun. When we saw his austerity, and his
wonderful beauty, we came down to that place, meaning to disturb his austerity.

"I and Saurabheyi, Samichi, Budbuda and Lata went up to the brahmin all at the
same moment, Bharata. We sang and laughed and tried to tempt that brahmin. But
he did not turn his mind to us at all, hero. The man of great power did not
waver. He stayed fixed in his pure austerity. Then the brahmin grew angry and
cursed us, bull among kshatriyas. 'You will be crocodiles, and live in the water
for a hundred years.'

"Then we were all deeply distressed, best of the Bharatas, and we went for
shelter to that brahmin, rich in austerity, who had not fallen. 'We were proud
of our beauty and our youth, and of love,' we said, 'and we did a wrong thing.
Please forgive us, brahmin. It is death enough for us, rich in austerity, that
we came here to tempt you, whose soul is disciplined. Those who think about
dharma hold that women were made not to be killed. So you should not harm us,
knower of dharma, by dharma. A brahmin is said to be a friend to all beings,
knower of dharma. Let this saying of the wise come true, good sir. The good
protect those who come to them for shelter. We have come to you for shelter. So
you should forgive us.'

"When we said this, the righteous brahmin, whose deeds were good, and whose
radiance was like the sun and the moon, showed us his grace, hero.

"'A hundred, a thousand, all,' said the brahmin, 'these are words that mean
without end. But "a hundred" here is a measure. It does not mean without end.
When you are crocodiles, seizing men in the water, some best of men will drag
you out of the water onto the land. Then you will all take your own forms again.
I have never told a lie, even in jest. And from now on all those bathing places
will be known everywhere by the name of the Women's Bathing Places, and they
will be holy, and purify the wise.'

"Then we bowed to the brahmin, walked round him, keeping him on our right, and
left that place in great distress. We thought, *Where can we all meet, in a
short time, the man who will give us back our forms?* As we thought about it,
Bharata, we saw, in a moment, the fortunate divine seer Narada. When we saw the
divine seer of boundless splendour, we were all glad. We greeted him, Partha,
and stood before him, our faces troubled. He asked us the cause of our sorrow,
and we told him. When he had heard what had happened, he said, 'In the lowlands
by the southern sea there are five bathing places, holy and lovely. Go there
without delay. There that tiger among men, the pure-souled Pandava Dhananjaya,
will soon free you from your sorrow, without a doubt.'

"When we heard what he said, hero, we all came here. And it has come true today.
You have freed me, sinless one. But these four friends of mine are still in the
water. Do a good deed, hero. Free them all."

Then the best of the Pandavas, undaunted and mighty, freed them all from that
curse, lord of the people. They rose from the water and took back their own
forms, and the apsarases looked as they had before, king.

When he had cleared the bathing places, and given the apsarases leave to go, the
lord went back to the city of Manalura to see Chitrangada again. On her he
fathered a son, King Babhruvahana. When he had seen him, the Pandava went on
towards Gokarna, king.

**Divergences.**

* **Manalura.** The CE's Adi Parva names Chitravahana's city Manalura (1.207.13–14; 1.209.23),
  and the novel keeps that name here. In Book 14 the CE calls Babhruvahana "lord of Manipura"
  (14.77.46; 14.78.2), the form Ganguli uses throughout. A warrior princess Chitrangada comes
  from later tellings. The CE says only that she was lovely, and that her father had made her
  an "appointed daughter". See ledger L-104.
* The vulgate has Arjuna reach Manalura "in the thirteenth month" (\*2032) and marry there after a
  formal wedding (\*2037), and adds a longer exchange with the king (\*2035). The vulgate also has
  Arjuna give Babhruvahana to Chitravahana as the bride-price (App. I No. 112). The CE says only
  that he fathered King Babhruvahana on her, saw him, and left (1.209.24).
* The vulgate names a route south by Shurparaka, the Godavari and the Kaveri (\*2030, \*2031). Not
  in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.207.1 *vajradharātmajaḥ*: "the son of the wielder of the thunderbolt", Arjuna as Indra's son.
* 1.207.21 *putrikā hetuvidhinā*: "an appointed daughter, by the rule made for such a case". Her
  son is to count as her father's heir.
* 1.207.23 *trihimāḥ samāḥ*: "three years", literally "three winters".
* 1.208.3: the five bathing places are named as in the text.
* 1.208.14 *dhanapateḥ*: "the lord of wealth", Kubera.
* 1.208.19: the five apsarases are Varga, Saurabheyi, Samichi, Budbuda and Lata.
* 1.209.3 *eṣa eva vadho 'smākaṃ suparyāptaḥ*: "it is death enough for us".
* 1.209.8: the brahmin's point is that "a hundred years" is a fixed term, so the curse will end.
* 1.209.11 *nārītīrthāni*: "the Women's Bathing Places".
* 1.209.24 *rājānaṃ babhruvāhanam*: the text calls the child "King" Babhruvahana, by what he
  would become.
