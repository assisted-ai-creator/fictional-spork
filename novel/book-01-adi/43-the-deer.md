# The Deer

"You have told me the birth of Dhritarashtra's sons," said Janamejaya, "which
was beyond the human, and came from a seer, best of those who know the Veda. And
I have heard their names from you, one by one. Now tell me of the Pandavas. You
said yourself, when you told of the parts of the gods coming down to earth, that
all of them, great men with the courage of the king of the gods, were portions
of the gods. So I want to hear the whole story of the births of those men whose
deeds were more than human. Tell it, Vaishampayana."

King Pandu was in a great forest, full of deer and beasts of prey. There he saw
the leader of a herd of deer, mating. Then Pandu shot the doe and the stag with
five sharp, swift arrows, with golden shafts and fine feathers.

That stag, king, was the son of a seer, a man of great splendour, rich in
austerity. He had been coupling with his wife in the form of a deer. Still
joined to the doe, he fell at once to the ground. His senses reeling, he cried
out in a human voice.

"Even men who are ruled by desire and anger," he said, "who have lost their
reason and delight in wrongdoing, keep away from cruelty. Wisdom does not
swallow fate. Fate swallows wisdom. Wisdom cannot understand what fate brings
about. You were born, Bharata, in the first of families, whose men always kept
to dharma. How did your mind go astray, overcome by desire and greed?"

"The way kings behave in killing enemies," said Pandu, "is the way laid down for
killing deer. You should not blame me, deer, out of folly. Kings are allowed to
kill deer openly, without trickery. That is the dharma of kings. You know it, so
why do you blame me? The seer Agastya, while he was holding a sacrifice, went
hunting. He consecrated the wild deer of the great forest to all the gods. Why
do you blame us for a dharma proven by that authority? By Agastya's spell your
fat was offered into the fire."

"Men did not let fly their arrows at enemies beforehand," said the deer. "It is
when enemies are open to attack that the time for killing them is most praised."

"Men kill with force, by every means, with sharp arrows," said Pandu, "whether
the beast is heedless or watchful, in the open. Why do you blame me, deer?"

"I do not blame you, king, for killing deer for my own sake," said the deer.
"But you should have been kind, and waited for me to finish mating. What wise
man would kill a deer mating in the forest, at a time that is good for all
beings, and that all beings long for? You have made fruitless the lovely fruit
of a man's purpose. It does not become you, Kaurava, born in the line of the
Pauravas and of the seers, whose deeds were never harsh. This was a cruel act, a
very great one, blamed by all the world. It does not lead to heaven or to fame,
and it is utterly against dharma, Bharata. You know the delights of women very
well, and you know the truth of the books of dharma and wealth. You are like a
god. You should not do a thing like this, which does not lead to heaven. It is
you, best of kings, who should restrain men who do cruel deeds and act wickedly,
men who have forsaken the three aims of life.

"What have you gained, best of men, by killing me, who did you no wrong? I am a
sage who lives on roots and fruit, wearing the form of a deer. I live always in
the forest, and I am devoted to calm. You have harmed me, so the same will
surely happen to you. You have been cruel to a pair of lovers, helpless and
blinded by desire. The moment of love will bring the end of your life in the
same way.

"I am a sage named Kindama, unmatched in austerity. I was ashamed to do this
before men, so I was mating with a doe. I roam the deep forest as a deer among
deer. It will not be the killing of a brahmin for you, because you did not know.
You killed me in a deer's form, when I was blinded by desire. But for this,
fool, you will reap the same fruit. When you lie with the woman you love,
blinded by desire, you too will go to the world of the dead in that state. And
the woman you lie with at the end, best of the wise, she will follow you out of
love, when you have fallen into the power of the king of the dead, whom no being
can escape. I was happy, and you brought me grief. In the same way, when you are
happy, grief will come to you."

When he had said this, in great pain, the deer parted from life. And at once
Pandu was stricken with grief.

---

The king and his wives stepped away from the dead deer, and he mourned for him
as if he had been one of his own kin. In his pain and sorrow he lamented.

"Men born even into families of good people come to grief by what they do, when
they have not mastered themselves and are trapped in the net of desire. My
father was born of a man who always kept to dharma, and he died while still a
boy, a slave of desire, so we have heard. In the field of that king, the slave
of desire, the blessed Krishna Dvaipayana himself, a seer who governs his
speech, fathered me. And now this low thought has come to me in my trouble. I
have been wicked, lost in hunting, and the gods have forsaken me for my folly.

"I will set my mind on freedom, for bondage is a great calamity. I will follow
the lasting way of my father. I will give myself up wholly to austerity. So I
will wander this earth alone, spending each day under a single tree, a shaven
sage, living on alms. I will be covered in dust. I will shelter in empty houses
or live at the foot of a tree. I will give up everything, loved and unloved. I
will neither grieve nor rejoice. Blame and praise will be the same to me. I will
have no hopes, bow to no one, be free of the pairs of opposites, and own
nothing. I will laugh at no one, and frown at no one. My face will always be
calm, and I will delight in the good of all beings. I will harm nothing of the
four kinds, moving or still. I will be the same to all living things, as if they
were my own children.

"I will beg once a day at seven houses. If there are no alms, I will go without
food. I will take a little at a time, as much as is fit to eat, and sometimes
only from the first house. When I am given something I will go no further, and
when I am given nothing I will finish the round of seven. If one man hacks at
one of my arms with an adze, and another anoints the other with sandal paste, I
will wish neither of them ill or well. I will not act like one who wants to
live, nor like one who wants to die. I will neither welcome death nor hate life.
I will pass beyond every rite a living man may perform to prosper, and stay in
the blink of an eye and the moments like it. In every state I will give up the
work of all my senses. I will let go of dharma itself, and wash my soul clean of
its stain. I will be free of every sin, and past every snare. I will be in no
one's power, like the wind. Living always in this way, I will keep my body
alive, set on the road without fear.

"I will not delight in the way of a dog, without courage, fit only for the
wretched, always cut off from my own dharma. The man who, honoured or
dishonoured, looks wretchedly for another way of life, a slave to desire, walks
the way of dogs."

When the king had said this, in great pain, sighing deeply, he looked at Kunti
and at Madri and spoke to them. "Kausalya, Vidura the steward, the king with our
kin, noble Satyavati, Bhishma, the royal family priests, the great brahmins who
drink soma and keep strict vows, and the elders of the city who live there under
our protection: make your peace with them all and tell them that Pandu has gone
to the forest as a wandering ascetic."

Kunti and Madri heard the words of their husband, whose mind was made up to live
in the forest. And they answered him with words to match.

"There are other stages of life, bull of the Bharatas, that you can live in,
with the two of us, your wives by dharma, practising great austerity. You
yourself will surely be the guide even to heaven. We will master our senses, set
on our husband's world, and give up desire and pleasure, and we too will
practise great austerity. If you leave us, great and wise lord of the people, we
will give up our lives this very day. There is no doubt of it."

"If this is your firm resolve, and it is in keeping with dharma," said Pandu,
"then I will follow my father's own lasting way. I will give up the comforts and
ways of the village. I will practise great austerity, wear bark and live on
fruit and roots, and wander the great forest. I will offer to the fire at dawn
and at dusk, and bathe at both times. I will grow thin, eating little, wearing
rags, skins and matted hair. I will bear cold, wind and sun, hunger, thirst and
weariness, and wither this body with austerity that is hard to bear. I will live
alone and reflect, living on what is ripe and what is unripe. I will satisfy the
ancestors and the gods with the food of the forest, with words and with water. I
will never do anything unkind to a forest-dweller, let alone to a
village-dweller, or even to one who lives with his family. So I will stay,
seeking out the fiercest and fiercer rule of the forest books, until this body
comes to its end."

When the king, born in the Kaurava line, had said this to his two wives, Pandu
gave away to brahmins his crest jewel, his gold necklace, his armlets and
earrings, his costly clothes and his wives' ornaments, everything. And he said
again, "Go to the City of the Elephant and tell them: Pandu has gone to the
forest as a wandering ascetic."

Then the bull of the Kurus set out with his wives, giving up everything: wealth,
desire, pleasure, and the highest delight. His followers and servants heard the
lion of the Bharatas' many pitiful words. They raised a terrible cry of grief
and wailed, "Alas! Alas!" Shedding hot tears, they left the king, and went
quickly to the City of the Elephant with all his message. When Dhritarashtra,
best of men, heard from them all that had happened in the great forest, he
grieved for Pandu.

But the Kaurava prince Pandu, living on roots and fruit, went with his two wives
to the mountain Nagasabha. He reached Chaitraratha, passed beyond Varishena,
crossed the Himalaya, and went on to Gandhamadana. Guarded by great beings,
siddhas and great seers, the king lived there, on level ground and rough. He
came to the lake of Indradyumna, passed beyond Hamsakuta, and on Shatashringa,
great king, he became an ascetic.

**Divergences.**

* **The curse includes Madri's death.** Kindama says that the woman Pandu lies with
  at the end will follow him in death (1.109.29). The CE sets up here what happens
  at 1.116.
* The vulgate adds a passage in which the deer praises the shamelessness of animals'
  mating (\*1163). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 1.109.10 *na vidhiṃ grasate prajñā*: "wisdom does not swallow fate; fate swallows
  wisdom". Literal.
* 1.109.14–15: Agastya's hunt during a sacrifice, and the deer's fat "offered by
  Agastya's rite", are Pandu's precedent.
* 1.109.16: the deer's reply is compressed and its point is unclear. It seems to say that one does not shoot at an enemy "beforehand" (*purā*), and that the praised time is when he is "open" (*randhra*). Rendered literally.
* 1.109.19 *puruṣārthaphalaṃ kāntam*: "the lovely fruit of a man's purpose", i.e.
  offspring.
* 1.109.23 *trivarga*: "the three aims", dharma, wealth and desire.
* 1.109.27: the killing is not *brahmahatyā*, the killing of a brahmin, "because you
  did not know".
* 1.110.6 *pitur*: "my father's" lasting way. Here the father must be Vyasa, the
  ascetic who fathered him (1.110.4), not Vichitravirya.
* 1.110.3: Pandu's legal father is Vichitravirya, who "died while still a boy, a
  slave of desire" (1.96.57).
* 1.110.7 *ekaikasmin vanaspatau*: "at one tree each", i.e. never two nights under
  the same tree.
* 1.110.12–13: the rules of begging are compressed and obscure in the Sanskrit. "Seven houses" follows *kulāni dve ca pañca ca*, "two and five houses"; "finish the round of seven" follows *alābhe sapta pūrayan*.
* 1.110.16 *nimeṣādiṣv avasthitaḥ*: "abiding in the blink of an eye and the like", i.e.
  living moment to moment.
* 1.110.17 *saṃparityaktadharmātmā*: "having given up dharma itself", the renunciate's
  going beyond even ritual merit.
* 1.110.18 *sadharmā mātariśvanaḥ*: "of the same nature as the wind".
* 1.110.23 *kṣattā*: Vidura's title, "the steward".
* 1.110.42–45: the route (Nagasabha, Chaitraratha, Varishena, Gandhamadana, the lake
  of Indradyumna, Hamsakuta, Shatashringa) is given in the text's order.
