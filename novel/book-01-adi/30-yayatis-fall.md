# Yayati's Fall

With Puru's youth, Yayati, the son of Nahusha, the best of kings, enjoyed the
pleasures he loved, full of joy. He enjoyed them as he wished and as his spirit
moved him, at the right times and happily, never against dharma, as was fitting
for him. He satisfied the gods with sacrifices and the forefathers with the
rites for the dead. He satisfied the poor with kindness and the best of brahmins
with the gifts they wanted, and guests with food and drink. He cared for the
vaishyas by protecting them and for the shudras by gentleness, and he held
robbers in check. Pleasing all his people rightly by dharma, Yayati protected
them like a second Indra in the flesh. That king, with the courage of a lion,
young, enjoying the world, took the highest happiness, without going against
dharma.

When he had enjoyed those fine pleasures, the lord of the earth was satisfied,
and weary, and he remembered the term of a thousand years. He knew how to reckon
time, and he counted the moments and the instants. When he saw that the time was
full, he spoke to his son Puru. "As I wished, as my spirit moved me, at the
right times, tamer of enemies, I have enjoyed the world with your youth, my son.
Puru, I am pleased with you, bless you. Take back your youth, and take this
kingdom too, for you are the son who has done what I love."

Then King Yayati, the son of Nahusha, took back his old age, and Puru took back
his own youth again.

The king wanted to consecrate his youngest son, Puru. But the classes, with the
brahmins at their head, said to him, "Lord, how can you pass over the eldest,
Yadu, Shukra's grandson, Devayani's son, and give the kingdom to Puru? Yadu is
your eldest son. After him Turvasu was born. Sharmishtha's son is Druhyu, then
Anu, and then Puru. How can the youngest have the kingdom over the heads of the
elder ones? We remind you of this. Keep to dharma."

"Let all the classes, with the brahmins at their head, hear what I have to say,"
said Yayati, "why the kingdom must not be given to the eldest, in any way. My
eldest, Yadu, did not do what I asked. A son who goes against his father is not
counted a son by the good. The son who does what his mother and father tell him,
who is good and helpful to them, who behaves like a son to his father and
mother, is a son. Yadu scorned me, and so did Turvasu, and Druhyu and Anu
scorned me deeply. Puru did what I said, and honoured me above all. The youngest
is my heir, for he carried my old age. Puru, in the form of a son, did what I
desired. And Shukra himself, Kavya Ushanas, gave me a boon: 'The son who follows
you will be king, lord of the earth.' So I ask you: let Puru be consecrated as
king."

"The son who has every virtue," said the people, "and is always good to his
mother and father, deserves every blessing, lord, even if he is the youngest.
Puru deserves this kingdom, since he is the son who has done what you love. And
because of Shukra's boon, nothing can be said against it."

When the people of the city and the country, content, had said this, the son of
Nahusha consecrated his own son Puru in his kingdom. When he had given the
kingdom to Puru, the king was consecrated for life in the forest, and he left
the city with brahmins and ascetics.

From Yadu came the Yadavas, and from Turvasu the Yavanas. Druhyu's sons were the
Bhojas, and from Anu came the barbarian tribes. From Puru came the Paurava line,
in which you were born, king, to rule this kingdom, master of yourself, for a
thousand years.

---

So King Yayati, the son of Nahusha, consecrated the son he wanted as king, and,
glad, he became a sage who lived in the forest. He lived in the forest with
brahmins, eating fruit and roots, and he held himself in check. From here he
went to heaven. He went to that home of the gods and lived there happily,
content. And not very long after, Shakra threw him down again. As he fell from
heaven, he did not reach the surface of the earth. He stayed in the air. So I
have heard. And then, it is told, he went to heaven again from there, the mighty
one, with King Vasumat, with Ashtaka, Pratardana and Shibi, meeting them, it is
said, in an assembly.

"By what deed did that lord of the earth reach heaven again?" said Janamejaya.
"I want to hear all of this, truly, in full, told by you, brahmin, in the
presence of these brahmins and seers. Yayati, the lord of the earth, was like
the king of the gods, the one who made the Kuru line grow, as bright as fire. I
want to hear all the life of that great one, of wide fame and true glory, both
in heaven and here."

Then I will tell you the later story of Yayati, in heaven and here, which is
holy and destroys all sins.

King Yayati, the son of Nahusha, consecrated his youngest son Puru as king, and
went off gladly to the forest. He placed his sons, with Yadu first, in the lands
at the borders, and the king lived a long time in the forest, eating fruit and
roots. Master of himself, his anger conquered, he satisfied the forefathers and
the gods and poured offerings into the fires by the rule, as the rules for a
forest dweller lay down. The lord honoured his guests with the offerings of the
forest, living by gleaning grain, and he ate only what was left.

The king lived like this for a full thousand years. For thirty years he lived on
water, his speech and his mind held in check. Then, tireless, he lived on air
for a year. For a year the king practised austerity in the middle of five fires.
For six months he stood on one foot, living on air. Then, his fame holy, he went
to heaven, filling heaven and earth.

---

The king of kings lived in heaven, in the home of the gods, honoured by the
thirty gods, the Sadhyas, the Maruts and the Vasus. He went from the world of
the gods to the world of Brahma, a man of holy deeds, master of himself. The
lord of the earth lived there a long time, so it is told.

Once Yayati, the best of kings, went to Shakra. At the end of their talk Shakra
asked the lord of the earth a question. "When Puru took your old age and went
about the earth in your form, king, and you gave him the kingdom, what did you
tell him? Tell me truly."

"I told him," said Yayati, "'All the land between the Ganga and the Yamuna is
yours. You are king of the middle of the earth. Your brothers are lords of the
lands at the edges. The man without anger is better than the angry. The patient
man is better than the impatient. Men are better than beings who are not men,
and the learned better than the unlearned.

"'When he is abused, a man should not abuse back. The anger of the patient man
burns up the one who abuses him, and he takes the other's merit. He should not
wound, nor speak cruelly. He should not take the highest knowledge from a low
man. He should not speak the harsh words that make others tremble, words that
lead to evil worlds. Know that the man who wounds, who speaks roughly and stings
men with the thorns of his words, is the unluckiest of men. He carries
destruction bound in his mouth.

"'Let him be honoured by the good in front of him, and guarded by the good
behind him. Let him always bear the insults of the bad, and let him take on the
conduct of the good, for his own conduct is noble. The arrows of words fly from
the mouth, and the man they strike grieves night and day. They fall on the
tender places of others, so a wise man should not loose them at anyone. There is
no charm in all three worlds like kindness to all beings, and giving, and sweet
speech. So always speak gently, and never harshly anywhere. Honour those who
should be honoured. Give, and never beg.'"

"You finished all your works, king," said Indra, "and left your home and went to
the forest. So I ask you, son of Nahusha: to whom are you equal in austerity,
Yayati?"

"Among gods and men," said Yayati, "among gandharvas and great seers, I see no
one equal to me in austerity, Vasava."

"Because you have despised your equals, your betters and your inferiors," said
Indra, "not knowing their power, these worlds of yours have an end. Your merit
is spent, and today you will fall, king."

"If my worlds are spent, Shakra, because I despised the gods, the seers, the
gandharvas and men," said Yayati, "then let me fall among the good, king of the
gods, when I am cast out of the world of the gods."

"You will fall near the good, king," said Indra, "and when you have fallen, you
will find a footing again. But know this, Yayati, and never again despise your
equals or your betters."

---

Then Yayati left the holy worlds loved by the king of the immortals. As he was
falling, Ashtaka saw him, the best of royal seers, the guardian of the rules of
true dharma, and he said,

"Who are you, young, as handsome as Vasava, blazing with your own splendour like
a fire? You are falling from the sky, from the darkness of the rising clouds,
like the sun, the best of all that move in the sky. When we saw you falling from
the path of the sun, as bright as fire and the sun, beyond measure, we all
wondered, *What is this that falls?* and we were bewildered. Then we saw you
standing on the path of the gods, with the power of Shakra, the sun or Vishnu.
So we all came up to you today, wanting to know the truth of your fall.

"We do not dare to be the first to ask you, and you do not ask us who we are. So
we ask you, whose form is so lovely: whose are you, and why have you come? Let
your fear go, and your sorrow and bewilderment. Let them go quickly, you who are
like the king of the gods. For while you are near the good, not even Shakra, the
slayer of Bala, can overpower you. The good are always the refuge of those who
have fallen from happiness, you who are like the king of the immortals. They
have come together here, the lords of all that moves and all that does not. You
stand among the good, your equals. Fire is master in burning, and the earth in
sowing. The sun is master in shining, and a guest is master among the good."

"I am Yayati, the son of Nahusha," he said, "Puru's father. Because I despised
all beings, I was thrown out of the world of the gods, the perfected ones and
the seers. My merit is small, and I am falling. I am older than you in years,
and so I do not greet you first. Among the twice-born, the one who is older in
knowledge, in austerity or in birth is honoured."

"You said that the one who is older in years is the elder, king," said Ashtaka.
"That is not so at all. The one who is learned, and old in years as well, is the
one the twice-born honour."

"They say that wrong goes against good deeds," said Yayati, "and that it lives
in the proud and leads to evil worlds. The good do not follow the bad in this,
for their own hearts speak for the good. I had great wealth. However I strive, I
will not win it back. The man who understands this, and lives set on his own
good, knows how to live.

"There are many kinds of beings in the world of the living. They depend on fate.
Their efforts and their rights are lost. Whatever comes to him, the steady man
should not be shaken by it, knowing in his own mind that fate is the stronger. A
creature finds happiness or sorrow by fate, not by his own power. So, thinking
fate the stronger, he should never burn with grief, and never exult. He should
not burn in sorrow nor exult in happiness, but should always live evenly,
steady, thinking fate the stronger, never burning with grief and never exulting.

"I am never bewildered by fear, Ashtaka. I have no pain in my mind. For I think:
*As the Maker shapes me in the world, so I will surely be.* Think of the
creatures born of sweat, of eggs and of sprouts, the creeping things, the worms
and the fish in the water, and the stones, grass and wood. When their fate is
spent, all of them go back to their own nature. Knowing that happiness and
sorrow do not last, why should I be in pain, Ashtaka? What should I do, and what
can I do to escape pain? So I keep pain away, and stay watchful."

"Tell me the chief worlds you have enjoyed, lord of kings," said Ashtaka, "and
how long, and how. Tell me all of it, as it was, king. You speak of dharma like
one who knows the field."

"I was a king here," said Yayati, "ruling the whole earth. Then I won great
worlds, and I lived there for a thousand years. Then I went on to a higher
world. Then I lived in the lovely city of Indra of many invocations, with its
thousand gates, a hundred yojanas wide, for a thousand years. Then I went on to
a higher world. Then I reached the heavenly, ageless world of the lord of
creatures, the lord of the world, which is hard to reach. I lived there a
thousand years. Then I went on to a higher world.

"I lived in the home of the god of gods as I wished, having won those worlds,
honoured by all the thirty gods, equal in power and splendour to the lords. And
I lived in Nandana, taking any form I wished, for a million years, playing with
the apsarases, seeing the lovely trees in flower, with their holy scents.

"I stayed there, attached to the pleasures of the gods. When a very long time
had passed, a messenger of the gods, terrible to look at, cried out to me. Three
times he cried it, with a long, drawn-out voice: 'Fall!'

"That is all I know, lion among kings. Then I fell from Nandana, my merit spent.
And I heard the voices of the gods in the air, grieving for me in pity, lord of
men: 'Alas, Yayati's merit is spent. He falls, though he did holy deeds and his
fame was holy.' As I fell, I said to them, 'How may I fall among the good?' They
showed me your place of sacrifice. When I saw it, I came here quickly. I took
the scent of the offerings of the place of sacrifice as my guide, and the trail
of the smoke, and I was glad."
