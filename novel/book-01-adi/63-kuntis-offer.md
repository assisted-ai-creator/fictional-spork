# Kunti's Offer

When she heard her grieving parents' words, so full of pain, their daughter, her
whole body stricken with grief, spoke to them.

"Why are you weeping so bitterly, like people with no one to protect them?
Listen to me, and when you have heard me, do what is right. By dharma, there is
no doubt that you should give me up. Give up the one who has to be given up, and
save everyone by losing me alone. People want children for this reason,
thinking, *She will carry me across.* Now that the time has come, cross over by
me, as if on a raft. A son carries his father across, here out of trouble or
after death. He carries him across in every way, and so the wise call him a son,
*putra*. And grandfathers always long for their daughters' sons too. I will save
them myself, by saving my father's life.

"This brother of mine is a child. If you go to the other world, he will surely
die before long. And if my father has gone to heaven and my little brother has
died, the offerings to the ancestors will stop, and that would displease them.
Left by my father, my mother and my brother, I would surely pass from sorrow to
greater sorrow and die, where I do not belong. But if you are safe and well, my
mother and my little brother, the family line and the offerings will surely
stand.

"A son is one's own self, a wife is a friend, but a daughter, they say, is a
trouble. Free yourself from trouble, then, and join me to dharma. Without you,
father, I will be a helpless, pitiful girl with no protector, going anywhere at
all. Or else I will set this family free. I will do a very hard deed and win its
fruit. Or if you go there, best of brahmins, and leave me, I will suffer. Think
of me too. So, for our sake, for dharma's sake and for the sake of your
children, save yourself, good father, and give me up, for I am the one to be
given up. Do not let the time pass for what must be done. The water you offer
for me will do me good.

"What could be worse than this, that when you have gone to heaven we should run
about like dogs, begging food from strangers? But if you and your kin are saved
from this trouble, safe and well, I shall live happily in the world of the
immortals."

When they heard her lament like this in so many ways, her father, her mother and
the girl herself all wept, the three of them.

Then, hearing them all weeping, their son, a little boy, opened his eyes wide
and said in his babbling voice, "Don't cry, father. Don't cry, mother. Don't
cry, sister." And he went up to each of them in turn, almost laughing. Then he
picked up a blade of grass and said happily, "I will kill the man-eating
rakshasa with this!"

Though they were overcome with grief, when they heard the child's babbling words
a great joy came over them.

*Now is the time*, Kunti thought. She came up to them and spoke, bringing the
dying back to life, as if with nectar.

---

"What is the root of this sorrow?" said Kunti. "I want to know the truth of it.
When I know, I will take it away, if it can be taken away."

"What you say is worthy of the good, rich in austerity," said the brahmin. "But
no human being can take this sorrow away. Near this town lives a rakshasa, Baka,
the mighty lord of this country and this town. He is fattened on human flesh,
the evil-minded man-eater. The mighty lord of the asuras always protects this
land. With a rakshasa's strength he guards the town and the country. Because of
him we have no fear of enemy armies or of other creatures.

"His wage is fixed: a cartload of rice as his food, two buffaloes, and one man
who takes them to him. Each man in turn gives him that meal. The turn comes
round after many years, and men cannot escape it. And any who ever try to get
free of it, the rakshasa kills and eats, with their sons and wives. The king
lives in Vetrakiyagriha. He does not follow the policy that would keep his
people safe for ever. We surely deserve this, living always in fear in the
country of a weak king, sheltering under a bad king. Whom should brahmins obey?
In whose country should they live as they like? They go where good qualities
draw them, like birds that fly where they please. A man should first find a
king, then a wife, then wealth. When he has gathered those three, he should
support his kin and his sons. I gathered all three the wrong way round, and now
that we have fallen into this danger, we suffer terribly.

"Now that turn, which will destroy my family, has come to us. I must give his
wage: the food, and one man. I have no money to buy a man anywhere. And I cannot
give up my friends or kin. I see no way to escape the rakshasa. I am drowning in
a great sea of sorrow that cannot be crossed. So today I will go to the rakshasa
with all these, my family, and then the wretch will eat us all together."

---

"You must not despair at all because of this danger," said Kunti. "I see a way
to free you from the rakshasa. You have only one son, a little boy, and one
daughter, poor girl. I do not think they should go there, nor your wife. I have
five sons, brahmin. One of them will go, taking the offering for that wicked
rakshasa for your sake."

"I will never do this to save my life," said the brahmin. "I will never let a
brahmin, a guest, lose his life for my sake. This is not done even among the
low-born and the wicked, that one should give up oneself and even one's son for
a brahmin's sake. I think I must look to my own good. Between killing a brahmin
and killing myself, it is better for me to kill myself. Killing a brahmin is the
worst of sins. There is no atonement for it. Even if I did it without meaning
to, it would be better for me to kill myself. I do not want death for myself,
good lady. But if others kill me, no sin will touch me. If I consented to the
death of a brahmin, I see no atonement for it. It would be cruel and base. To
abandon one who has come to one's house, one who has come for shelter, or to
kill one who asks for help, is held to be the cruellest thing. The great men of
old, who knew the dharma for times of danger, knew that one should never do a
deed that is condemned, never a cruel one. It is better that I myself should die
today with my wife. I will never consent to the death of a brahmin."

"I too hold firmly that brahmins must be protected, brahmin," said Kunti. "And
no son of mine is unloved, even if I had a hundred. But that rakshasa cannot
kill my son. My son is mighty, perfected in mantras, and full of splendour. He
will take all that food to the rakshasa, and he will free himself. I am sure of
it. The hero has met rakshasas before, mighty and huge, and has killed many of
them. But you must not tell anyone about this, brahmin, at all. People who want
learning would pester my sons out of curiosity. And the good hold that if my son
taught this knowledge to anyone without his teacher's leave, it would do that
man no good."

When Pritha said this, the brahmin and his wife were overjoyed, and they
honoured her words, which were like nectar. Then Kunti and the brahmin together
said to the son of the Wind, "Do it." And he said to them, "So be it."

**Divergences.**

* **Baka's cave and the thirteen years.** The vulgate adds that Baka lives in a cave
  two miles away on the Yamuna, and that he has troubled the town for thirteen years
  (\*1626–\*1627). The CE says only that he lives "near this town" (1.148.3).

**Choices.**

* 1.147.5 *putra*: the daughter's etymology, a son is one who "carries across"
  (*tārayet*). The vulgate's familiar etymology from the hell *put* is \*1622.
* 1.147.11 *kṛcchraṃ tu duhitā kila*: "but a daughter, they say, is a trouble". Her
  words.
* 1.147.21 *kalam avyaktam*: "a sweet, indistinct babble".
* 1.148.4 *asurarāṭ*: Baka is called "lord of the asuras".
* 1.148.6 *śālivāhasya bhojanam*: "a cartload of rice as food".
* 1.148.9 *vetrakīyagṛhe*: the king's seat. The name is given as a place.
* 1.148.12: the brahmin's maxim on king, wife and wealth, "in that order", is his.
* 1.149.14 *mantrasiddhaḥ*: "perfected in mantras". Kunti's claim about Bhima.
* 1.149.17–18: Kunti's reason for secrecy is given as she says it.
* 1.149.20 *anilātmajam*: Bhima, "son of the Wind".
