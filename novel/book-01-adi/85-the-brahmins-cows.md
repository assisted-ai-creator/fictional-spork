# The Brahmin's Cows

When they had made that agreement, the Pandavas lived there, bringing other
kings under their power by the might of their weapons. Krishna was obedient to
all five Parthas, those lions among men of boundless energy. She was very happy
with those five heroes as her husbands, and they with her, like the Sarasvati
with the elephants. While the great Pandavas behaved according to dharma, all
the Kurus prospered, free of faults and full of happiness.

After a long time, lord of the people, some thieves stole the cows of a certain
brahmin, best of kings. As his wealth was being carried off, the brahmin came to
Khandavaprastha, beside himself with anger, and cried out to the Pandavas.

"Pandavas! My cows are being taken from your country by force, by low, cruel,
unbridled men. Run after them! When the brahmin is careless, the crows steal his
offering. A base jackal is pawing at the empty den of a tiger. A brahmin's
property has been taken by thieves, and dharma and wealth are destroyed, and I
am weeping. Take up your weapons!"

The Pandava Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, was near the weeping brahmin, and he heard
these words. As soon as he heard them, the strong-armed man said to the brahmin,
"Do not be afraid."

But the room where the great Pandavas kept their weapons was the room where the
Dharma King Yudhishthira was sitting with Krishna. The Pandava could neither go
in nor go away, while the words of the distressed man urged him on, again and
again. As the crying went on, Kunti's son thought about it in distress.

*This ascetic brahmin's wealth is being stolen, and I must wipe away his tears.
That is certain. If I do not protect him today, as he weeps at the door, the
king will be guilty of a great wrong, the wrong of looking on and doing nothing.
And the world will lose its faith in our power to protect, and we shall be in
the wrong. If I go without asking the king's leave, there is no doubt I will
displease King Ajatashatru, and myself too. But if I go in on the king, I must
live in the forest. Let me face a great wrong, or death in the forest. Dharma
counts for more, even than the loss of one's body.*

When he had made up his mind, Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, went in on the king and
took his leave, lord of the people. He took up his bow, and said gladly to the
brahmin, "Come quickly, brahmin, while those low men who covet what is not
theirs have not gone far. Let us go together, and I will bring back your wealth
from the thieves' hands today."

The strong-armed man followed them, with his bow and armour, in his chariot,
with his banner flying. He scattered the thieves with his arrows, defeated them,
and won back the cows. The Pandava gave them back to the brahmin, and, having
drunk his fill of glory, the hero Savyasachin, the tamer of enemies, came back
to the city.

He greeted all his elders, and they welcomed him in turn. Then he said to the
Dharma King, "Give me my vow. I have broken our agreement by seeing you. I will
go and live in the forest, for that is the agreement we made."

When he heard these unwelcome words so suddenly, the Dharma King Yudhishthira
was stricken with grief. "How can that be?" he said, in a faltering voice, to
his brother Gudakesha, the unfallen. "If I am any authority for you, listen to
what I say, sinless one. Whatever you did that displeased me when you came in,
hero, I forgive it all, and there is no grudge in my heart. When the younger
brother comes in on the elder, it is no offence. It is when the elder comes in
on the younger that the rule is broken. Turn back, strong-armed one. Do as I
say. You have not broken dharma, and you have not wronged me."

"I have heard from you," said Arjuna, "that one should not practise dharma by
evasion. I will not swerve from the truth. I take up my weapon by the truth."

Then, taking leave of the king, he took the vow of the celibate student and set
out to live in the forest for twelve years.

---

As he went, many great brahmins who had mastered the Veda followed the
strong-armed man, who brought glory to the Kauravas. There were scholars of the
Veda and its limbs, and thinkers on the self, and pure devotees of the Lord, and
bards who knew the old stories. There were storytellers too, king, and wandering
ascetics who lived in the forest, and brahmins who recited heavenly tales in
sweet voices. Surrounded by these and many other companions, with their graceful
talk, the joy of the Pandavas went on his way, like Indra with the Maruts.

He saw lovely and varied forests and lakes, rivers and seas and lands, Bharata,
and holy bathing places, bull of the Bharatas. When he came to Gangadvara, the
lord made his camp there.

Hear from me, Janamejaya, the wonderful thing that he did there, that
pure-souled man, the best of the Pandu warriors. When Kunti's son and the
brahmins had settled there, Bharata, the brahmins lit many sacred fires. When
the fires had been kindled and were blazing and fed with offerings, and flowers
had been offered, and the fires set on the far banks, Gangadvara shone brightly,
king, with those great men, learned and self-controlled, who kept to the good
path, fresh from their bathing.

When the camp was full like this, the joy of the Pandavas went down into the
Ganga to bathe. He bathed there and offered water to his forefathers. Then, as
he was about to climb out of the water to tend the fire, king, the strong-armed
man was pulled down under the water by Ulupi, the daughter of the king of the
snakes, who desired him.

There the Pandava saw a fire, well laid, in the richly adorned house of the
snake Kauravya. Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, performed the fire rite there. He made
his offering without a qualm, and the fire was pleased. When he had done the
fire rite, Kunti's son smiled and said to the daughter of the snake king, "What
is this rash thing you have done, timid lady? What is this lovely place, and who
are you, lovely one, and whose daughter?"

"There is a snake named Kauravya, born in the line of Airavata," said Ulupi. "I
am his daughter, Partha, the snake woman Ulupi. When I saw you go down into the
river that runs to the sea to bathe, Kaunteya, I was overcome with love. I am
tormented by love for you, joy of the Kurus, and I have no one else. Make me
happy today by giving yourself to me in private."

"Good lady," said Arjuna, "this is my twelve years of celibacy, and the Dharma
King has laid it on me. I am not my own master. Yet I want to please you too,
you who move in the water. I have never told a lie in my life. So how can I do
what you want without its being a lie, snake woman, and without harm to my
dharma?"

"I know, Pandava," said Ulupi, "why you are wandering the earth, and how your
elder laid this celibacy on you. You made an agreement among yourselves about
Drupada's daughter: whichever of you in his folly came in on another would live
in the forest as a celibate for twelve years. So this banishment of one another
was made for Draupadi's sake, for the sake of dharma. Here, dharma is not
harmed. And those in distress must be rescued, large-eyed one. If you rescue me,
your dharma is not lost. And even if there were some small breach of this
dharma, it would still be dharma for you to have given me my life, Arjuna.

"Love me, Partha, for I love you. Good men hold this to be right, lord. If you
will not do it, know that I am dead. Practise the highest dharma, strong-armed
one, by giving me my life. I have come to you for shelter today, best of men.
You always protect the wretched and the helpless, Kaunteya. I come to you for
shelter, and I weep in my misery. I beg you, because I love you. So do what
pleases me. You should fulfil my wish by giving yourself to me."

When the daughter of the lord of the snakes said this, Kunti's son did all that
she asked, for the sake of dharma. The mighty man spent that night in the
snake's house, and when the sun rose, he rose and left Kauravya's house.

**Divergences.**

* **No son born here.** The vulgate adds that Arjuna fathered Iravan on Ulupi that night
  (\*2025), and has Ulupi grant him a boon that he cannot be beaten in the water (\*2029). In the
  CE's Adi Parva no child is born. Iravan's birth is told in Book 6 (6.86.6–8), where his mother
  is "the daughter of the snake king", a childless widow given to Arjuna by Airavata, and she
  is not named. See ledger L-103.
* The vulgate has Ulupi say the brothers' rule was "one year divided into five parts" at
  Narada's order (\*2023). The CE has only the rule of 1.204.28. See ledger L-16.
* The vulgate has Arjuna cover his face with his upper cloth as he goes in (\*2017). Not in the CE.

  text's.
* 1.205.8: the brahmin's two proverbs (crows and the offering, the jackal in the tiger's den)
  are his.
* 1.205.14 *upaprekṣaṇajo 'dharmaḥ*: "the wrong of looking on and doing nothing".
* 1.205.25 *sahasā*: "so suddenly".
* 1.205.27: Yudhishthira's argument that only the elder's intrusion on the younger breaks the
  rule is his.
