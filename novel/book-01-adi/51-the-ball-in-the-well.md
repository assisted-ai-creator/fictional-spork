# The Ball in the Well

Then the mighty son of Bharadvaja came to Drupada and said to Prishata's son,
"Know that I am your friend, king."

"Brahmin," said Drupada, "your understanding is unformed, and hardly fitting,
when you boldly tell me, 'I am your friend.' Kings in high places never make
friends, fool, with men like you, who have no fortune and have lost their
wealth. Friendships too wear out with time, as men wear out. There was
friendship between us once, but it was bound by our being equals. No friendship
is ever seen in the world that does not grow old. Desire pulls it apart, and
anger cuts it down. Do not cling to a friendship that has worn out. Make a new
one. Our friendship, best of brahmins, was tied to what each of us needed.

"A poor man is no friend of a rich man, nor an unlearned man of a learned one,
nor a coward of a hero. Why do you want a friendship that is past? Friendship
and marriage are between two whose wealth is equal and whose family is equal,
not between the well-fed and the starving. A man who does not know the Veda is
no friend of one who does, nor a man without a chariot of a chariot fighter. A
man who is not a king is no friend of a king. Why do you want a friendship that
is past?"

When Drupada said this to him, the mighty son of Bharadvaja thought for a
moment, flooded with anger. He made up his mind about the Panchala, and the wise
man went to the city of the Kuru chiefs, named after the elephant.

---

The princes had come out of the City of the Elephant together, and the heroes
were wandering about happily, playing with a ball. As they played, the ball fell
into a well. They could find no way to get it back.

Then Drona saw the princes at a loss. The mighty man smiled a little, and spoke
to them with a clever smile. "Shame on your kshatriya strength! Shame on your
skill with weapons! You are born in the line of Bharata, and you cannot get your
ball back. Here is a handful of reeds. I have charged it with the power of a
weapon. See its power, which nothing else has. I will pierce the ball with one
reed, and that reed with another, and that one with another. By joining them
together I will get hold of the ball."

The princes watched, their eyes wide with wonder. And when they saw the ball
drawn out, they said to the man who had pierced it, "We bow to you, brahmin. No
one else can do this. Who are you? How should we know you? What can we do for
you?"

"Describe me to Bhishma," said Drona, "my looks and my qualities. He is very
wise. He will know me at once."

"So be it," they all said, and they told Bhishma, their grandfather, the
brahmin's words, and what he had done, so out of the ordinary. When Bhishma
heard the princes, he knew it was Drona. *He is a fitting teacher*, he thought.

So Bhishma, the best of those who bear arms, brought him in himself, and
honoured him well. Then he asked him carefully why he had come, and Drona told
him everything.

"Long ago, Bhishma, I went to the great seer Agniveshya for weapons, to learn
archery, unfailing one. I lived there a very long time, a humble student with
matted hair, keeping the vow of celibacy, for many years, to master archery. The
son of the king of the Panchalas, the mighty Yajnasena, studied with me, working
hard for our teacher and paying close attention. He was my friend there. He
helped me, and I loved him. I spent a long time with him and was happy, alas. We
studied together from childhood, Kaurava.

"He came to me there, doing me kindnesses and speaking kind words, and said
something to me that made my love grow, Bhishma. 'Drona, I am my great father's
dearest son. When the king of the Panchalas consecrates me as king, the kingdom
will be yours to enjoy, friend. I swear it to you on the truth. My pleasures, my
wealth and my happiness will be yours to command.'

"When he had said this, I went away, having mastered the weapons, wanting
wealth. When I heard that he had been consecrated, I thought, *I have got what I
wanted.* I went back to my dear friend on his throne, full of joy, remembering
our time together and what he had said. I came to Drupada, lord, and, as an old
friend, I said to him, 'Know that I am your friend, tiger among men.'

"When I came up to him like a friend, Drupada laughed at me as if I were
nothing, and he said, 'Brahmin, your understanding is unformed, and hardly
fitting, when you boldly tell me, "I am your friend." Kings in high places never
make friends, fool, with men like you, who have no fortune and have lost their
wealth. A man who does not know the Veda is no friend of one who does, nor a man
without a chariot of a chariot fighter. A man who is not a king is no friend of
a king. Why do you want a friendship that is past?'

"When Drupada said this to me, I was flooded with anger. So I came to the Kurus,
Bhishma, looking for pupils of good quality."

Bhishma accepted him as teacher, together with Pandu's sons. He brought all his
grandsons, and wealth of many kinds, and gave them to Drona as his pupils, king,
with the proper rites. And the great archer accepted the Kauravas as his pupils.

When he had accepted them all, and they had come to him as pupils, Drona spoke
to them alone, in private, with confidence. "There is a task I long for in my
heart. When you have mastered your weapons, you must do it for me. Promise me
this truly, sinless ones."

When they heard this, the Kauravas fell silent, lord of the people. But Arjuna,
the tamer of enemies, promised it all. Then Drona kissed Arjuna on the head,
again and again. He embraced him lovingly and wept for joy.

Then mighty Drona taught Pandu's sons weapons of many kinds, divine and human.
Other princes too came together to Drona, the best of brahmins, to learn
weapons, bull of the Bharatas: the Vrishnis and the Andhakas, and kings from
many lands. And Radha's son, the son of the suta, came to Drona as his teacher.
But the suta's son was very resentful, and he rivalled Partha. He took shelter
with Duryodhana, and he looked down on the Pandavas.

**Divergences.**

* **No ring, no milk.** The vulgate adds Drona's ring, which he drops in the dry
  well and draws out with an arrow (\*1365, \*1368), and, in his story to Bhishma, his
  poverty and the child Ashvatthama given flour and water to drink in place of milk
  (App. I No. 75; G 1.133). Neither is in the CE. See ledger L-82.
* **Karna is Drona's pupil.** The CE says Karna "came to Drona as his teacher"
  (1.122.47). Drona does not turn him away. What Drona later refuses him is the
  Brahma weapon only (12.2.9–13). See ledger L-83.
* **Where Drona lived.** The vulgate has Drona live quietly in Kripa's house
  (\*1356–\*1357). The CE does not say.

**The CE's own doublet.**

* Drupada's speech is given twice, first as it happens (1.122.2–9) and then as Drona
  retells it to Bhishma (1.122.35–37). The novel keeps both, the second shorter, as in
  the text.

**Choices.**

* 1.122.4 *sāmarthyabandhanam*: "bound by ability", i.e. by their being equal then.
* 1.122.6 *arthanibandhanam*: "tied to purpose", "to need".
* 1.122.12 *vīṭā*: a small ball or a piece of wood used in a game. Rendered "ball".
* 1.122.14 *paiśalyāt*: "cleverly", "with a knowing air".
* 1.122.26 *yajñasena*: Drupada's other name.
* 1.122.41 *kṛtopasadanān*: "who had formally come to him as pupils".
* 1.122.42: Drona does not name the task here. It is the capture of Drupada
  (1.128).
* 1.122.44 *mūrdhni samāghrāya*: "smelling his head", the traditional kiss of an
  elder.
