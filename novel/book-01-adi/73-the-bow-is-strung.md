# The Bow Is Strung

"Duryodhana, Durvishaha, Durmukha, Dushpradharshana," said Dhrishtadyumna,
"Vivimshati, Vikarna, Saha and Duhshasana; Yuyutsu, Vatavega, Bhimavegadhara,
Ugrayudha, Balaki, Kanakayu and Virochana; Sukundala, Chitrasena, Suvarchas,
Kanakadhvaja, Nandaka, Bahushali, Kundaja and Vikata. These and many other
mighty sons of Dhritarashtra have come for you with Karna, heroes, a hundred of
them, great-hearted, famous bulls among kshatriyas.

"Shakuni, Bala, Vrishaka and Brihadbala, all the sons of the king of Gandhara,
have come. Ashvatthama and Bhoja, the best of all who carry weapons, have come
together for you, great-hearted, finely adorned.

"Brihanta, Maniman, mighty Dandadhara, Sahadeva, Jayatsena and Meghasandhi of
Magadha. Virata with his two sons, Shankha and Uttara. Vardhakshemi, Suvarchas
and King Senabindu. Abhibhu with his son, the glorious Sudama. Sumitra,
Sukumara, Vrika and Satyadhriti. Suryadhvaja, Rochamana, Nila and Chitrayudha,
Amshuman, Chekitana, and mighty Shreniman. Samudrasena's son, the valiant
Chandrasena. Jalasandha, and the father and son Sudanda and Danda. Paundraka
Vasudeva, and mighty Bhagadatta. The king of Kalinga, the king of Tamralipta,
and the lord of Pattana. The king of Madra, Shalya, the great warrior, with his
sons, the hero Rukmangada and Rukmaratha. Somadatta of the Kuru line, and his
three sons, great warriors and heroes, Bhuri, Bhurishravas and Shala. Sudakshina
of Kamboja, and Dridhadhanva of the Kuru line. Brihadbala, Sushena, and Shibi,
Ushinara's son.

"Samkarshana, Vasudeva, and Rukmini's mighty son. Samba, Charudeshna, Sarana and
Gada. Akrura, Satyaki, and mighty Uddhava. Kritavarma, Hridika's son, Prithu and
Viprithu. Viduratha, Kanka, Samika, Saramejaya, the hero Vatapati, Jhilli and
Pindaraka, and valiant Ushinara. These are named as the Vrishnis.

"Bhagiratha, Brihatkshatra, and Jayadratha of Sindhu. Brihadratha, Bahlika, and
Shrutayu, the great warrior. King Uluka, son of the gambler, Chitrangada and
Shubhangada, wise Vatsaraja, and the lord of Kosala.

"These and many other lords of many lands have come for you, good lady,
kshatriyas famous on earth. These valiant men will shoot at the fine target for
you. Choose today, lovely one, the man who hits this target."

---

The young men, adorned, wearing earrings, came together, vying with one another.
Each thought that weapons and strength were in himself, and they all sprang up
in their pride. Proud of their beauty, their courage, their families, their
dharma and their youth, they were like the lordly elephants of the Himalaya in
rut, bursting with passion. Eyeing one another as rivals, their bodies flooded
with desire, they rose quickly from the kings' seats, saying, "Krishna is mine!"
The kshatriyas gathered in the arena, each wanting to win Drupada's daughter,
shone like the hosts of the gods gathered round Uma, daughter of the king of the
mountains. Their bodies pierced by the arrows of the god of love, their hearts
gone to Krishna, the kings came down into the arena for Drupada's daughter, and
there even friends became enemies.

Then the hosts of the gods came in their flying chariots: the Rudras and
Adityas, the Vasus and the two Ashvins, all the Sadhyas and the Maruts, with
Yama and the lord of wealth in front. The daityas came, and the great birds, the
great snakes, the divine seers, the guhyakas and the charanas, Vishvavasu,
Narada and Parvata, and the chief gandharvas with the apsarases.

Balarama was there, and Keshava, with the leading men of the Vrishnis and
Andhakas. Those bulls of the Yadus watched, and they held to Krishna's view.

Then the hero of the Yadus saw the five Parthas. They were like mighty elephants
in rut, like lordly elephants with their foreheads marked for battle, like fires
covered with ash. He thought about them, and he pointed out to Rama
Yudhishthira, Bhima, Jishnu and the twin heroes. Rama looked at them slowly, one
by one, and then looked at Janardana with a glad heart.

The other kings, the sons and grandsons of many kings, their eyes, minds and
hearts gone to Krishna, strained to look at her as she moved about, biting their
lips, their faces red. So too the Parthas, strong-armed, and the twin heroes,
the great-spirited pair, all looked at Draupadi then, and all of them were
struck by the arrows of the god of love.

The place was crowded with divine seers and gandharvas, visited by the great
birds, the snakes, the asuras and the siddhas. It was filled with a heavenly
scent, and heavenly garlands were scattered over it. The sky was full of the
great roar of kettledrums. It was thronged on every side with flying chariots,
and it rang with the sound of flutes, lutes and drums.

---

Then the companies of kings, one after another, tried their strength for
Krishna. But they could not string that tough bow, even with all their might. As
they strained at it, the stiff bow sprang back and threw the lords of men. They
were seen writhing on the ground, wretched, their hearts broken. The stiff bow
had crushed and broken their armlets and earrings. Crying out, their hopes of
Krishna gone, the circle of kings was in distress.

When all the people in the gathering were in turmoil, and the lords of men had
given up their boasting, the hero Jishnu, Kunti's son, wanted to string that bow
and fit the arrows to it.

---

When the kings had turned away from stringing the bow, generous Jishnu rose from
among the brahmins. When they saw Partha setting out, bright as Indra's banner,
the chief brahmins shouted and waved their deerskins. Some of them were
downcast, and some were glad.

Some who were shrewd and lived by their wits said to one another, "Brahmins, the
bow that Karna, Shalya and the other kings could not bend, men famous in the
world, strong, and devoted to the science of the bow, how can a mere lad string
it, untrained in weapons and much weaker in strength? The brahmins will be
laughed at before all the kings if this task, taken on in rashness and without
thought, is not carried through. If he has set out to draw the bow out of pride,
or in excitement, or from a brahmin's rashness, he should be stopped. It would
be good if he did not go. Then we will not be laughed at, we will not be made
light of, and we will not earn the hatred of the lords of the earth."

Others said, "He is young and handsome, like the trunk of a lord of elephants,
with thick shoulders, thighs and arms, and as steady as the Himalaya. This deed
is possible for him. You can tell from his spirit. He has power, and great
spirit. A man with no power would not go up of his own accord. And there is
nothing that happens in the worlds, among all that move and all that stay, that
brahmins cannot do. Brahmins who live on water, who live on air, who eat fruit,
firm in their vows, are strong though they are weak, for brahmins have the power
of Brahman. A brahmin should not be despised, whether he does good or bad,
whether the task that comes to him is pleasant or painful, great or small."

While the brahmins were talking like this, saying many things, Arjuna stood by
the bow, as unmoving as a mountain. He walked round the bow, keeping it on his
right. He bowed his head, and joyfully the tamer of enemies took it up. In the
blink of an eye he strung it. He took the five arrows and shot the target, and
the target fell through the opening to the ground, pierced through at once.

Then there was a noise in the sky, and a great uproar in the middle of the
gathering. The god rained heavenly flowers on the head of Partha, the slayer of
his enemies. People waved their cloths, and there were shouts on every side, and
showers of flowers fell from the sky all round. The musicians played on
instruments of a hundred kinds, and companies of bards and heralds sang praises
there in fine voices.

When Drupada, the destroyer of his enemies, saw him, he was delighted, and he
wanted to help Partha with his army.

While the great noise was going on, Yudhishthira, the best of those who uphold
dharma, went quickly back to their lodging with the twins, the best of men.

When she saw the target pierced, and when she saw Partha, like Shakra, Krishna
took the white garland of choice and went to Kunti's son, smiling.

He took her, having won her in the arena. Honoured by the brahmins, the man of
unimaginable deeds went out of the arena, and his wife followed him.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi does not reject Karna.** In the popular story, and in the vulgate, Karna strings the
  bow and draws it at the target, and Draupadi cries out, "I will not choose a suta", so he
  throws the bow down with a bitter laugh (\*1827, in Ganguli at G 1.189). Other rejected lines
  have the bow fling Karna back when he has it within a hair's breadth of being strung (\*1830,
  \*1834). The CE has none of this. Karna comes with the Kauravas (1.177.4). He is not
  singled out among the kings who fail (1.178.15–17), but the brahmins speak of the bow "that
  Karna, Shalya and the other kings could not bend" (1.179.4). See ledger L-95.
* **No Shishupala or Jarasandha in the list.** The vulgate adds Shishupala, Jarasandha, Karna
  with his son Vrishasena, and the kings of the Pandyas, Keralas and Cholas (\*1816, \*1819,
  \*1821). The CE's list has none of them. Its "Jalasandha" (1.177.11) is not Jarasandha of
  Magadha.
* **Krishna does not compete.** The Yadus watch and hold to Krishna's view (1.178.8). The
  vulgate adds Krishna pressing Balarama's hand in joy (\*1835).
* The vulgate has Yudhishthira signal Arjuna to rise (\*1836), Arjuna ask Dhrishtadyumna whether
  brahmins may try (\*1845), and Arjuna think of Shiva and Krishna before lifting the bow
  (\*1846). None of these are in the CE.
* **The garland.** The CE says only that Draupadi took the white garland and went to Arjuna,
  smiling (1.179.22). The vulgate has her place it round his neck (\*1850, \*1852).

**Choices.**

* 1.177.1 *saho … samaḥ*: Saha is taken as a name, as in Ganguli. *samaḥ*, "equal", is read as
  an epithet and dropped.
* 1.177.12 *pauṇḍrako vāsudevaś ca*: read as one king, Paundraka Vasudeva. Ganguli takes it as
  two names.
* 1.177.16 *raukmiṇeyaḥ*: "Rukmini's son", left unnamed as in the text.
* 1.177.18 *vīro vātapatiḥ*: "the hero Vatapati".
* 1.177.20 *ulūkaḥ kaitavo rājā*: King Uluka, "son of the gambler", that is, Shakuni's son.
* 1.178.3 *kṛṣṇā mamaiṣā*: "Krishna is mine!" Here Krishna is Draupadi, as in the text.
* 1.178.9 *pañcābhipadmān*: elephants with the marks (*padma*) painted on their foreheads.
* 1.178.16: the bow "springing back" (*sphuratā*) threw them. The text gives no more detail.
* 1.179.5 *baṭumātreṇa*: "a mere lad", a brahmin student.
* 1.179.16 *daśārdhasaṃkhyān*: "numbering half of ten", five arrows.
* 1.179.18 *celāvedhān*: the waving of cloths.
* 1.179.22 *utsmayantī*: "smiling".
