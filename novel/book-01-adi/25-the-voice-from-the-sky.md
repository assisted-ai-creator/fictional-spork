# The Voice from the Sky

When Dushyanta had given his word and gone, Shakuntala, the lovely one, bore a
boy of boundless power. It was when three full years had passed, Janamejaya: the
son of Dushyanta, blazing like fire, full of beauty, generosity and virtue.
Kanva, the best of those who do holy deeds, performed the rite of birth and the
other rites for the wise boy as he grew.

His teeth were white and pointed, and his body was built like a lion's. His
hands were marked with the wheel, his head was large, and he was glorious and
very strong. The boy, shining like a child of the gods, grew quickly there. When
he was only six years old, the strong child caught tigers, lions and boars,
elephants and buffaloes, and tied them to the trees all round Kanva's hermitage.
He rode them and tamed them, and ran about playing with them.

So the people who lived in Kanva's hermitage gave him a name. "Let him be
Sarvadamana, the Tamer of All," they said, "for he tames everything." So the boy
became Sarvadamana, full of courage, force and strength.

The seer saw the boy and his more than human deeds, and he said to Shakuntala,
"It is time for him to be crown prince." Kanva knew the boy's strength, and he
said to his pupils, "Take Shakuntala, with her son, from this hermitage to her
husband quickly, today. She has every good mark and is honoured by all. It is
not good for women to live long among their own kin. It destroys their good
name, their conduct and their dharma. So take her without delay."

"So be it," they all said, and those men of boundless power set out for the city
named for the elephant, with Shakuntala and her son ahead of them. The fair
woman took her lotus-eyed son, who shone like a child of the immortals, and came
from the forest where Dushyanta had known her. She came to the king and was
announced, and she was brought in with her son, who was as bright as the rising
sun.

She greeted him properly, and Shakuntala said to him, "This is your son, king.
Let him be consecrated as crown prince. For this son, like a god, was born to me
by you, king. Keep the agreement you made about him, best of men. Remember the
agreement you made, fortunate one, when we came together in Kanva's hermitage."

When he heard her words, the king remembered. And yet he said, "I do not
remember. Whose woman are you, wicked ascetic? I do not remember any bond with
you, of dharma, love or wealth. Go or stay as you please. Do what you like."

When he said this, the lovely woman of spirit stood as if ashamed, as if stunned
with pain, as still as a post. Her eyes grew red with fury and indignation, and
her lips trembled. She looked at the king sideways, as if she would burn him
with her glances. She hid what she felt, though anger drove her, and she held in
the fire she had stored up by austerity. For a moment she thought, full of grief
and indignation. Then she looked straight at her husband and spoke in anger.

---

"You know, great king. Why do you speak like this? 'I do not know', you say,
freely, like any common man. Your heart knows what is true and what is false
here. You are the witness, good sir. Do not despise yourself. A man who is one
thing and makes himself out to be another: what evil has that thief not done,
who has robbed himself of himself?

"You think, *I am alone.* You do not know the ancient sage who lives in the
heart. He knows the evil deed, and it is in front of him that you do wrong. A
man who does evil thinks, *No one knows me.* But the gods know him, and so does
the person within him. The sun and the moon, the wind and the fire, the sky, the
earth and the waters, the heart and Yama, the day and the night and both
twilights, and Dharma: they all know what a man does. Yama, the son of Vivasvat,
takes away the evil deeds of the man with whom the knower of the field is
pleased, the witness of deeds who lives in the heart. But the wicked man with
whom he is not pleased, Yama makes that evildoer pay for his evil deeds. The
gods do not favour a man who despises himself and makes himself out to be other
than he is, for even his own self does not stand for him.

"Do not despise me because I came to you of my own accord. I am a faithful wife.
You do not honour me, though I deserve the guest offering, your own wife who has
come to you. Why do you look down on me in the assembly like a common woman? I
am not crying in an empty place. Do you not hear me? If you will not do what I
ask you, Dushyanta, your head will split into a hundred pieces today.

"A husband enters his wife, and he is born again from her. That is why the poets
of old knew that a wife is called *jaya*, the one in whom a man is born. The
child born to a man who knows the sacred tradition saves by its line his
forefathers who have died before. Because a son saves his father from the hell
called *Put*, the Self-born himself called him *putra*, the son.

"She is a wife who is skilled in the house. She is a wife who bears children.
She is a wife whose husband is her life. She is a wife who is faithful to her
husband. A wife is half a man. A wife is the best of friends. A wife is the root
of dharma, wealth and love. A wife is a friend to the dying. Men with wives
perform the rites. Men with wives are householders. Men with wives are glad. Men
with wives have good fortune. Sweet-spoken wives are friends in lonely places.
They are fathers in the duties of dharma, and mothers to the suffering. Even in
the wilderness, a wife is a rest to a man on the road. A man with a wife is
trusted. So wives are the highest refuge.

"When a husband has died and passes through the world, going alone through hard
places, only his wife, if she is faithful, follows him always. If the wife dies
first, she waits for her husband after death. If the husband dies first, the
good wife follows him afterwards. For this reason, king, marriage is sought, so
that the husband has his wife in this world and in the next.

"The wise say that a son is the self, born of the self. So a man should look on
his wife, the mother of his son, as his mother. When the father sees the son
born in his wife, like his own face in a mirror, he is glad, like a man of good
deeds who has reached heaven. Men who burn with the pains of the mind, or suffer
with sickness, are glad in their wives, as men who suffer from the heat are glad
in water. A wise man should never say a harsh word to women, even when he is
very angry, seeing that love, joy and dharma depend on them. Women are the
eternal, holy field of a man's own birth. What power do even the seers have to
make children without women?

"When a son runs up, covered in the dust of the earth, and hugs his father's
limbs, what could be better than that? Here is your son, who has come to you of
his own accord, looking at you longingly out of the corner of his eye. Why do
you scorn him? Even ants carry their own eggs and do not break them. How can you
not support your own son, when you know dharma? The touch of clothes, of women,
of water, is not as sweet as the touch of your own little son when you hold him
in your arms. The brahmin is the best of two-footed beings, the cow the best of
four-footed ones. The teacher is the best of those to be honoured, and a son the
best of all things to touch. Let this lovely son hold you and touch you. There
is no touch in the world sweeter than the touch of a son.

"I bore this boy, tamer of enemies, when three full years had passed, king of
kings, to take away your sorrow. 'He will perform a hundred horse sacrifices,
Paurava.' So a voice from the sky said to me long ago, when I was lying in after
his birth.

"Surely men who go to another village take their sons on their laps with love,
and smell their heads, and rejoice in them. The twice-born, as you know too,
speak this verse from the Vedas at the birth rites of their sons: 'From limb
after limb you come into being. From the heart you are born. You are the self
that is called a son. Live a hundred autumns. My life depends on you, and my
line that never ends. So live, my child, in great happiness, a hundred autumns.'

"This boy was born from your limbs, a second man from a man. Look at your son,
like your second self in a clear pool. As the offering fire is carried from the
household fire, so this boy was born from you. You were one, and you have become
two.

"When you were hunting, king, led away by a deer, you came upon me, a girl in my
father's hermitage. Urvashi, Purvachitti, Sahajanya and Menaka, Vishvachi and
Ghritachi are the six finest apsarases. Of them the one called Menaka, sprung
from Brahma, the finest of the apsarases, came down from heaven to earth and
bore me to Vishvamitra. That apsaras Menaka bore me on a slope of the Himalaya,
and she threw me away and went, the heartless woman, as if I were someone else's
child. What evil did I do in a former life, that I was abandoned by my family as
a child, and now by you? If you cast me off, I will go back to the hermitage.
But you should not cast off this child, your own son."

"I do not know this son that was born to you, Shakuntala," said Dushyanta.
"Women are liars. Who will believe what you say? Your mother Menaka is a
heartless wanton, who threw you away on a slope of the Himalaya like a used
garland. And your father, born of a kshatriya womb, the heartless Vishvamitra,
greedy for the rank of a brahmin, is a slave to lust. Menaka is the finest of
the apsarases, and your father one of the great seers. How can you, their child,
talk like a wanton? Are you not ashamed to tell such an unbelievable story, and
to me above all? Go away, wicked ascetic! Where is the great seer, always
fierce, and where is that apsaras Menaka, and where are you, so poor, dressed as
an ascetic woman? And your son is too big. He is a child, and yet so strong. How
has he shot up in so short a time, like the trunk of a sal tree? Your birth is
very low. You look like a wanton to me. Menaka bore you by chance, out of lust.
All that you say is beyond my knowledge, ascetic. I do not know you. Go where
you like."

---

"King," said Shakuntala, "you see the faults of others, though they are as small
as mustard seeds. Your own, as big as bilva fruits, you see and do not see.
Menaka is among the thirty gods, and the thirty gods follow Menaka. My birth is
higher than yours, Dushyanta. You walk on the earth, king. I move through the
sky. See the difference between us, like Meru and a mustard seed. I go to the
palaces of great Indra, of Kubera, of Yama and of Varuna. See my power, king.

"There is a true saying I will tell you, sinless one, as an example, not out of
hatred. When you have heard it, you should forgive me. As long as an ugly man
does not see his face in a mirror, he thinks himself more handsome than others.
But when he sees his ugly face in the mirror, then he knows the difference
between himself and others. A man who is very handsome despises no one. A man
who talks too much, and speaks ill, becomes a nuisance here. A fool who hears
men talking, of good things and bad, takes the bad words, as a pig takes dung. A
wise man who hears men talking, of good things and bad, takes the good words, as
a goose takes the milk from the water. As a good man grieves when he speaks ill
of others, so a wicked man is glad when he speaks ill of others. As the good
find happiness when they bow to their elders, so the fool is happy when he
abuses the good. Those who do not look for faults live happily. The fools look
for faults. Where the good should be spoken of by others, such men call others
what they are themselves. And there is nothing more laughable in the world than
when a wicked man calls a good man wicked.

"From a man who has fallen from truth and dharma, even an unbeliever shrinks, as
from an angry snake. How much more does a believer? When a man has fathered a
son like himself and then despises him, the gods destroy his fortune, and he
does not reach the worlds he hoped for. The forefathers called a son the
foundation of the family and the line, the highest of all dharmas. So one should
not abandon a son. Manu named five kinds of son: those born of one's own wife,
those received, those bought, those raised, and those fathered on other women.
Sons bring men dharma and fame, they gladden the heart, and once born, they save
their forefathers from hell, rafts of dharma. So, tiger among kings, you should
not abandon your son. Guard yourself, and truth, and dharma, lord of the earth.
Lion among kings, you should not carry deceit.

"One pond is better than a hundred wells. One sacrifice is better than a hundred
ponds. One son is better than a hundred sacrifices. Truth is better than a
hundred sons. A thousand horse sacrifices and truth were weighed in the balance,
and truth outweighed the thousand horse sacrifices. The study of all the Vedas,
and bathing at all the holy fords, may or may not be equal to speaking the
truth, king. There is no dharma higher than truth, and nothing greater than
truth. Nothing here is harsher than falsehood. Truth, king, is the highest
Brahman. Truth is the highest promise. Do not abandon your promise, king. Let
truth be your companion.

"If you cling to falsehood, and if you have no faith in yourself, then I will
go. There can be no bond with a man like you. Even without you, Dushyanta, my
son will rule this earth to its four ends, whose crown is the king of the
mountains."

When she had said this much, Shakuntala turned to go. Then a voice without a
body spoke from the sky to Dushyanta, as he sat surrounded by his priests, his
family priest, his teachers and his ministers.

"The mother is only the bellows. The son belongs to the father. The one who
fathers him, he is that one. Support your son, Dushyanta. Do not despise
Shakuntala. The man who places the seed raises his son up from the house of
Yama, god among men. You placed this child in the womb. Shakuntala speaks the
truth. A wife bears the son, a part of oneself split in two. So support your
son, Dushyanta, the son of Shakuntala, king. It is misfortune indeed for a
living man to abandon his living son. Support the great-souled son of Shakuntala
and Dushyanta, Paurava. Because you must support him, *bhartavya*, and at our
word too, let this son of yours be named Bharata."

When the Paurava king heard what the dwellers of heaven had said, he was full of
joy, and he said to his family priest and his ministers, "Listen, all of you, to
what this messenger of the gods has said. I myself know that he is my son. If I
had taken this boy as my son on her word alone, people would have doubted, and
he would not be pure."

Then the king was glad and joyful, now that the messenger of the gods had proved
the boy pure, Bharata, and he took his son. He smelled his head and held him
lovingly, honoured by the brahmins and praised by the bards, and he felt the
great joy that comes from the touch of a son.

And the king, who knew dharma, honoured his wife as dharma required. He spoke
gently to her. "Our union was made out of the sight of the world. So I thought
this over, queen, to prove you pure. People would think that you came together
with me only as a woman does, and this son has been chosen for the kingdom. So I
thought it over. And the harsh things you said to me in your anger, my dear,
loving me as you do, wide-eyed one, lovely one, I have forgiven you."

When he had said this to his dear queen, the royal seer Dushyanta honoured her
with clothes, food and drink, Bharata. Then King Dushyanta named Shakuntala's
son Bharata, and consecrated him as crown prince.

The famous wheel of that great soul began to roll, shining, heavenly,
unconquered, great, and loud through the worlds. He conquered the kings of the
earth and made them subject to him. He practised the dharma of the good and won
unequalled fame. That mighty king was an emperor who ruled the whole earth, and
he offered many sacrifices, like Shakra, the lord of the Maruts. Kanva was his
priest, and he gave rich gifts, as Daksha did. The glorious king performed the
horse sacrifice called Govitata, at which Bharata gave Kanva a thousand padmas.

From Bharata comes the fame of the Bharatas. By him this family is the Bharata
family. Those who came after, and those who came before, are known as the
Bharatas. In the line of Bharata there were many great kings, like gods and like
Brahma, of great splendour. Their names cannot be counted. But I will tell you
the chief of them, Bharata, those greatly fortunate ones, like gods, devoted to
truth and honesty.

**Divergences.**

* **No curse, no ring.** In the CE Dushyanta "remembered, and yet he said, 'I do
  not remember'" (1.68.18, *smarann api*). He later says he waited for public
  proof for her sake and the boy's (1.69.35–41). Durvasa's curse, the ring and
  the fish that swallowed it are from Kalidasa's play. See ledger **L-68**.

**Choices.**

* 1.68.2 *triṣu varṣeṣu pūrṇeṣu*: the boy is born "when three full years had
  passed". Shakuntala repeats it at 1.68.58. The text says it, and the novel keeps
  it.
* 1.68.18 *smarann api*: "though remembering". This one word makes his denial a
  lie, and the novel keeps it plain.
* 1.68.30 *kṣetrajña*: "the knower of the field", the witness in the heart.
* 1.68.36–38: the word-plays on *jāyā* (the wife, in whom a man is born) and
  *putra* (the one who saves from the hell *put*) are explained in the sentence.
* 1.68.62–63: the birth-rite verse is given whole.
* 1.68.68 *brahmayoniḥ*: Menaka, "sprung from Brahma".
* 1.69.2–4: Shakuntala claims a higher birth through Menaka. Her words are kept.
* 1.69.18: Manu's five kinds of son are listed as she lists them.
* 1.69.21: the famous ladder of worth, from wells to truth, is given whole.
* 1.69.33 *bhartavya … bharata*: the name is explained from "to be supported".
* 1.69.48 *govitatam*: the name of the horse sacrifice. *Padma*: a very large
  number, here a gift of a thousand padmas (of wealth). Kept as the word.
