# Shakuntala

Then the strong-armed king went on alone, sending his ministers away. He did not
see the seer of strict vows in the hermitage. When he did not see the seer, and
saw that the hermitage was empty, he called out loudly, as if to make the forest
ring: "Who is here?"

When she heard his voice, a girl came out of the hermitage, like the goddess of
fortune in bodily form, dressed as a woman ascetic. As soon as the dark-eyed
girl saw King Dushyanta, she said at once, "Welcome." She honoured him with a
seat, with water for his feet and the guest offering, and she asked after the
king's health and welfare, king. When she had honoured him properly and asked
after his health, she said, almost smiling, "What can I do for you?"

The king had been honoured as was right. He saw that the girl was faultless in
every limb, and he said to her, as she spoke so sweetly, "I have come to wait on
the fortunate seer Kanva. Where has the blessed one gone, good lady? Tell me,
lovely one."

"My father, the blessed one, has gone out of the hermitage to fetch fruit," said
Shakuntala. "Wait a moment, and you will see him when he comes."

The king did not see the seer, and she had told him this. He looked at her,
lovely and glorious, with her charming smile, shining with beauty, austerity and
self-control, full of youth and grace, and the lord of the earth said, "Who are
you, and whose, you with the lovely hips? Why have you come to the forest? With
such beauty and such virtue, where do you come from, lovely one? From the moment
I saw you, you have stolen my heart. I want to know who you are. Tell me, lovely
one."

When the king said this to her in the hermitage, the girl laughed, and answered
in the sweetest words. "I am held to be the daughter of the blessed Kanva,
Dushyanta: the famous ascetic, who is steadfast and knows dharma."

"The fortunate one, the blessed one honoured by the world, keeps his seed," said
Dushyanta. "Dharma itself might stray from its course, but he of the strict vows
would not stray. How could you, so beautiful, be his daughter? I have a great
doubt about this. You must cut it away."

"Hear, king," said Shakuntala, "how I came to know this, and how it happened
long ago, and how I am the sage's daughter. A seer came here once, and asked
about my birth. Hear, lord of the earth, what the blessed one told him.

---

"'Long ago, they say, Vishvamitra was practising great austerity, and he
tormented Shakra, the lord of the hosts of the gods, very much. *His power is
blazing with austerity*, Indra, the breaker of cities, thought in fear. *He may
throw me from my place.* So he said to Menaka,

"'"Menaka, you are the first of the apsarases in heavenly gifts. Do me a
kindness, lovely one. Hear what I have to tell you. Vishvamitra, a great
ascetic, as bright as the sun, is practising terrible austerity, and he makes my
heart shake. Vishvamitra is your task, Menaka, you of the slender waist. His
spirit is sharpened, he is very hard to overcome, and he is deep in fierce
austerity. So that he does not throw me from my place, go to him and tempt him.
Break his austerity. Do me this great kindness. Tempt him with your beauty and
youth, your sweetness, your movements, your smiles and your words, lovely one,
and turn him from his austerity."

"'"That blessed one is full of power," said Menaka, "and always a great ascetic,
and quick to anger. You know him too, blessed one. You yourself are afraid of
the splendour, the austerity and the anger of that great soul. How could I not
be afraid? He is the one who took the fortunate Vasishtha's beloved sons from
him. He was born a kshatriya, and he made himself a brahmin by force. For his
bathing he made a river, hard to cross because of its many waters, the holiest
in the world, which people know as the Kaushiki. There, long ago, in a hard
time, the righteous royal seer Matanga, who had become a hunter, supported that
great soul's wife. When the famine was over, the sage came back to his
hermitage, and then the lord named the river Para. There, glad at heart, he
himself made Matanga sacrifice, and you, lord of the gods, went to drink the
soma out of fear of him. In his anger he made new lines of stars, a rival wealth
of stars, beginning with Shravana.

"'"These are his deeds, and I am very much afraid of him. Tell me what to do,
lord, so that he does not burn me in his anger. He could burn up the worlds with
his splendour. He could shake the earth with his foot. He could shrink great
Meru and spin it round in a moment. How could a girl like me touch a man like
that, joined to austerity, blazing like fire, the master of his senses? His
mouth is blazing fire, his eyes are the sun and the moon and the stars, his
tongue is Time, best of the gods. How could a girl like me touch him? Yama,
Soma, the great seers, the Sadhyas, the Vishvadevas and all the Valakhilyas fear
his power. Why should a woman like me not fear him?

"'"But you have told me to go, lord of the gods. How could I not go near the
seer? Only think of how to protect me, king of the gods, so that I can move
about safely for your sake. Let the Wind lay bare my clothes as I play there,
god. And by your grace, let Manmatha, the god of love, be my helper in the task.
And let a sweet wind blow from the forest when I tempt the seer."

"'Indra said, "So be it," and he arranged it. Then she went to the hermitage of
the Kaushika.

"'Shakra gave his orders to the wind, which is always moving, and Menaka set
out, when the time came, with the Wind. The timid, lovely Menaka saw Vishvamitra
in his hermitage, practising austerity, his sins burned away by it. She greeted
him, and then she began to play near the seer. And the Wind carried off her
garment, white as the moon. She ran to the ground quickly, reaching after the
garment, smiling shyly, it seemed, at the Wind, that lovely woman.

"'The best of sages saw Menaka bare, flustered, eager for her garment, her youth
and beauty beyond words. When that bull among brahmins saw her beauty and her
charm, he set his heart on lying with her, overcome by desire. He invited her,
and the faultless woman was willing. The two of them spent a very long time
there in the forest, taking their pleasure as they wished, and it seemed to them
like a single day.

"'The sage fathered Shakuntala on Menaka, on a lovely slope of the Himalaya,
beside the river Malini. Menaka left the newborn child on the bank of the
Malini, and, her task done, she went quickly back to Shakra's court.

"'Birds saw the baby lying in the lonely forest, full of lions and tigers, and
they gathered round her on every side. So that the flesh-eaters, greedy for
meat, would not harm the little girl in the forest, the birds, the *shakuntas*,
guarded Menaka's daughter there.

"'I went there to bathe, and I saw her lying in the lonely wild forest, with
birds all around her. I brought her home and took her as my daughter. The one
who makes the body, the one who gives life, and the one whose food we eat: these
three, in that order, are called fathers in the rules of dharma. Because she was
guarded by *shakuntas* in the lonely forest, I named her Shakuntala. Know, my
friend, that Shakuntala is my daughter, and the faultless Shakuntala thinks of
me as her father.'

"That is what he told the great seer when he was asked about my birth. So know
that I am Kanva's daughter, lord of men. I think of Kanva as my father, for I do
not know my own. That is what I have told you, king, as it happened and as I
heard it."

---

"It is plain that you are a king's daughter, from what you say, lovely one,"
said Dushyanta. "Be my wife, you of the lovely hips. Tell me what I can do for
you. Today I will bring you golden garlands, clothes, earrings of fine gold,
bright jewels and gems from many cities, golden coins and skins. Let my whole
kingdom be yours today. Be my wife, lovely one. Come to me, timid one, by the
gandharva marriage, beautiful one. For of all marriages, you whose thighs are
like the plantain, the gandharva is said to be the best."

"My father has gone out from this hermitage to gather fruit, king," said
Shakuntala. "Wait a moment for him. He will give me to you."

"I want you, lovely one, faultless one, to be willing," said Dushyanta. "Know
that I am here for your sake, for my heart has gone to you. The self is its own
kin. The self is the only refuge of the self. You may give yourself, by dharma.

"In short, eight kinds of marriage are remembered as lawful: the brahma, the
daiva, the arsha, the prajapatya, the asura, the gandharva, the rakshasa, and
the paishacha is remembered as the eighth. Manu, the son of the Self-born, told
the dharma of each in order. Know that the first four are praised for a brahmin,
and, faultless one, that six in order are lawful for a kshatriya. For kings the
rakshasa is also allowed, and the asura is remembered for vaishyas and shudras.
Of the last five, three are lawful and two unlawful. The paishacha and the asura
must never be done. This is the rule to follow. This is remembered as the way of
dharma. The gandharva and the rakshasa are both lawful for a kshatriya. Do not
fear it. They may be done singly or together. There is no doubt of it. So,
lovely one, you desire me as I desire you, and you should become my wife by the
gandharva marriage."

"If this is the path of dharma," said Shakuntala, "and if I am my own mistress
to give myself, then hear my condition, best of the Pauravas, my lord. Promise
me truly what I tell you here in private. The son born to me must be crown
prince after you, great king. Tell me this truly. If this is so, Dushyanta, let
me be joined with you."

"So be it," the king answered her, without stopping to think. "And I will take
you to my own city, you of the bright smile, as you deserve, lovely one. I tell
you this truly."

When he had said this, the royal seer took the hand of that girl, whose walk was
graceful, by the rite, and he lay with her. He gave her his word, and then he
went away, saying again and again, "I will send an army of four divisions for
you. With it I will bring you to my home, you of the bright smile."

So the king promised her, Janamejaya, and went away, thinking about the
Kashyapa. *When the blessed one, so rich in austerity, hears of this, what will
he do?* So he wondered as he entered his own city.

A moment after he had gone, Kanva came back to the hermitage. Shakuntala was
ashamed, and she did not go to meet her father. But Kanva, the great ascetic
with heavenly knowledge, knew. Seeing with his heavenly eye, the blessed one was
pleased, and said, "What you did today, you who are of a royal line, joining
with a man without asking me, does not harm dharma. For a kshatriya the
gandharva marriage is said to be the best: a desiring woman with a desiring man,
in private, without mantras. Dushyanta, the best of men, whom you went to,
loving him, Shakuntala, is righteous and great-souled. A great-souled son will
be born to you in the world, very strong. He will rule this whole earth, whose
edges are the oceans. And when that great one marches against his enemies, the
wheel of his chariot will roll on, never checked, for he will be an emperor."

Then she washed the feet of the sage when he had rested, took his load from him,
and set out the fruit. And she said to him, "Dushyanta, the best of men, is the
husband I have chosen. You should be gracious to him and to his ministers."

"I am gracious to him already, for your sake, lovely one," said Kanva. "And for
his sake, choose from me whatever boon you wish."

Then Shakuntala, wanting good for Dushyanta, chose that the Pauravas should
always be righteous and should never fall from their kingdom.

**Divergences.**

* **Kalidasa's play is not Vyasa.** The friends Anasuya and Priyamvada, the
  bee, the ring, and the curse of Durvasa that makes the king forget are all from
  Kalidasa's *Abhijñānaśākuntalam*. None of them is in the CE, its rejected
  passages or Ganguli. In the CE there is no curse. The king leaves, promising to
  send for her. See ledger **L-68**, and chapter 25 for what happens at court.

**Choices.**

* **Nested telling.** Shakuntala repeats what Kanva told a visiting seer, who
  repeats Indra's and Menaka's words. The novel keeps the nesting with quotation
  marks at each level.
* 1.65.15 *duḥṣanta*: Shakuntala calls the king by his name before he has
  given it. The text has it, and the novel keeps it.
* 1.65.29 *putrair iṣṭair vyayojayat*: Vishvamitra "parted Vasishtha from his
  dear sons". The story is told later in Book 1.
* 1.65.31–33: the Kaushiki and Para rivers and Matanga's sacrifice are only
  alluded to here. The novel adds nothing to them.
* 1.65.34 *prati śravaṇapūrvāṇi nakṣatrāṇi sasarja*: he made "counter-stars"
  beginning with Shravana.
* 1.66.13: the three kinds of father (the one who makes the body, the one who
  saves life, the one who feeds) are given as the text lists them.
* 1.67.8–13: the eight marriages and their rules are given as the king states
  them. It is his argument, and the novel reports it without comment.
* 1.67.18 *avicārayan*: "without thinking it over". Kept.
* 1.67.25 *rājānvayayā*: Kanva calls her "of a royal line", since her father
  Vishvamitra was born a kshatriya.
* 1.67.29 *cakravartin*: "one whose wheel rolls everywhere", an emperor.
  Rendered through the image of the chariot wheel.
