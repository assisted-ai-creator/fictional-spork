summary: The twelfth morning, told from Drona's side. Once Arjuna has been drawn away, Drona draws up the army in the shape of Garuda and advances to take Yudhishthira, who answers with a half-circle. Bhagadatta sits on his elephant in the middle, like the rising sun. Dhrishtadyumna, whose very sight is ill-omened for Drona, promises to hold him off, and Durmukha holds Dhrishtadyumna. The elephants clash and the fighting loses all order. Drona reaches Yudhishthira. Satyajit, left to guard the king, fights Drona hard and is killed, and Yudhishthira withdraws, as Arjuna told him to. Drona kills Virata's younger brother Shatanika and many others, and makes another river of the dead. Duryodhana laughs to Karna that the Pandavas are beaten. Karna warns him that Bhima will never leave the field, remembering the poison, the fire, the dice and the forest, and that Drona carries too great a load. Duryodhana and his brothers go to help Drona.

# The Garuda Array

"When the night had passed," said Sanjaya, "the great chariot fighter
Bharadvaja's son had talked long with King Suyodhana, king. He had arranged for
Partha to be engaged with the hosts of the sworn band. Partha went out of the
battle to kill the sworn band. Then Drona drew up his army and rode at the great
army of the Pandavas, best of the Bharatas, wanting to capture the King of
Dharma.

"When Yudhishthira saw the array Bharadvaja's son had made, in the shape of the
bird Suparna, he answered it with an array in the shape of a half-circle. The
great chariot fighter, Bharadvaja's son, was the bird's beak. King Duryodhana,
with his brothers and his followers, was its head. Kritavarma and Gautama, the
best of bowmen, were its eyes. Bhutavarman, Kshemasharman and mighty Karakarsha
stood in its neck, with the Kalingas, the Simhalas and the peoples of the east.
With them were the Shuras and Abhiras, the Dasherakas, the Shakas, Yavanas and
Kambojas, and the Hamsapadas, the Shurasenas, the Daradas, the Madras and the
Kekayas, with hosts of elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers in their
hundreds of thousands. Bhurishravas, Shala, Shalya and Somadatta the Bahlika,
heroes surrounded by an akshauhini, held the right wing. Vinda and Anuvinda of
Avanti and Sudakshina the Kamboja held the left wing, with Drona's son in front
of them. At the back were the Kalingas with the Ambashthas, the Magadhas, the
Paundras and the Madrakas, the Gandharas with Shakuni in front, the mountain
peoples and the Vasatis. In the tail stood Vaikartana Karna, with his sons, his
kin and his friends, and a great army raised under many banners. Jayadratha,
Bhimaratha and Jaya, Bhumimjaya, Vrishakratha and the mighty Naishadha,
surrounded by a great army and set on the world of Brahma, stood in the body of
the array, king, skilled in battle.

"Drona's array, made of foot soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants, looked
like the ocean stirred up by the wind, and it seemed to be moving. From its
wings and its outer wings the fighters rushed out, eager for battle, like clouds
with lightning and thunder from every direction at the end of the hot season. In
its middle the king of Pragjyotisha sat on an elephant fitted out by the rules,
and shone like the sun at its rising, king. With a white umbrella held over him,
hung with garlands, the king was like the full moon joined with the Krittikas.
His elephant, blind with rut, looked like a heap of black collyrium, like a
great mountain rained on by great clouds. He was surrounded by the mountain
kings, heroes with every kind of weapon and ornament, like Shakra among the
hosts of the gods.

"Yudhishthira saw that array, more than human, which no enemy could beat in
battle. He said to Parshata, 'Lord, you whose horses are the colour of doves,
make a plan so that I do not fall into the brahmin's power today.'

"'You will not fall into Drona's power, however hard he tries, man of good
vows,' said Dhrishtadyumna. 'I will hold Drona back today, with his followers.
While I live, Kaurava, you need not be anxious. Drona can never beat me in
battle.'

"When he had said this, mighty Drupada's son, whose horses were the colour of
doves, rushed at Drona himself, scattering arrows," said Sanjaya. "When Drona
saw Dhrishtadyumna standing before him, a sight of ill omen, he was all at once
none too cheerful. Seeing this, your son Durmukha, the tamer of enemies, wanting
to please Drona, held Dhrishtadyumna back. The fight between brave Parshata and
Durmukha was wild, Bharata, and the two seemed well matched. Parshata quickly
covered Durmukha with a net of arrows and held off Bharadvaja's son with a great
flood of arrows. When your son saw Drona held back, he pressed hard and
bewildered Parshata with swarms of arrows of every kind. While the chief of the
Panchalas and the chief of the Kurus were locked together in battle, Drona
struck Yudhishthira's army in many places with his arrows. As clouds are torn
apart by the wind on every side, so Partha's troops were torn apart here and
there.

"For a moment the battle was pleasant to look at. Then it went on like a
madman's, with no order at all. Neither our men nor theirs could tell each other
apart, king. The fighting went on by guesswork and by watchwords. On crest
jewels, gold coins and ornaments, on swords and shields, rays shone out the
colour of the sun. With its scattered banners, its chariots, elephants and
horses, the battle looked like clouds dappled with cranes. Men killed men, proud
horses killed horses, chariot fighters killed chariots, and elephants killed the
finest elephants.

"In a moment a wild and terrible battle broke out between elephants with their
banners raised and the finest tuskers. As they locked bodies and dragged at one
another, fire and smoke sprang from the grinding of their tusks. With their
banners scattered and fire struck from their tusks, they were like clouds with
lightning in the sky. With elephants dripping, trumpeting and falling, the earth
was strewn like the autumn sky with clouds. As they were struck by showers of
arrows and lances, the elephants roared like clouds at the end of the world.
Some fine elephants, struck by lances and arrows, took fright. Others ran only
at the sound of the creatures around them. Some elephants, gored by others'
tusks, cried out in pain, like the clouds that come as portents. Some, driven
back by the finest elephants, broke free and came on again, urged by the fine
goads. Elephant riders struck down by other riders with arrows and lances fell
from their elephants to the ground, their weapons and goads let go. Elephants
without riders ran trumpeting here and there, crashing into one another like
broken clouds. Great elephants, pinned by great weapons, carried off the dead in
every direction, some of them wandering alone. Struck and being struck with
lances, swords and axes, the elephants fell in that slaughter, crying in pain.
As their bodies, like mountains, fell on every side, the earth was struck at
once, and it shook and roared. With the elephants cut down with their riders and
banners on every side, the earth looked as if it were strewn with mountains.

"Elephant riders on their elephants were pierced to the heart in battle and
brought down by chariot fighters with broad arrows, their goads and lances
scattered. Other elephants, struck by iron arrows, cried out like herons and ran
in every direction, trampling friend and foe. The earth, king, was covered with
heaps of bodies of elephants, horses and chariots, and became a mire of flesh
and blood. Great chariot fighters, with their chariots, wheels and all or
without their wheels, were crushed by the tusks of elephants and thrown into the
air. Chariots without their fighters, horses without men, and elephants whose
riders had been killed ran in every direction, tormented by arrows.

"There a father killed his son and a son his father. So wild was the battle that
nothing could be told apart. Men sank to their ankles in the mire of blood, like
great trees caught in a forest fire. Clothes and armour, umbrellas and flags
were soaked with blood, and everything looked red. Herds of horses, chariots and
men were struck down and turned over again and again by the rims of the chariot
wheels. That ocean of an army had herds of elephants for its great current, dead
men for its weed and hosts of chariots for its whirlpools. The warriors, wanting
the wealth of victory, plunged into it in the great boats of their mounts, and
sank, but they did not lose their wits. As the warriors, their marks of victory
not yet won, were showered with arrows, none of them lost heart, though they
bore their wounds. While that terrible, frightening battle went on, Drona
bewildered his enemies and rushed at Yudhishthira.

"When Yudhishthira saw Drona close at hand, he met him fearlessly with a great
shower of arrows. Then there was an uproar in Yudhishthira's army, as when a
great lion wants to seize the leader of a herd of elephants. When brave
Satyajit, whose courage was true, saw Drona wanting to seize Yudhishthira, he
rushed at the teacher. Then the teacher and the Panchala fought each other,
throwing the army into turmoil, like Indra and Virochana's son. Then the teacher
quickly pierced Satyajit with ten sharp arrows that split the vital parts, and
cut his bow and its arrow. Mighty Satyajit quickly took up another bow and
struck Drona at once with twenty arrows fledged with heron feathers. When Vrika
of Panchala saw Drona almost swallowed by Satyajit in battle, he struck Drona
with hundreds of sharp arrows. When they saw the great chariot fighter Drona
covered in the battle, the Pandavas cried out, king, and waved their clothes.
Mighty Vrika, in a great fury, pierced Drona in the chest with sixty arrows,
king, and it was a marvel.

"Drona, the great chariot fighter, covered by that shower of arrows, rolled his
eyes in anger and gathered all his great speed. Then Drona cut Satyajit's bow
and Vrika's, and with six arrows he killed Vrika, with his charioteer and his
horses. Satyajit took up another, faster bow, and pierced Drona with arrows,
with his horses, his charioteer and his banner. Drona could not bear being
tormented by the Panchala in battle. He loosed arrows at once to destroy him. He
covered his horses, his banner, his bow and the grip of his hand, and both his
flank drivers, with showers of arrows by the thousand. Though his bows were cut
again and again, the Panchala, a great master of weapons, fought on against the
one with red horses. When Drona saw Satyajit so fierce in the great battle, he
cut off the great man's head with a crescent-headed arrow.

"When that great warrior, the bull among the Panchala chariot fighters, had been
killed, Yudhishthira was afraid of Drona, and withdrew on his swift horses. The
Panchalas, the Kekayas and the Matsyas, the Chedis, the Karushas and the
Kosalas, seeing Yudhishthira, rushed at Drona with a will. Then the teacher, the
killer of hosts of enemies, still seeking Yudhishthira, scattered those ranks as
the wind scatters heaps of cotton. As Drona burned up those ranks, one after
another, again and again, Shatanika, the Matsya king's younger brother, came at
him. He pierced Drona, with his charioteer and his horses, with six arrows like
the rays of the sun, polished by the smith, and he roared loudly. As he roared,
Drona struck his head with its earrings from his body with a razor-headed arrow,
and the Matsyas fled.

"When he had beaten the Matsyas, Bharadvaja's son beat the Chedis, the Karushas,
the Kekayas, the Panchalas, the Srinjayas and the Pandavas, again and again.
When they saw the warrior of the golden chariot in his anger, burning up the
ranks as an angry fire burns a forest, the Srinjayas trembled. As the quick one
bent his fine bow and killed his enemies, the twang of his bowstring was heard
in every direction. The terrible arrows loosed by his skilled hand crushed
elephants, horses and foot soldiers, chariot fighters and elephant riders. Like
a thundering rain cloud with a mixed wind at the end of the cold, he rained down
a hail of stones and put fear into the enemy. Mighty, brave, a great archer, who
gave safety to his friends, he ranged in every direction, as if churning the
army. We saw his bow, set with gold, in every direction, like lightning in the
clouds, the bow of Drona of boundless power.

"Drona made a great slaughter in the Pandava army, as Vishnu, honoured by gods
and demons, did among the hosts of the daityas. That hero, truthful, wise and
strong, true in courage and of great power, set flowing there a river that went
to Death, as at the end of time. It was terrible, and it terrified the timid.
Armour was its waves, banners its whirlpools, and it carried off mortals like
its banks. Elephants and horses were its great crocodiles, swords its fish, and
it was hard to reach. Heroes' bones were its gravel. Drums and tabors were its
turtles, shields and armour its boats, and hair its weed and grass. Its floods
were arrows and its currents bows, and it was full of arms like snakes. It
carried the battlefield along, and it carried the armies of the Kurus and the
Srinjayas. Men's heads were its stones, spears its fish and maces its rafts.
Turbans were its foam and its cloth, and spilled entrails its creeping things.
It carried off heroes, and it was fierce, a mire of flesh and blood. Elephants
were its sharks, banners its trees, and it drowned kshatriyas. It was cruel, its
dams were heaps of bodies, horsemen were its crocodiles, and it was hard to
cross. Flocks of flesh-eaters cried over it, packs of dogs and jackals came to
it, and terrible eaters of raw flesh haunted it on every side.

"As that great chariot fighter burned up the ranks like the Ender, those led by
Kunti's son rushed at Drona from every side. Your men, kings and princes with
weapons raised, surrounded those brave great archers on every side. Then Drona,
true to his word, like an elephant in rut, went past the ranks of chariots and
brought down Dridhasena. Then he came at King Kshema, who was fighting
fearlessly, and pierced him with nine arrows, and he fell dead from his chariot.
Reaching the middle of the armies, ranging in every direction, he became the
protector of the others, and needed no protection at all. He pierced Shikhandi
with twelve arrows and Uttamaujas with twenty, and sent Vasudana to Yama's house
with a broad arrow. He pierced Kshatravarman with eighty and Sudakshina with
twenty-six, and knocked Kshatradeva from his chariot seat with a broad arrow. He
pierced Yudhamanyu with sixty-four and Satyaki with thirty, and then the warrior
of the golden chariot rushed at Yudhishthira.

"Then Yudhishthira, the gambler, the best of kings, quickly withdrew on his
swift horses, and a Panchala rode at Drona. Drona destroyed him, with his bow,
his horses and his driver, and he fell dead from his chariot to the ground, like
a star falling from the sky. When that prince, the glory of the Panchalas, had
been killed, a great uproar rose: 'Kill Drona! Kill Drona!'

"Mighty Drona threw into confusion the Panchalas, Matsyas and Kekayas, the
Srinjayas and the Pandavas, in their great fury. Satyaki and Chekitana,
Dhrishtadyumna and Shikhandi, Vriddhakshema's son, Chitrasena, Senabindu and
Suvarchas, these and very many other lords of many lands, Drona beat them all in
battle, with the Kurus all around him. Your men, great king, having won the day
in the great battle, killed the Pandavas as they fled in every direction. The
Panchalas, the Kekayas and the Matsyas trembled, Bharata, like the danavas
struck by great Indra."

"When the Pandavas and all the Panchalas were broken in the great battle by
Bharadvaja's son," said Dhritarashtra, "did anyone else turn back to face him?
Did anyone set his mind on the noble way in battle, which brings kshatriyas
fame, which cowards do not follow and bulls among men do? For he is a hero,
charioteer, the man who turns back when others are broken. Oh, was there no man
at all who turned back when he saw Drona standing there? He was like a tiger
yawning, like an elephant in rut, ready to give his life in battle, armoured, a
wonderful fighter. He was a great archer, a tiger among men who made his enemies
suffer, grateful, devoted to truth, wanting Duryodhana's good. When they saw
brave Bharadvaja's son standing in the ranks like that, which heroes turned
back? Tell me that, Sanjaya."

"The Panchalas, the Pandavas and the Matsyas, the Srinjayas, the Chedis and the
Kekayas were shaken in the battle, driven back by Drona's arrows," said Sanjaya.
"They were carried off by the flood of life-taking arrows loosed from Drona's
bow, like boats carried off by the great flood of the Sindhu. When the Kauravas
saw them, they surrounded them on every side with lions' roars and the sound of
many instruments, with chariots, elephants, men and horses.

"King Duryodhana, standing in the middle of the army, surrounded by his own
people, saw them. Delighted and almost laughing, he said to Karna, 'Look,
Radheya, at the Panchalas driven back by Drona's arrows, frightened by the
archer with the strong bow, like forest deer by a lion. I do not think they will
ever want to fight again. Drona has broken them, as the wind breaks great trees.
Tormented by the great man's arrows with golden feathers, they are not even
going one way, but reeling here and there. Hemmed in by the Kauravas and by
great Drona, others are going round in circles, like elephants caught by a fire.
As if they were stung by bees, Drona's sharp arrows have gone into them, and
they cling to one another, set only on running away. Here is Bhima, of fixed
anger, without the Pandavas and the Srinjayas, surrounded by my warriors, Karna,
and he seems to threaten me. Clearly the fool sees the world full of Drona
today. Surely the Pandava has lost hope of life, and of the kingdom, today.'

"'That strong-armed man will never leave the battle while he lives,' said Karna.
'Nor will he bear these lions' roars, tiger among men. Nor do I think the
Pandavas will break in battle. They are brave and strong, masters of weapons,
mad for battle. The Pandavas remember the poison, the fire and the misery of the
dice, and their life in the forest, and they will not leave the battle. That is
what I think. For strong-armed Wolf-belly, of boundless power, has been wronged,
and Kunti's son will kill the finest chariot fighters, one after another. With
sword, bow and spear, with horses, elephants, men and chariots, and with his
iron club, he will kill them in their hosts. Satyaki and the chariot fighters
with him follow him, the Panchalas, the Kekayas and the Matsyas, and above all
the Pandavas. They are brave and strong, bold great chariot fighters, and above
all they are urged on by Bhima in his fury. Those bulls of the Kurus are closing
on Drona from every side, wanting to save Wolf-belly, like clouds round the sun.
They are set on one thing, and they could crush the man of strict vows, if he is
left unguarded, as moths about to die crush a lamp. No doubt they are masters of
weapons, and able to hold him back. I think too great a load has been laid on
Bharadvaja's son. Let us go quickly to where Drona is, so that they do not kill
the man of strict vows, as crows kill a great elephant.'

"When King Duryodhana heard Radheya's words, king, he went with his brothers to
Drona's chariot. There a great din went up from the Pandavas, who had turned
back, wanting to kill Drona alone, with their fine horses of many colours."

**Divergences.**

* **A second telling of the morning.** 7.19 goes back to the start of the twelfth day, after the sworn band has drawn Arjuna away (7.16–7.18), and tells it from Drona's side.
* **Satyajit.** Arjuna left Satyajit to guard Yudhishthira and told the king to leave the field if Satyajit fell (7.16.44–45). Drona kills him (7.20.16), and Yudhishthira withdraws (7.20.17).
* **Shatanika, the Matsya's younger brother.** Drona kills "Shatanika, the Matsya king's younger brother" (7.20.20–22). In Book 6, Bhishma has already killed "Virata's dear brother Shatanika" (6.113.24). The CE has both; the novel tells both as the text does. Neither is Nakula's son Shatanika.
* **"The gambler."** At 7.20.46 Yudhishthira is called *kitava*, "the gambler", as he withdraws. The word is kept.
* **Sudakshina.** The Sudakshina Drona pierces at 7.20.44 is on the Pandava side; he is not the Kamboja king, who holds the Kaurava left wing (7.19.10).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*142–\*158. \*142 explains Drona's gloom: Dhrishtadyumna was born to kill him.
