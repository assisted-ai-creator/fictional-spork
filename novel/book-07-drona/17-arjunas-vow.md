summary: At sunset Arjuna drives back from the sworn band, uneasy at ill omens. The camp is silent, the music stopped, and his men turn away from him. Not seeing Abhimanyu, he guesses what has happened and breaks into grief: the boy knew how to enter the wheel array but not how to get out. He recalls that he and Krishna heard Yuyutsu shaming the Kauravas for killing a boy. Krishna tells him that this is the road of all brave kshatriyas who do not turn back, and to comfort his brothers. Arjuna asks how it happened, and rages at his brothers for not protecting his son. Yudhishthira tells him everything: the wheel array, how they sent Abhimanyu in and could not follow because of Jayadratha's boon, the six chariot fighters and Duhshasana's son. Arjuna falls senseless. When he comes to, he swears to kill Jayadratha tomorrow, unless he flees or seeks their protection, calling down on himself the worlds of every sinner if he fails, and vows that if the sun sets with Jayadratha alive he will enter the fire. Krishna blows Panchajanya, and Arjuna Devadatta.

# Arjuna's Vow

"That day of dreadful slaughter of living things came to an end," said Sanjaya.
"The sun had set and the time of twilight had come, and the armies went away to
rest, bull of the Bharatas. Having killed the hosts of the sworn band with his
divine weapons, glorious Jishnu, with the monkey on his banner, set off for his
camp in his victorious chariot. As he went, he spoke to Govinda in a choked
voice.

"'Why is my heart afraid, Keshava? My words stick. Ill omens throb, and my body
is weak, Achyuta. Something bad clings to my heart and will not leave it. Fierce
portents on the earth and in every direction frighten me. They are seen in many
forms, and all of them foretell disaster. Is it well with the king, my elder,
and his counsellors?'

"'It will surely be well with your brother and his counsellors,' said Vasudeva.
'Do not grieve. Something else bad will happen there.'

"Then the two heroes worshipped at twilight, there in that slaughter of heroes,
and went on in their chariot, talking of what had happened in the battle. Then
Vasudeva and Arjuna reached their own camp, having done very hard deeds. It was
joyless and without its light. The killer of enemy heroes saw the camp so
changed. Sick at heart, Bibhatsu said to Krishna, 'Today the lucky instruments
do not sound, Janardana, the conches mixed with the boom of drums and
kettledrums. The lutes are not played today, with the sound of cymbals and hand
bells. In my ranks the bards do not sing or chant lucky songs, lovely and full
of praise. And the warriors, when they see me, turn back with their heads bowed,
and they do not greet me with an account of their deeds as they used to. Is it
well with my brothers today, Madhava? My mind is not easy when I see my own
people so upset. Is the king of Panchala whole, and Virata, and all the
warriors, Achyuta, giver of honour? And today Subhadra's son does not come out
with his brothers, glad and almost laughing, to meet me as I come from battle,
as he always does.'

"Talking like this, the two went into their own camp and saw the Pandavas deeply
upset and lost. When he saw his brothers and his sons, and did not see
Subhadra's son, he of the monkey banner was downcast, and he said:

"'Your faces all look pale and unhappy. And I do not see Abhimanyu, and you do
not greet me. I heard that Drona had made the wheel array, and none of you could
break it in battle except Subhadra's son. But I had not taught him how to get
out of the array. Did you send the boy into the enemy's army? Did the great
archer, Subhadra's son, the killer of enemy heroes, break into the enemy's army
many times in battle, and is he lying dead? Tell me how he was killed in battle,
red-eyed, strong-armed, like a lion born in the mountains, the equal of Upendra.
Tell me how he was killed in battle, the gentle great archer, the grandson of
Vasava, always dear to me. The hero loved by the Vrishni woman, always cherished
by me, always dear to his mother, who killed him, driven by Time? Like great
Keshava, the lion of the Vrishnis, in courage, learning and greatness, how was
he killed in battle? Always dear to Subhadra, to Draupadi and to Keshava, if I
do not see my son, I will go to Yama's house.

"'He had soft, curling hair. He was a boy with the eyes of a young deer and the
gait of an elephant in rut, like a young shala tree shooting up. He spoke with a
smile, he was self-controlled, and he always did what his elders said. Even as a
child he did no childish deeds. He spoke kindly and was never jealous. He was
full of spirit, strong-armed, with long lotus eyes, kind to those who loved him,
and he never followed low men. He was grateful and full of knowledge, a master
of weapons who never turned back, always delighting in battle and bringing ruin
on his enemies. He was devoted to the good of his own people and eager for his
fathers' victory. He was never the first to strike in battle, and never
flustered. If I do not see my son, I will go to Yama's house.

"'Without seeing that face, with its fine forehead and hair, its fine brows,
eyes and lips, what peace can my heart have? Without hearing his voice, lovely
and pleasing as the sound of a lute, like the call of the male cuckoo, what
peace can my heart have? Without seeing today that hero's beauty, beyond
compare, rare even among the thirty gods, what peace can my heart have? If I do
not see him today, so ready to bow, devoted to his fathers' words, what peace
can my heart have? The tender hero, always used to costly beds, surely lies
today on the ground like a man with no one, the best of those who have someone.
He whom the finest women waited on as he lay, today ill-omened jackals wait on
as he lies with his limbs torn. He who was woken from sleep by bards, heralds
and singers is surely woken today by beasts of prey with their ugly cries. That
lovely face of his, used to the shade of an umbrella, the dust of battle will
surely cover today.

"'Oh, my son! I could never see enough of you, and now, unlucky as I am, Time
drags you away from me by force. Surely today Samyamani, the city that is always
the goal of those who do good, lovely and bright with its own light, is lit up
even more by you. Surely Vaivasvata and Varuna, Shatakratu and the lord of
wealth are honouring you now you have come, a welcome guest who knew no fear.'

"He wept like this in many ways, like a merchant whose ship has broken, overcome
with great grief. Then he asked Yudhishthira, 'Did he, the bull among men, make
havoc of the enemy, son of Pandu, and go to heaven fighting, facing them in
battle? Surely, fighting many careful bulls among men, alone and wanting help,
he thought of me. As the boy was tormented with arrows, he cried out, "Father,
come to me quickly!" I think the cruel ones killed him as he cried like that,
the many of them. Or rather, born of me, the son of Madhava's sister, born of
Subhadra, he would not speak like that. My heart must be made of the stuff of
thunderbolts, and very hard, since it does not break when I do not see him,
long-armed and red-eyed. How could the cruel men who split the vital parts shoot
arrows at the boy, the great archer, the son of Vasudeva's sister, my son? He
who always came out to meet me with an unfallen spirit and greeted me as I came
back from killing my enemies, why does he not see me today? Surely he lies
fallen on the ground, bathed in blood, making the earth beautiful with his
limbs, like the sun brought down.

"'Grief will surely kill Subhadra when she hears that her son, who never ran,
has been killed in battle. What will Subhadra say to me, when she does not see
Abhimanyu? And Draupadi, in pain with her grief, what shall I say to the two of
them? My heart must be made of thunderbolts, since it will not break into a
thousand pieces when I see my son's young wife weeping, worn with grief.

"'I heard the lions' roar of the delighted sons of Dhritarashtra, and Krishna
heard Yuyutsu reproaching the heroes. "Great chariot fighters, you could not
beat Bibhatsu, and you have killed a boy. Why are you roaring, you who do not
know dharma? Show your strength against Partha. Why do you roar like lions,
pleased, when you have done wrong to Keshava and Arjuna in battle, when a time
of grief has come? The fruit of your evil deed will come to you soon. You have
done a bitter adharma. How could it go long without its fruit?" Saying this to
them, the wise son of the vaishya woman went away, throwing down his weapons,
full of anger and grief. Why did you not tell me this in battle, Krishna? I
would have burned up all those cruel great chariot fighters then.'

"Then Vasudeva held back Partha, overwhelmed with sorrow for his son and deeply
grieved. 'Not like this,' said Krishna. 'This is the road of all brave men who
do not turn back, and above all of kshatriyas, who live by war. This is the way
laid down by those who know the law, for brave men who fight and do not turn
back, best of those who know the way. Death in battle is certain for brave men
who do not turn back. Abhimanyu has gone to the worlds of those who do good,
beyond doubt. This is what all heroes long for, bull of the Bharatas: "May we
meet death in battle, facing the enemy," giver of honour. And he, having killed
brave and mighty princes in battle, has met the death heroes long for, facing
them in battle. Do not grieve, tiger among men. This is the eternal dharma made
by the makers of dharma of old: the end of kshatriyas in battle. All your
brothers here are miserable, best of the Bharatas, now that you are overcome
with grief, and the kings and your friends too. Comfort them with gentle words,
giver of honour. You know what is to be known. You should not grieve.'

"So Krishna, whose deeds were wonderful, comforted him. Then Partha spoke to all
his brothers, whose voices were choked. 'I want to hear how it happened to
long-armed, broad-shouldered Abhimanyu, with his long lotus eyes. You will see
his enemies killed by me in battle, with their elephants, chariots and horses
and their kin. How could Subhadra's son meet his death, when all of you were
there, masters of weapons with weapons in your hands, even if the wielder of the
thunderbolt had come against him? If I had known that the Pandavas and the
Panchalas could not guard my son, I would have guarded him myself. How was
Abhimanyu brought to his death, scorned by the enemy, while you stood in your
chariots loosing showers of arrows? Oh, you have no manhood and no courage,
since Abhimanyu was brought down in battle while you looked on. I should blame
myself, for I went out knowing you to be so weak, cowards who have never worked
hard. Or are your armour, your weapons and your arms only for show, and your
words only for speaking in the assembly, since you did not guard my son?'

"When he had said this, standing with his bow and his fine sword, Bibhatsu could
not be looked at by anyone. He was like Death in his anger, sighing again and
again, burning with grief for his son, his face full of tears. His friends could
not speak to Arjuna or look at him, except Vasudeva or the eldest son of Pandu.
In every state those two were good to Arjuna and followed his mind, and out of
great respect and love they could speak to him. Then the king spoke to the
lotus-eyed one, angry, his heart deeply tormented by grief for his son.

"'When you had gone against the army of the sworn band, strong-armed one,' said
Yudhishthira, 'the teacher made a fierce effort to capture me. We held back
Drona, who had drawn up his ranks, on every side, opposing his chariot ranks
with our own as he strove in battle. Held back by the chariot fighters, and with
me guarded, he struck us quickly and tormented us with sharp arrows. Tormented
by Drona, we could not even look at Drona's army in battle, let alone break it.
So we said to your son, Subhadra's son, of matchless courage, "Break the army,
my son." Urged on by us, like a good horse, the mighty boy set out to carry that
load, unbearable as it was. With the weapons you taught him and his own courage,
the boy went into that army like Suparna into the sea. We followed the hero, the
son of the Satvata woman, into battle, wanting to go in the way he had gone into
the army. Then the Saindhava king, the mean Jayadratha, my son, held us all back
by the boon of Rudra.

"'Then Drona, Kripa, Karna, Drona's son, Brihadbala and Kritavarma, six chariot
fighters, surrounded Subhadra's son. Surrounded in battle by all those great
chariot fighters, the boy, striving with all his might, was made chariotless by
the many of them. Then, when they had made him chariotless, Duhshasana's son,
though in great danger himself, quickly brought him to his fated end. He had
killed thousands of elephants, horses, chariots and horsemen, the best hundred
princes and many heroes no one noticed, and sent King Brihadbala to heaven in
battle. Then that man of the highest dharma met his fated end. This is all that
has happened to increase our grief. And so that tiger among men has reached the
world of heaven.'

"When Arjuna heard the words the King of Dharma had spoken, he sighed, 'Oh, my
son!', and fell to the ground in pain. All of them, their faces downcast, held
Dhananjaya and looked at one another, miserable, with unblinking eyes. Then
Vasava's son came to himself, faint with rage, trembling as if with fever,
sighing again and again. He wrung his hands, breathing hard, his eyes full of
tears. Looking about like a madman, he said:

"'I promise you truly, tomorrow I will kill Jayadratha, unless, frightened of
being killed, he leaves the sons of Dhritarashtra, or comes to us for refuge, or
to Krishna, the highest of men, or to you, great king. Tomorrow I will kill
Jayadratha, who does what Dhritarashtra's son likes, who has forgotten his
friendship with me, the evil man who was the cause of the boy's killing. Whoever
fights me in battle to protect him, even the heroes Drona and Kripa, I will
cover with my arrows.

"'If I do not do this in battle, bulls among men, let me not reach the worlds of
those who do good, which heroes prize. May I go to the worlds of those who kill
their mothers and their fathers, of those who go to their teacher's wife, and of
slanderers. May I go to the worlds of those who resent the good and those who
speak ill of others, of those who steal what was left in their keeping and those
who betray a trust. May I go to the worlds of those who speak ill of a woman
they once enjoyed and those who speak of sin, the worlds of the killers of
brahmins and the killers of cows. May I go to the worlds of those who eat milk
rice, barley food, vegetables, rice with sesame, cakes and meat for no purpose.
May I reach those worlds in a single day if I do not kill Jayadratha.

"'There is the road of one who despises a brahmin, deeply learned in the Veda
and strict, or the old, the good or his teachers. There is the road of those who
touch a brahmin, a cow or the fire with their foot, and of those who pass
phlegm, dung or urine into water. May I go by that terrible road if I do not
kill Jayadratha. There is the road of one who bathes naked and of the one whose
guest goes away empty, of bribe-takers, liars and cheats, of those who betray
themselves and of false accusers. There is the road of the mean who eat fine
food without sharing it, while their servants, sons and wives, who depend on
them, look on. May I go by that terrible road if I do not kill Jayadratha. There
is the cruel one who abandons a good man who has come to him for shelter and is
devoted to his word, and does not support him, and speaks ill of one who helped
him. There is the one who does not give a funeral meal to a worthy neighbour,
and gives it to one who is unworthy, and the husband of a shudra woman. There
are the drinker, the breaker of bounds, the ungrateful and the one who speaks
ill of his brother. May I go quickly by their road if I do not kill Jayadratha.
There are those who have fallen from dharma whom I have not named here, and
those I have named. May I quickly reach their road if, when this night has
passed, I do not kill Jayadratha tomorrow.

"'Hear also this other vow of mine. If the sun sets with that evil man not
killed, I will enter the blazing fire right here. Demons, gods and men, birds or
snakes, ancestors or night-walkers, brahmin seers or divine seers will not be
able to protect my enemy. Not all that moves and does not move, nor whatever is
beyond it, will be able to protect him. He may go into the underworld, or beyond
it, or into the sky, the city of the gods or the city of Diti's sons. Even then
at dawn I will fall on him with hundreds of arrows and cut off my enemy's head.'

"When he had said this, he drew the Gandiva to the left and to the right. Its
sound rose above his voice and touched the sky. When Arjuna had made his vow,
Janardana in his anger blew Panchajanya, and Dhananjaya blew Devadatta.
Panchajanya was filled by the breath of Achyuta's mouth, its hollow full. Its
sound shook the world, with the underworld, the sky, the quarters and their
lords, as at the end of an age. Then the sound of instruments rose on every
side, and the Pandavas roared like lions, when the great man had made his vow."

**Divergences.**

* **"I had not taught him how to get out."** Arjuna's own words (7.50.21) match Abhimanyu's at 7.34.19. See ledger L-39.
* **Yuyutsu's reproach.** Arjuna says that Krishna heard Yuyutsu shame the Kauravas for killing a boy, and that Yuyutsu threw down his weapons and went away (7.50.55–59). The CE has Arjuna report it; it is not told where it happened.
* **The vow.** Arjuna swears to kill Jayadratha the next day unless he flees the Kauravas or seeks the protection of the Pandavas, Krishna or Yudhishthira (7.51.20–21), and adds a second vow: if the sun sets with Jayadratha alive, he will enter the fire (7.51.37).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*343–\*373. \*343 has Vyasa leave Yudhishthira, following the rejected consolation (see ledger L-203); \*371 restates the vow; \*373 has the Gandiva seem to dance.
