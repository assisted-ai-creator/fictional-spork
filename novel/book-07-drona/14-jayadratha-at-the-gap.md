summary: Abhimanyu reminds Duhshasana of his cruel words in the assembly hall and strikes him senseless; his charioteer carries him away. Karna attacks, and Abhimanyu cuts his bow and banner, kills Karna's younger brother and drives Karna from the field. The Kaurava army breaks, and only Jayadratha stands. # Jayadratha at the Gap

"His body was cut by arrows," said Sanjaya, "but wise Abhimanyu smiled at his
enemy Duhshasana, who stood before him. He said, 'By good luck I see in battle
the proud enemy who has come here, cruel, who has given up dharma and lives to
abuse others. In the assembly hall, while King Dhritarashtra listened, you
angered Yudhishthira, the King of Dharma, with your harsh words, and Bhima too,
talking much wild nonsense, mad with your win. You stole others' wealth. Your
anger would not be quieted. You were greedy, you lost your wisdom, you were
treacherous and did great harm, and you took the kingdom of my fathers, those
fierce archers. For all this the anger of those great men has come upon you.
Take now at once the fierce fruit of adharma, you fool. Today, while the whole
army looks on, I will punish you with my arrows. Today in battle I will pay my
debt to that anger, to angry Krishnaa, and to my father's wish. Today, Kaurava,
I will pay my debt to Bhima in battle. You will not get away from me alive,
unless you leave the battle.'

"When he had said this, the strong-armed killer of enemy heroes fitted an arrow
that would be Duhshasana's end, blazing like the fire of Time and the wind. It
reached his chest quickly and split him at the collarbone. Then he struck him
again with twenty-five arrows. Deeply pierced and in pain, Duhshasana sank down
in his chariot, great king, and a deep faint came over him. His charioteer
quickly carried the senseless Duhshasana out of the middle of the battle,
tormented by Subhadra's son's arrows. When they saw him, the Pandavas,
Draupadi's sons and Virata, the Panchalas and the Kekayas roared like lions. The
Pandavas' soldiers, full of joy, played every kind of instrument, smiling as
they watched what Subhadra's son did, and seeing that proud enemy, their
bitterest foe, beaten.

"The great chariot fighters, Draupadi's sons, carried on their banner tops the
images of Dharma, the Wind, Shakra and the Ashvins. With Satyaki and Chekitana,
Dhrishtadyumna and Shikhandi, the Kekayas and Dhrishtaketu, the Matsyas, the
Panchalas and the Srinjayas, and the Pandavas led by Yudhishthira, they charged
together at Drona's ranks, full of joy, eager to break them. Then there was a
great battle between your men and the enemy, heroes who would not turn back,
each side longing for victory.

"Duryodhana said to Radheya, great king, 'Look at the hero Duhshasana, fallen
into Abhimanyu's power. And the Pandavas are charging to save Subhadra's son,
who is killing his enemies in battle like the burning sun.' Then Karna, in a
fury, wanting to do your son a good turn, poured sharp arrows on Abhimanyu, so
hard to face. With the best of arrows, scornfully, the hero pierced Subhadra's
son's followers in the battle. Abhimanyu, great of mind, quickly pierced Radheya
with seventy-three arrows, king, wanting to reach Drona. No one could hold him
back from Drona in the battle, as he crushed the best chariot fighters, like the
wielder of the thunderbolt crushing the demons.

"Then Karna, proud, the first of all bowmen, wanting victory, pierced Subhadra's
son with hundreds of arrows, showing his finest weapons. With his weapons the
mighty pupil of Rama, the best of those who know weapons, pressed Abhimanyu
hard, though enemies could not overcome him. Hard pressed as he was by Radheya's
showers of weapons, Subhadra's son, like an immortal in battle, did not lose
heart. Then with sharp, smooth-jointed broad arrows, sharpened on stone,
Arjuna's son cut the bows of the heroes and struck at Karna. He cut Karna's
banner and bow and brought them down to the ground.

"When he saw Karna in trouble, Karna's younger brother came quickly at
Subhadra's son, raising his strong bow. Then the Parthas and their followers
shouted, played their instruments and praised Subhadra's son. Roaring, bow in
hand, drawing his string again and again, the brother leapt at once between the
two great men. With ten arrows he quickly pierced Abhimanyu, so hard to face,
with his umbrella, banner, driver and horses, as if smiling. When they saw
Krishna's son, doing more than human deeds, the deeds of his fathers and
grandfathers, tormented by arrows, your men were delighted. Abhimanyu, smiling,
drew his bow and with a single arrow struck off his head. He fell from his
chariot to the ground, like a karnikara tree torn up and shaken by the wind from
a mountain. When Karna saw his brother killed, king, he was stricken with grief.

"When Subhadra's son had turned Karna back with his heron-fledged arrows, he
rushed quickly at other great archers. Then famous Abhimanyu broke that wide net
of elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers, as an angry fish breaks a net.
Karna, badly hurt by many of Abhimanyu's arrows, fled on his swift horses, and
the army broke. With Abhimanyu's arrows filling the sky, as if with locusts or
streams of rain, nothing could be seen, king. As your warriors were killed by
his sharp arrows, no one stood his ground, king, except the Saindhava.

"Then Subhadra's son, the bull among men, blew his conch and charged quickly at
the Bharata army, bull of the Bharatas. Like a fire let loose in dry brush,
burning up his enemies with speed, Arjuna's son ranged in the middle of the
Bharata armies. Tormenting chariots, elephants, horses and men with sharp
arrows, he went in and made the ground crowded with headless trunks. Cut down by
the fine arrows sprung from Subhadra's son's bow, they fled, wanting to live,
killing their own men who faced them. The terrible, cruel arrows, vipathas,
broad and sharp, killed chariots, elephants and horses and went quickly into the
ground. Arms cut off in the battle were seen, with their weapons and finger
guards, their swords and armlets, adorned with golden ornaments. Arrows, bows
and swords, bodies and heads with earrings and garlands, lay on the ground by
the thousand. There were gear and seats, poles, frames and fittings, broken
axles and wheels, and chariots smashed in many ways. There were spears, bows and
weapons, great fallen banners, and dead kshatriyas, horses and elephants. In a
moment, lord of the people, the earth was almost impassable and terrible. As the
princes were killed, crying to one another, a great sound arose that deepened
the fear of the cowards, and that sound filled all the quarters, best of the
Bharatas.

"Subhadra's son charged the army, killing horses, chariots and elephants, and
ranged in every direction and every point between, breaking the enemy. Then we
could not see him, covered as he was by the army's dust, as he took the lives of
elephants, horses and men, Bharata. In a moment we saw him again, like the sun
at midday, scorching the hosts of his enemies, great king. In battle Abhimanyu,
the son of Vasava's son, Vasava's equal, shone in the middle of the army, great
king."

"He was a boy," said Dhritarashtra, "who had always lived in great comfort. He
was proud of his strength, which could not be held back, skilled in battle, a
hero of good family, ready to give up his life. As he plunged into the ranks
with his fine three-year-old horses, did any chariot fighter from Yudhishthira's
army follow him?"

"Yudhishthira, Bhimasena, Shikhandi, Satyaki and the twins, Dhrishtadyumna,
Virata and Drupada with the Kekayas, Dhrishtaketu in a fury and the Matsyas
followed him in the battle," said Sanjaya. "They charged to save him, strikers
with their ranks drawn up. When they saw those heroes charging, your men turned
away. When he saw that great army of your son's turned away, your splendid
son-in-law rushed at them to stop them. The king, Jayadratha, the son of the
Saindhava, great king, held back the Parthas with their armies as they strove
for their son. That fierce archer, the great bowman, Vriddhakshatra's son,
calling up a divine weapon, drove them back like elephants from a slope."

"I think too great a load was laid on the Saindhava, Sanjaya," said
Dhritarashtra, "if he alone held back the angry Pandavas as they strove for
their son. I think this strength and courage in the Saindhava a great marvel.
Tell me of the great man's power and his supreme deed. What had he given, or
offered, or sacrificed, or what hard austerity had he practised, that the Sindhu
king alone could hold back the angry Parthas?"

"When Draupadi was carried off," said Sanjaya, "Bhimasena beat him. Out of pride
the king then practised a very great austerity, seeking a boon. He turned his
senses away from all the pleasant things of the senses and bore hunger, thirst
and heat, until he was thin, his veins showing. He worshipped the god Sharva,
chanting the eternal Brahman. The blessed one, who has compassion for those who
love him, took pity on him. At the end of a dream Hara spoke to the son of the
Sindhu king. 'Choose a boon,' he said. 'I am pleased with you, Jayadratha. What
do you want?'

"When Sharva said this, Jayadratha, the Sindhu king, bowed and joined his hands.
With his mind controlled, he said to Rudra, 'Let me alone hold back in battle
all the sons of Pandu, of terrible strength and courage.'

"When he said this, Bharata, the lord of the gods said to Jayadratha, 'I grant
you the boon, gentle one, except for Partha Dhananjaya. You will hold back the
four sons of Pandu in battle.' 'So be it,' the king said to the lord of the
gods, and woke. By that boon and by the strength of his divine weapons he alone
held back the Pandavas' army. At the slap of his bowstring fear came over the
kshatriyas, and your army, the enemy's foe, felt the greatest joy. When the
kshatriyas saw that the whole load had been laid on the Saindhava, they shouted
and charged, king, to where Yudhishthira's army was.

"You ask me, king of kings, about the Sindhu king's courage," said Sanjaya.
"Hear it all, and I will tell you how he fought the Pandavas. Great Sindhu
horses, obedient to their driver, good at pulling, carried him, prancing, as
swift as the wind. His chariot, fitted out by the rules, was like a city of the
gandharvas. His great silver banner, with a boar on it, graced it. With a white
umbrella and flags, and a fan of fly whisks, those marks of a king, he shone
like the lord of the stars in the sky. His screen of iron, adorned with pearls,
diamonds, gems and gold, shone like the sky covered with lights.

"Drawing his great bow and scattering many swarms of arrows, he filled the gap
that Arjuna's son had made. He pierced Satyaki with three arrows, Wolf-belly
with eight, Dhrishtadyumna with sixty and Virata with ten. He pierced Drupada
with five sharp arrows and Shikhandi with ten, the Kekayas with twenty-five and
each of Draupadi's sons with three. He pierced Yudhishthira with seventy, and
then drove back the rest with a great net of arrows. It was a marvel. Then
mighty King Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, laughing, aimed a sharp, yellow
broad arrow at his bow and cut it. In the blink of an eye he took up another bow
and pierced Partha with ten arrows and each of the others with three. Seeing his
quick hand, Bhima again cut his bow, banner and umbrella to the ground at once
with three broad arrows. The mighty man took up another bow, strung it, and
brought down Bhima's banner, bow and horses, sir. With his bow cut and his
horses killed, Bhima leapt from his fine chariot onto Satyaki's, like a lion
onto a mountain top.

"When your men saw the Sindhu king's supreme and unbelievable deed, they were
delighted and cried, 'Well done! Well done!' All beings honoured his deed, that
he alone held back the angry Pandavas by the power of his weapons. The path that
Subhadra's son had shown the Pandavas, by killing elephants with their riders
and weapons, was blocked by the Saindhava. The heroes, the Matsyas, the
Panchalas, the Kekayas and the Pandavas, strove to reach him, one by one. But
whoever of your enemies tried to break Drona's ranks, the Saindhava held him
back by the power of the god's boon.

"When the Pandavas, greedy for victory, had been blocked by the Saindhava, a
very terrible battle broke out between your men and the enemy. Arjuna's son,
true to his word and hard to face, had gone into the army and was churning it
up, full of power, like a sea monster in the ocean. As the tamer of enemies
churned it up with his showers of arrows, the leading men of the Kurus came at
Subhadra's son, one after another. Between him and them, all of boundless power,
loosing showers of arrows without pause, a terrible struggle began. Hemmed in by
those enemies with a crowd of chariots, Arjuna's son killed Vrishasena's
charioteer and cut his bow. The mighty one pierced his horses with straight
arrows, and the horses, galloping like the wind, carried Vrishasena out of the
battle. In that gap Abhimanyu's driver moved his chariot away, and the crowd of
chariots, delighted, cried, 'Well done! Well done!'

"As he came on from far off like an angry lion, crushing his enemies with
arrows, the Vasatiya came up and rushed at him fast. He covered Abhimanyu with
sixty golden-feathered arrows and said, 'You will not get away from me alive
while I live.' Subhadra's son pierced him in the heart, through his iron armour,
with a swift arrow, and he fell lifeless to the ground. When they saw the
Vasatiya killed, the bulls among the kshatriyas surrounded your grandson in a
fury, king, wanting to kill him. They drew their bows of every kind, and the
fight between Subhadra's son and his enemies was fierce. In his anger Phalguna's
son cut their arrows and bows, their bodies, and their heads with earrings and
garlands. Arms were seen cut off, with swords, finger guards, axes and lances,
adorned with golden ornaments. The ground was strewn with garlands, ornaments
and clothes, with fallen great banners, armour, shields, necklaces, crowns,
umbrellas and fly whisks. It was strewn with gear, seats, poles and frames, with
broken axles and wheels and yokes smashed in many ways. It was strewn with
under-frames and flags, with drivers and horses, with broken chariots and dead
elephants. The earth, covered with brave kshatriya lords of many lands, killed
while greedy for victory, was terrible.

"As Abhimanyu ranged in his fury in every direction and every point between, his
form vanished in the battle. Of him we saw only the gold of his armour and his
ornaments, and of his bow and his arrows. No one could look at him with his
eyes, as he stood in the middle taking the lives of warriors with his arrows,
like the sun."

**Divergences.**

* **Abhimanyu's debt.** Abhimanyu calls Duhshasana to account for his words in the assembly hall (2.60–2.71), and says he will pay his debt to Draupadi's anger, to his father and to Bhima (7.39.3–8).
* **Karna's brothers.** Here Abhimanyu kills "Karna's younger brother" (*karṇād anantaraḥ*, 7.39.30; 7.40.4). The day before, Arjuna killed "Karna's younger brother" and two more (7.31.58–60). The CE names neither of the two younger brothers.
* **Shiva's boon.** Jayadratha's penance follows his defeat by Bhima when he tried to carry off Draupadi (3.248–256). Shiva grants that he will hold back "the four sons of Pandu", but not Arjuna (7.41.16–17). There is no "one day" in the CE; that is only in rejected lines (\*303). See ledger L-153.
* **"Your son-in-law."** Jayadratha is Dhritarashtra's son-in-law, husband of Duhshala (7.41.5; 1.108.18).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*282–\*304 and @6. \*291 gives Karna a boast; \*302 says Krishna guards Arjuna.
