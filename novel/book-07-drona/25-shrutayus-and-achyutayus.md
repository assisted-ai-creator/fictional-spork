# Shrutayus and Achyutayus

"When brave Sudakshina and Shrutayudha had been killed, king," said Sanjaya,
"your soldiers rushed angrily at Partha. The Abhishahas, the Shurasenas, the
Shibis and the Vasatis poured rains of arrows on Dhananjaya, king. The Pandava
crushed six thousand of those noble fighters with his arrows. They fled in fear,
like small animals from a tiger. They turned back and surrounded Partha on every
side, as he struck down his rivals in battle, eager to win. As they fell on him,
Dhananjaya quickly struck off their heads and arms with arrows sent from
Gandiva. The ground there was covered with fallen heads, with no space left
between them. Crows, vultures and ravens over the battle made a shade like
cloud.

"As their men were cut down, Shrutayus and Achyutayus, full of anger and rage,
fought Dhananjaya. They were strong, proud and brave, of good family, strong in
the arm. They shot rains of arrows at him from left and right. Hurrying, great
king, seeking great fame, the two bowmen wanted Arjuna dead for your son's sake.
In their anger they filled Arjuna with a thousand smooth-jointed feathered
arrows, as two rainclouds fill a pond.

"Then Shrutayus, best of chariot fighters, in his anger, struck Dhananjaya with
a sharp, well-tempered lance. Pierced deep by his strong enemy, the scourge of
enemies fell into a deep faint, and it threw Keshava into dismay. At that very
moment Achyutayus, the great warrior, struck the Pandava with a very sharp
trident. He was rubbing salt into great-souled Pandu's son's wound. Partha, deep
in pain, leaned on his banner staff.

"Then there was a great lion roar from all your army, lord of the people,
thinking Dhananjaya had been killed. Krishna, deeply pained to see Partha
senseless, comforted Dhananjaya there with friendly words. Then those two best
of chariot fighters, who had found their mark, made Dhananjaya and Vasudeva of
the Vrishnis vanish in the battle under rains of arrows, from every side. The
chariot, with its wheels and its pole, its horses, banner and flags, could not
be seen. It seemed a wonder.

"Slowly, Bharata, Bibhatsu came round, as if he had reached the city of the king
of the dead and come back again. He saw his chariot and Keshava covered by a net
of arrows, and the two enemies facing him like two blazing fires. Then Partha,
the great warrior, called up Shakra's weapon. From it came thousands of
smooth-jointed arrows. They struck the two great bowmen, and the arrows the two
had loosed flew about in the sky, split by Partha's arrows. The Pandava quickly
beat back their arrows with the force of his own, and went on here and there,
fighting the great warriors.

"The floods of Phalguna's arrows left the two without arms or heads, and they
fell to the earth like two trees blown down by the wind. The death of Shrutayus
and the killing of Achyutayus amazed the world, like the drying up of the sea.

"Partha killed the five hundred chariots that followed them, and went on into
the Bharata army, killing the best of the best. When they saw Shrutayus and
Achyutayus killed, Bharata, their sons, Ayutayus and Dirghayus, best of men,
came at Kunti's son in great anger, scattering arrows of every kind, stricken by
their fathers' death. In a moment Arjuna, in great anger, sent them to the house
of Yama with smooth-jointed arrows.

"As he churned up the divisions, like an elephant in a lotus pool, the bulls
among Kshatriyas could not hold Partha back. Then the Angas, thousands of
well-trained elephant riders, came round the Pandava with a troop of elephants,
king, in anger. At Duryodhana's order, the kings of the east and the south, led
by the Kalingas, came with elephants like mountains. As they rushed at him, the
fierce Arjuna cut off their heads with arrows sent by Gandiva, and their arms in
their fine ornaments. The ground was strewn with those heads and with arms
wearing armlets, and looked like golden stones covered with snakes. Arms cut off
by the arrows, and heads torn away, were seen falling like birds from trees.
Elephants pierced with thousands of arrows, streaming blood, looked like
mountains in the rains running with water red with ochre.

"Others lay killed by Bibhatsu's sharp arrows, mlecchas who had ridden on the
elephants' backs, strange and misshapen to look at. They wore every kind of
dress, king, and carried floods of every kind of weapon. Their limbs smeared
with blood, they gleamed, killed by the bright arrows. Thousands of elephants,
struck by Partha's arrows, spewed blood, their bodies cut, with their riders and
the men who ran at their feet. They screamed and fell, and others wandered in
every direction. In great terror, the elephants trampled their own men in many
places, maddened elephants carrying fighters with their weapons, like sharp
poison.

"There were Yavanas, Paradas and Shakas, with the Sunikas, very dreadful, with
dreadful eyes, who knew the magic of the asuras. There were mlecchas sprung from
the cow, like Death, hard strikers, and Darvabhisaras, Daradas and Pundras, with
the Bahlikas. Their troops could not be counted, by the hundred thousand. Their
rain of arrows was like a swarm of locusts coming on.

"Dhananjaya made a shade like cloud over their army with his arrows. With the
magic of his weapons he cut down all the mlecchas gathered there, shaven and
half-shaven and matted-haired, unclean, with hairy faces. Pierced by arrows in
their hundreds, those troops who moved in troops, the dwellers in mountain
caves, fled in fear from the battle. Ravens, herons and wolves gladly drank on
the ground the blood of the mlecchas who had fallen from elephants and horses,
struck by hundreds of arrows.

"He set flowing a fierce river, running with waves of blood. Foot soldiers,
horses, chariots and elephants were the bridges built across it, rains of arrows
were its rafts, hair was its moss and grass. Helmets were its little fish. He
made it at the end of an age, gathered by Time, crowded with elephants, a river
running high with blood from the bodies of princes and of those who rode
elephants, horses and chariots. As, when Vasava rains, there is no high ground
or low, so the whole earth was flooded with blood.

"The bull among Kshatriyas sent six thousand fine Kshatriya heroes, and another
thousand, to the world of death. Elephants, properly fitted out, pierced by
thousands of arrows, lay on the ground like mountains struck by thunder. Arjuna
moved about killing horses, chariots and elephants, like an elephant in rut
trampling a bed of reeds. A fire driven by the wind burns up a forest full of
trees, creepers and bushes, dry wood, grass and reeds. So the Pandava fire,
Dhananjaya, driven by the wind that was Krishna, burned up the forest of your
army with flames that were arrows, in his anger. Emptying the chariot boxes and
strewing the ground with men, Dhananjaya, bow in hand, seemed to dance in the
crush. Making the earth run with blood with his arrows like thunderbolts,
Dhananjaya went on into the Bharata army in anger.

"As he went, Shrutayus, the Ambashtha king, blocked him. Arjuna quickly brought
down his horses with sharp heron-feathered arrows as he struggled, sir. With
other arrows Partha cut his bow, and pressed on. The Ambashtha, his eyes wild
with anger, took up a mace and came at Partha and the great warrior Keshava in
the battle. Then the hero laughed, Bharata, raised his mace, blocked the chariot
with it and struck Keshava.

"Seeing Keshava struck by the mace, Arjuna, killer of enemy heroes, turned in
great anger on the Ambashtha, Bharata. He covered that best of chariot fighters,
mace and all, with gold-feathered arrows in the battle, as a cloud covers the
risen sun. Then with other arrows Partha smashed the great man's mace to pieces.
It seemed a wonder. When he saw it had fallen, he took up another great mace and
struck Arjuna and Vasudeva again and again. With two razor arrows Arjuna cut off
his raised arms, holding the mace, like the poles of Indra's banner, and with
another feathered arrow his head. He fell dead, king, and the earth rang with
it, like the pole of Indra's banner let go, its ropes loosed from the frame.

"Then Partha, plunged into the chariot host, surrounded by hundreds of elephants
and horses, was seen like the sun covered by clouds."

**Divergences.**

* **Two men called Shrutayus.** The brother of Achyutayus (7.68.7–24) and the Ambashtha king (7.68.56–65) are both called Shrutayus. Neither is the Kalinga Shrutayus killed on the second day (6.50), and neither is Shrutayudha, killed in the last chapter.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*492–\*499. \*493 adds Dravidas among the troops with insulting epithets. \*496 describes the river of blood at greater length.
