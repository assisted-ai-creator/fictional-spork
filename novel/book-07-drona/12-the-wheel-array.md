# The Wheel Array

"The five Pandavas with Krishna, of terrible deeds in battle," said Sanjaya,
"whose toil shows in what they have done, are hard to face even for the gods. In
courage, deeds, birth, wisdom, nature, fame and fortune, there has never been
and never will be a man with virtues equal to Krishna's. King Yudhishthira,
devoted to truth and dharma, generous, with virtues such as the honouring of
brahmins, has always won heaven, they say. Death at the end of an age, mighty
Jamadagni's son, and Bhimasena standing in battle: these three are called alike,
king. For Partha, the bearer of the Gandiva, skilled at making his promises come
true in battle, I can find no equal on earth. Great love for his elders,
modesty, good manners, self-control, matchless beauty and bravery: these six are
fixed in Nakula. In learning, depth, sweetness, strength, courage and daring,
the hero Sahadeva is the equal of the two divine Ashvins, they say. All the rich
virtues in Krishna and all the virtues in the Pandavas are seen gathered
together in Abhimanyu alone, they say. He was Yudhishthira's equal in steadiness
and Krishna's in conduct, the equal in deeds of Bhimasena of terrible deeds. He
was Dhananjaya's equal in beauty, courage and learning, and the equal of
Sahadeva and Nakula in good manners."

"I want to hear all of it, charioteer," said Dhritarashtra, "how Abhimanyu,
Subhadra's son, who was never beaten, was killed in battle."

"The teacher made the wheel array, great king," said Sanjaya, "and placed all
the kings in it, each like Shakra. All the princes were massed together, each
bound by a pact, with banners of worked gold. All of them wore red clothes and
red ornaments. All had red flags, and all wore golden garlands. There were ten
thousand of them, with strong bows, and they put your grandson in front, the
handsome Lakshmana. They shared each other's pain and each other's daring. They
vied with one another and delighted in each other's good. King Duryodhana,
surrounded by the great chariot fighters Karna, Duhshasana and Kripa, splendid
as the king of the gods, sat under a white umbrella, fanned with fly whisks,
like the rising sun. At the front of that army, whose leader was Drona, stood
the Sindhu king, splendid and as still as Mount Meru. At the Sindhu king's side
stood thirty of your sons, great king, like the thirty gods, led by Ashvatthama.
The Gandhara king, the gambler, Shalya and Bhurishravas, great chariot fighters,
shone at the Sindhu king's side.

"The Parthas, led by Bhimasena, came on against that invincible army guarded by
Bharadvaja's son. Satyaki and Chekitana came, Dhrishtadyumna Parshata, brave
Kuntibhoja and the great chariot fighter Drupada. Arjuna's son came,
Kshatradharman and mighty Brihatkshatra, Dhrishtaketu, lord of the Chedis, the
two sons of Madri and Ghatotkacha. Brave Yudhamanyu came, and unbeaten
Shikhandi, invincible Uttamaujas and the great chariot fighter Virata. The sons
of Draupadi came in their fury, and the mighty son of Shishupala, the very
mighty Kekayas and the Srinjayas by the thousand. These and others, with their
companies, masters of weapons mad for battle, rushed at once at Bharadvaja's
son, eager to fight. As they came on together, mighty Bharadvaja's son held them
all back, unshaken, with a great flood of arrows. Like great floods of water
meeting a mountain that cannot be split, like lakes meeting their shore, they
could not get past Drona. Tormented by the arrows loosed from Drona's bow, king,
the Pandavas could not stand in front of Bharadvaja's son. We saw the marvellous
strength of Drona's arms, that the Panchalas and the Srinjayas could not get
past him.

"When Yudhishthira saw Drona coming on in fury, he thought hard about how to
hold Drona back. Thinking that no one else could stand up to Drona, Yudhishthira
laid that heavy, unbearable load on Subhadra's son. He said this to Abhimanyu,
the killer of enemy heroes, no less than Vasudeva, of power as boundless as
Phalguna's:

"'Act so that Arjuna does not reproach us when he comes back, my son. We do not
know how to break the wheel array at all. You, or Arjuna, or Krishna, or
Pradyumna could break the wheel array, strong-armed one. There is no fifth.
Abhimanyu, my son, you should grant the boon your fathers, your uncles and all
the soldiers ask of you. For Dhananjaya might reproach us when he comes back
from the battle, my son. Take up your weapons quickly and cut down Drona's
ranks.'

"'I will plunge into Drona's firm, steady, finest army in battle, and break it,
wanting victory for my fathers,' said Abhimanyu. 'My father taught me how to
break into this army. But I cannot get out if some danger arises.'

"'Break the army, best of fighters, and make a gateway for us,' said
Yudhishthira. 'We will follow you, my son, wherever you go. In battle we hold
you equal to Dhananjaya, my son. We will follow you with care, guarding you on
every side.'

"'I will follow you,' said Bhima, 'and Dhrishtadyumna and Satyaki, the
Panchalas, the Kekayas, the Matsyas and all the Prabhadrakas. Once you have
broken the array, we will smash it again and again, here and there, killing the
best of them.'

"'I will go into Drona's army, so hard to reach,' said Abhimanyu, 'like an angry
moth into a blazing fire. Today I will do the deed that is good for both
families, which will please my uncle and my father. Today all beings will see
the enemy armies driven in their masses by one child in battle.'

"'As you speak so, son of Subhadra, may your strength grow,' said Yudhishthira.
'You dare to break Drona's army, so hard to break. It is guarded by tigers among
men, great archers and strikers like the Sadhyas, the Rudras and the Maruts,
with the courage of the Vasus, Agni and the Adityas.'

"When he heard these words," said Sanjaya, "he urged on his charioteer:
'Sumitra, drive the horses quickly at Drona's ranks.' When Subhadra's son heard
the wise King of Dharma's words, Bharata, he urged his charioteer on at Drona's
ranks. As he urged him on, 'Go! Go!', the charioteer said to Abhimanyu, king:

"'The Pandavas have laid too great a load on you, long-lived one. Think it
through with your mind and see what you can bear before you fight. For the
teacher Drona is skilled, and has worked hard at the greatest weapons. You have
grown up in great comfort, though you are skilled in battle.'

"Then Abhimanyu laughed and said to the charioteer, 'Charioteer, who is this
Drona? Or the whole kshatra, for that matter? I would fight Shakra on Airavata,
with the hosts of the immortals, at the front of the battle. I have no wonder at
the kshatriyas today. This enemy army is not worth a sixteenth part of me. Even
if I met Vishnu, who conquered all, my uncle, charioteer, and my father Arjuna
in battle, fear would not come near me.'

"Then Abhimanyu brushed aside the charioteer's words and said only, 'Drive at
Drona's army, and quickly.' The charioteer, none too happy, urged on his
three-year-old horses, with their golden harness and trappings. Driven by
Sumitra at Drona's ranks, the horses charged at Drona, king, with great speed
and force. When they saw him coming, all the Kauravas, led by Drona, turned to
face him, and the Pandavas followed him.

"Arjuna's son, finer even than Arjuna, had a tall banner bearing the finest
karnikara tree, and golden armour. Eager for battle, he came up against the
great chariot fighters led by Drona, like a lion cub against elephants. They
stood ready at twenty paces and began to strike. For a moment there was a
whirlpool, like the Ganga's meeting the sea. As the heroes fought and killed one
another, a wild and terrible battle began, king. As that fearful battle went on,
Arjuna's son broke the array under Drona's very eyes and went in.

"As he went in, killing his enemies in their midst, masses of elephants, horses,
chariots and foot soldiers surrounded that mighty man with their weapons raised.
With the din of every kind of instrument, with shouts, cries and roars, with the
grunt of 'Hum!' and lions' roars, with cries of 'Stand! Stand!', with fierce
yells of 'Don't go! Stand! Come to me! I am here, over there!' said again and
again, with the trumpeting of elephants, the jingle of bells, laughter and the
sound of hooves and wheels, they rushed at Arjuna's son, making the earth ring.
As they charged, the hero, quick with his weapons, knowing the vital parts,
killed them in their companies with arrows that split the vital parts, quickly
and hard.

"Struck like that by sharp arrows of every kind, they still fell on him in the
battle, like moths on a flame. With their bodies and the parts of their bodies
he quickly strewed the ground, as the altar at a sacrifice is strewn with kusha
grass. Arjuna's son cut off the arms of your men by the thousand. They were
round arms, with their iguana-skin finger guards tied on. They held bows, arrows
and guards, swords and shields, goads and reins, lances and axes. They held iron
balls, iron-mouthed darts and spears, lances, javelins and axes, slings, iron
bars, spears and fine kampanas. They held whips and great conches, kuntas and
grips on the hair, hammers and missiles, nooses, bars and stones. They wore
armlets and bracelets, and were anointed with sweet-smelling ointment. Writhing
and covered in blood, great king, they looked like five-headed snakes cut to
pieces by Garuda, sir.

"Phalguna's son covered the earth with the heads of his enemies. They had fine
noses, faces and hair, no wounds, lovely earrings, and lips bitten in anger, and
they streamed with blood. They had fine garlands, crowns and turbans, bright
with gems and jewels, like lotuses without their stalks, shining like the sun
and the moon. Many of them, sweet-smelling, had once spoken kind and pleasant
words in their time.

"He was seen in every direction cutting to pieces with his arrows chariots
fitted out by the rules, like cities of the gandharvas. Their fronts, their
three bamboos, their poles and frames were scattered. Their shafts, poles and
axles were gone, and they had no rims or spokes. Their wheels, fittings and
seats were broken, their gear smashed, and their warriors killed by the
thousand. Again, with his sharp-edged, sharp-pointed arrows, he cut down the
enemy's elephants and elephant riders. He cut down their flags, goads and
banners, quivers and armour, girths, neck chains and blankets, bells, trunks and
tusks, and the foot guards who followed them.

"He shone as he brought down your fine horses, forest-bred and mountain-bred,
Kambojas, Arattas and Bahlikas. They had steady tails, ears and eyes, and were
swift and good at pulling. Trained warriors rode them well, fighting with spear,
lance and dart. Their fly whisks and saddlecloths were torn, their trappings
scattered, their tongues and eyes thrust out, their entrails, livers and lungs
spilled. Their riders were killed, their harness broken, and they delighted the
hosts of flesh-eaters. Their armour was cut away, and they were soaked in dung,
urine and blood.

"Alone, like Vishnu, beyond thought, having first done a deed that was hard to
do, he churned up your great army of three parts. Then he killed your masses of
foot soldiers, Bharata. When your men and your sons saw that army badly battered
by Subhadra's son alone with his sharp arrows, like the army of the demons by
Skanda, they looked in all ten directions. Their mouths were dry, their eyes
darting, they were sweating and their hair stood on end. Eager to run and with
no spirit to beat the enemy, wanting to live, they called to one another by
family name and personal name. They left their sons, fathers, friends, relatives
and kin lying dead, and fled, urging on their horses and elephants."

**Divergences.**

* **"I cannot get out."** Abhimanyu says his father taught him how to break into the array, "but I cannot get out if some danger arises" (7.34.19). The popular story that he learned it in his mother's womb, from Krishna's lips, is a rejected star passage (\*259). See ledger L-39.
* **"There is no fifth."** Yudhishthira names four who could break the wheel: Abhimanyu, Arjuna, Krishna and Pradyumna (7.34.15).
* **The charioteer.** Abhimanyu's charioteer is named Sumitra (7.34.29; 7.35.10).
* **Jayadratha at the front.** The Sindhu king stands at the front of the array (7.33.18), where he will hold back the Pandavas (7.39–7.43; ledger L-153).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*251–\*270. \*254 describes the array as a lotus, with Duryodhana at its centre; \*262 has Abhimanyu swear not to be his parents' son if anyone escapes him.
