# Arjuna Breaks the Front

"When the armies had been drawn up, sir, and had raised their shout," said
Sanjaya, "the war drums were beaten and the tabors sounded. The armies roared
and the instruments rang out, the conches were blown, and the din made the hair
stand on end. The Bharatas, eager to fight, slowly moved forward. Then, at a
dreadful hour, the Left-Handed Archer was seen.

"Many thousands of crows and ravens played in front of the Left-Handed Archer,
Bharata. Beasts with a dreadful cry, and jackals, a sight of ill omen, howled on
our right as we went forward. Blazing meteors fell on every side with a crash of
thunder, and the whole earth shook, as a dreadful fear arose. Harsh winds blew
from all sides, with thunder, raining grit, as Kunti's son came on and the
battle began.

"Nakula's son Shatanika and Dhrishtadyumna, Prishata's grandson, both wise men,
drew up the Pandava divisions.

"Then your son Durmarshana, with a thousand chariots, a hundred elephants, three
thousand horse and ten thousand foot, took his place in front of the whole army,
fifteen hundred bow-lengths ahead. He said, 'Today I will hold back the Gandiva
bowman, the scorcher, wild in battle, as the shore holds back the sea. Today let
them see Dhananjaya, hard to face and never willing to bear a wrong, stuck fast
against me in battle, like one rock against another.' Speaking so, great king,
the great-souled, high-minded great bowman took his place, surrounded by great
bowmen.

"Then Arjuna came, like the Ender in his anger, like Vasava with his
thunderbolt, like Death with his staff, not to be withstood, sent on by Time. He
was like the holder of the trident, not to be shaken, like Varuna with his
noose, like the fire at the end of the age, about to burn up all creatures
again. Tossed up by anger, rage and strength, the destroyer of the
Nivatakavachas, Jaya the conqueror, standing by the truth and set on fulfilling
his great vow, wore his armour and his sword. He wore a diadem of gold, white
armour and clothes, fine armlets and beautiful earrings. Standing in his fine
chariot, Nara, who follows Narayana, shook Gandiva in the battle and shone like
the risen sun.

"The mighty Dhananjaya stopped his ready chariot an arrow's shot from the great
front line and blew his conch. Then Krishna too, unshaken, sir, blew with all
his strength Panchajanya, the best of conches, together with Partha. At the
sound of the two conches, lord of the people, your troops stood with their hair
on end, trembling, their wits gone. As all creatures shake at the sound of a
thunderbolt, so your soldiers shook at the sound of the conches. The animals
everywhere let fall their dung and urine. So the whole army, animals and all,
was in turmoil. Men sank down at the sound of the conches, king, sir. Some
fainted, king, and some were terrified.

"Then the monkey, with the creatures that lived on the banner, gave a great cry
with its mouth gaping, and frightened your soldiers. Then the conches and war
drums, tabors and kettledrums were beaten again, to hearten your army. Among the
din of instruments of every kind, among the war cries and the slapping of arms,
the lion roars and the music, the great warriors were challenged.

"In that tumult of sound, which made the fearful more afraid, the son of the
Punisher of Paka said to the Dasharha, full of joy, 'Drive the horses,
Hrishikesha, to where Durmarshana stands. I will break this mass of elephants
and go into the enemy host.' Told this by the Left-Handed Archer, strong-armed
Keshava drove the horses to where Durmarshana stood.

"A clash began then, wild and very cruel, one man against many, and chariots,
elephants and men were destroyed. Then Partha poured arrows on his enemies like
rain from a raincloud, as a cloud pours rain on the mountains. All those chariot
fighters too, quick and skilled of hand, poured nets of arrows on Krishna and
Dhananjaya. Then the strong-armed Partha, angry at being held back in battle,
cut the heads of the chariot fighters from their bodies with his arrows.

"The ground was strewn with handsome faces, eyes rolling, lips bitten, and with
heads in earrings and helmets. The warriors' faces lay scattered everywhere and
looked like lotus beds torn apart. Bright with gold and wet with blood, they
looked like masses of cloud lit with lightning, king. The sound of heads falling
on the ground was like the sound of palm fruits falling, ripened by time, king.

"Then a headless trunk would stand holding its bow, and another would draw its
sword, raise it in its arm and stand. Bulls among men, who could not bear
Kunti's son and were greedy for victory, did not know that their heads had
fallen on the ground. The earth was strewn with horses' heads and elephants'
trunks, and with the arms and heads of heroes.

"'This is Partha! Where is Partha? There is Partha!' my lord. Among the fighters
of your army everything seemed to have become Partha. Some struck each other,
and some struck themselves. Deluded by Time, they thought the whole world had
become Partha. Many heroes lay groaning, bloody, senseless, in deep pain,
calling the names of their friends.

"Arms lay in the battle like great snakes, like iron bars. They still held
javelins, lances, spears, swords and axes, bows, arrows and maces, and wore
their armour, their ornaments and their armlets. Cut off by the fine arrows,
they writhed and twisted and coiled everywhere, and jerked about in fury.
Whatever man stood up to Partha in battle, a deadly arrow went into his body.

"As Partha danced along the chariot tracks and drew his bow, no one there saw
the smallest gap in him. The enemy were amazed at the speed of Pandu's son, as
he strove and shot his arrows quickly. Phalguna split with his arrows elephant
and elephant driver, horse and horseman, chariot fighter and charioteer. Turning
round, turned back, fighting or standing in front of him, there was no one
Pandu's son did not kill. As the rising sun in the sky destroys the great dark,
so Arjuna slew the elephant host with his heron-feathered arrows.

"Your army, with its elephants fallen and split open, looked like the earth at
the end of time, strewn with fallen mountains. As the midday sun is always hard
for living things to look at, so angry Dhananjaya in battle was hard for his
enemies to look at. So your son's army in the battle, tamer of enemies, was
broken, running, in turmoil, badly hurt by the arrows. Driven off like a mass of
cloud scattered by a great wind, that army could not even look back.

"Your men fled fast, horsemen, chariot fighters and foot, harried by Arjuna.
They urged on their horses with goads and the tips of their bows, with shouts
and good driving, with whips, kicks of the heel and fierce words. Others drove
their elephants with heels, toes and hooks. Others, confused by the arrows, went
straight at him. Your fighters had lost heart then, and their minds were
reeling."

Dhritarashtra said, "When the front of the army was broken and being killed by
the diadem-wearer, which heroes went against Dhananjaya in that battle? Or did
they give up their purpose, go into the cart array and stand there, relying on
Drona as a wall, safe from all danger?"

Sanjaya said, "Your army had been so broken by Arjuna. Its heroes were killed,
its heart was gone, and it was looking for a chance to run, while the son of the
Punisher of Paka kept striking it with his fine arrows. No one there in the
battle could look at Arjuna. Then your son Duhshasana, king, seeing the army in
that state, went against Arjuna to fight, full of anger. He was covered in
armour bright with gold and wore a helmet of gold, a brave man of fierce
courage. With a great mass of elephants, as if swallowing the earth, Duhshasana
surrounded the Left-Handed Archer, great king.

"With the clang of the elephants' bells, the blare of the conches, the twang of
bowstrings and the trumpeting of the tuskers, the earth, the quarters and the
sky were filled with sound. For a while it was dreadful and cruel.

"Dhananjaya, a lion among men, saw them rushing at him, driven on by hooks,
trunks hanging, furious, like mountains with wings. With a great lion roar he
cut down the enemy's elephant host all round with his arrows. Like a sea monster
going into the great ocean, its great waves whipped up by the wind, the
diadem-wearer went into that elephant host. Partha, conqueror of enemy cities,
was seen in every direction, like the sun blazing beyond its bounds at the end
of an age.

"The horses' hooves and the rumble of the wheels, the shouting and the twang of
bowstrings, the blare of Devadatta and the thunder of Gandiva made the elephants
lose their wits and slow. The Left-Handed Archer split them open with arrows
whose touch was like the bite of poisonous snakes. The elephants in battle,
struck in every limb by hundreds of thousands of sharp arrows driven by Gandiva,
gave a great scream as the diadem-wearer killed them. They fell to the ground
without end, like mountains with their wings cut off. Other elephants, their
arrows set in their tusk sockets, their brows and their temples, cried again and
again like cranes.

"With his smooth-jointed broad arrows the diadem-wearer cut off the heads of the
men riding on the elephants' necks. As the heads fell to the ground in their
earrings, it was as if Partha were making an offering of heaps of lotuses. Men
tied into their seats, their armour gone, sick with wounds and soaked in blood,
hung from the elephants as they wandered about the battle. Some were pierced by
a single well-shot feathered arrow, two or three at once, and fell to the
ground.

"With his smooth-jointed broad arrows he cut to pieces the bowstrings, bows and
banners, the yokes and the poles of the chariot fighters. He was not seen fixing
an arrow, or drawing, or letting go, or taking one from the quiver. Partha was
seen only dancing, his bow drawn into a circle. Other elephants, pierced deep
with iron arrows, spewing blood from their mouths, fell to the ground in a
moment. Countless headless trunks were seen standing up all round, great king,
in that great crush. Arms were seen cut off in the battle, holding bows, in
finger guards, with swords and armlets, bright with golden ornaments.

"There were fittings and platforms, poles, shafts and chariot boxes, wheels
smashed, axles broken, yokes in many pieces. Armour, bows and arrows were
scattered here and there, with garlands, ornaments and clothes, and great fallen
banners. With the killed elephants and horses, and the Kshatriyas struck down,
the earth there was dreadful to see.

"So Duhshasana's army, great king, killed by the diadem-wearer, fled in pain,
leader and all. Then Duhshasana, terrified, hurt by the arrows, went with his
troops to the cart array, hoping for Drona to save him."

**Divergences.**

* Rejected lines not narrated: \*459–\*476. \*476 has Arjuna shoot Duhshasana with ten arrows and taunt him with his insults to Draupadi. \*465 has a trunk dancing with a head held by the hair. \*467 calls Duhshasana the crown prince.
* **The omens.** The CE says the jackals howled "on our right" (7.64.5). \*460, which spells out that the omens were bad for the Kauravas and good for Partha, is rejected. The novel says only "a sight of ill omen", as the text does (*aśivadarśanāḥ*).

**Choices.**

* 7.64.9 *padātīnāṃ śataiḥ śataiḥ*: "hundreds upon hundreds" of foot, taken as ten thousand.
* 7.64.10 *adhyardhamātre dhanuṣāṃ sahasre*: "fifteen hundred bow-lengths ahead".
* 7.64.16 *jayo jetā*: "Jaya the conqueror"; Jaya is one of Arjuna's names. *nivātakavacāntakaḥ*: "the destroyer of the Nivatakavachas", the demons he killed for Indra in Book 3.
* 7.64.18 *naro nārāyaṇānugaḥ*: "Nara, who follows Narayana".
* 7.64.25 *saha bhūtair dhvajālayaiḥ*: "with the creatures that lived on the banner".
* 7.64.27: the verse lists sounds without a main verb. It is closed with "the great warriors were challenged" (*samāhūtair mahārathaiḥ*).
* 7.64.28 *pākaśāsaniḥ*: "the son of the Punisher of Paka", Indra's son.

* 7.65.13 *kāṣṭhātīta ivādityaḥ*: "like the sun blazing beyond its bounds".
* 7.65.20 *padmānām iva saṃghātaiḥ pārthaś cakre nivedanam*: "as if Partha were making an offering of heaps of lotuses".
