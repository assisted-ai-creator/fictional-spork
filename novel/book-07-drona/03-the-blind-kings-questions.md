summary: Dhritarashtra cannot believe that Drona, master of every weapon, has been killed by Dhrishtadyumna. He asks how it happened, and who guarded Drona's wheels, and then faints. When he comes round he asks, hero by hero, who held back each of the Pandava warriors who went for Drona, from Yudhishthira and Bhima to Arjuna, the twins, Satyaki, Abhimanyu, Ghatotkacha and the rest. Then he turns to Krishna, and lists his deeds: the demons he killed as a boy and a man, Kamsa, Jarasandha and the king of Chedi, the lands he conquered, the conch Panchajanya and the discus. With Krishna and Arjuna on their side, Nara and Narayana, he says, the Pandavas cannot lose. Fate is stronger than men's plans, and he asks Sanjaya to tell him everything as it happened.

# The Blind King's Questions

"What was Drona doing in battle," said Dhritarashtra, "when the Pandavas and the
Srinjayas killed him? He was so skilled with weapons, even among all who bear
arms. Did his chariot break? Did his bow shatter as he shot? Or was Drona off
his guard, and so met his death? How did Parshata kill him, my son? He was so
hard for enemies to overcome. He scattered arrows with golden feathers in
swarms. He was quick of hand, the best of the twice-born, skilled and a master
of every way of fighting. He shot far, he was self-controlled, and he had gone
to the far shore of the fighting with weapons. He was doing terrible work in the
battle, the great chariot fighter, and he was on his guard. How did the son of
the Panchala king kill that unfallen hero, so lucky in his boon?

"It is plain to me that fate is stronger than human effort, since brave Drona
has been killed by great Parshata. The four kinds of weapon lore were all there
in that hero. You tell me that Drona, the teacher of the finest archery, is
dead. When I hear that the warrior of the golden chariot, with its tiger-skin
cover and its ornaments of gold, has been killed, I cannot drive away my grief
today. No one, surely, dies of another's sorrow, Sanjaya, since I hear that
Drona is dead, and I live on and do not die. My heart must be made of stone, and
very hard, since it does not break into a hundred pieces at the news that Drona
has been killed. Brahmins and princes sat at his feet for his virtues, to learn
the Veda and the use of weapons. How has Death taken him? I cannot bear the fall
of Drona, any more than the drying up of the ocean, the moving of Meru or the
falling of the sun. He held back the proud and protected the righteous. That
tamer of enemies would give up even his life for a man in need. My foolish sons
put their hope of victory in his courage. In wisdom he was the equal of
Brihaspati and Ushanas. How has he been killed?

"And what of those great red horses of Sindh, with their golden garlands? They
were swift as the wind when they were yoked to his chariot, and louder than all
the noise of battle. They were strong and neighing, well trained and good at
pulling. Did they not falter in the thick of the fighting? They could bear the
trumpeting of elephants in battle, the sound of conches and drums, the twang of
bowstrings, the showers of arrows and the clash of weapons. They longed to beat
the enemy, and they had mastered their breath and their pain. The swift, eager
horses that drew Bharadvaja's son, yoked to his golden chariot and handled by a
hero among men, how did they not break through the army of the Pandavas, my son?
What did the brave son of Bharadvaja do in battle, mounted on his fine chariot
adorned with gold, he who made the enemy cry out? What did strong Drona do in
battle, true to his word, whose learning all the archers in the world live by?
He was the best of bowmen, a great man, like Shakra in heaven. What chariot
fighters went out against him in battle, when he was doing terrible deeds?

"Surely, when the Pandavas saw the golden chariot, they ran, as he spread his
divine weapons and wore down their army without tiring? Or did the King of
Dharma, with his brothers and his whole army, with Panchala's son holding the
reins, close in on Drona from every side? Surely Partha held back the other
chariot fighters with his straight arrows, and then Parshata, who does evil
deeds, went up against Drona. For I can see no one else who could kill that
fiery man except fierce Dhrishtadyumna, guarded by the diademed one. The teacher
was surrounded on every side by those heroes, by the Kekayas, the Chedis and the
Karushas, the Matsyas and other kings. I think the wretch of the Panchalas then
killed him while he was busy with a hard deed and hemmed in, as ants bring down
a snake.

"He had studied all four Vedas, with the stories as a fifth. He was the support
of brahmins, as the ocean is of rivers. How did that old brahmin die by the
sword? He was quick to anger, yet he bore it when I troubled Kunti's son again
and again, though Kunti's son did not deserve it. This is the fruit of that
deed. All the archers in the world live by his work. How was that true,
righteous man killed by men who wanted wealth? He was the best, like Shakra in
heaven, of great spirit and great strength. How was he killed by the Parthas,
like a whale by little fish? He was quick of hand and strong, with a firm bow, a
crusher of enemies. No one who wanted to live stayed alive once he came into his
range. Two sounds never left him while he lived: the sound of the Veda for those
who love the Veda, and the twang of the bowstring for those who bear the bow.

"I cannot bear that Drona is dead, whose courage was like a lion's or an
elephant's. How, Sanjaya, when he was so hard to overcome, and his fame and
strength so hard to assail? Who guarded the great man's right wheel in the
battle, and who his left? Who fought in front of the hero? Which heroes there
gave up their bodies and went face to face with death? Which heroes showed the
greatest steadiness for Drona in the battle? This is what a noble man must do in
hard times, Sanjaya: fight with all his might. And that was firmly in him. My
mind is failing, my son. Stop the story for now. When I come to myself again,
Sanjaya, I will ask you more."

When he had questioned the charioteer's son like this, said Vaishampayana,
Dhritarashtra, deeply wounded by the grief in his heart and hopeless of his
sons' victory, fell to the ground. When he lay there senseless, his attendants
sprinkled him with very cold water and fanned him with sweet-smelling fans. When
they saw that he had fallen, the Bharata women gathered round the great king on
every side and stroked him with their hands. The fine women, with tears in their
throats, lifted the king gently from the ground and brought him to his seat.
When he reached his seat, the king was still overcome by his faint. He stayed
there without moving, and they fanned him on every side. Slowly the king came to
himself, trembling, and he questioned the charioteer, the son of Gavalgana,
again about what had happened.

"Ajatashatru came on like the rising sun, driving away the darkness with his
light," said Dhritarashtra. "Who held him back from Drona? He was like an
elephant in rut, angry and swift, his mind set on its purpose, blazing. Such an
elephant kills any that stands against it, and no rival leader of the herd can
beat him when he goes to meet the she-elephants. That hero, the best of men,
went beyond the other warriors in battle. Strong-armed, steady and true to his
word, he could by himself burn up the whole army of Duryodhana with his terrible
eye. He kills with his eye, he was set on victory, he was guarded by the best of
archers, he was self-controlled and honoured in the world. What heroes
surrounded him? Which of my men went up against the king, Kunti's son, the tiger
among men, the best of archers, unfallen and so hard to overcome?

"Bhimasena rushed at Drona with great speed. What heroes surrounded him as he
came?

"When the chariot of great power came on like a cloud, and Bibhatsu, like the
god of rain, loosed his terrible thunderbolt, what were your thoughts then? He
rained down showers of arrows, as Maghavan rains down rain. The one with the
monkey banner filled the sky with arrows and made all the quarters echo with the
slap of his palm on the string and the rumble of his wheels. He was a terrible
storm cloud. His bow was the lightning, his chariot ranks were the clouds, the
rumble of his wheels was the thunder, and the sound of his arrows was its roar.
His fury outdid the rain clouds, and he moved as swift as thought. His rain was
arrows that pierced to the vital parts, his water was blood, and he flooded the
whole earth and spread it with men. With the thunder of maces, fierce, called up
by Duryodhana, Vijaya drenched the battle with sharp arrows winged with
vultures' feathers, holding the Gandiva. What were your thoughts then? Did your
army not fall apart at the sound of the Gandiva, when Arjuna came at you, making
that terrible roar? Did Dhananjaya not drive you away from Drona with his
arrows, as the wind blows the clouds apart, as he swept through like a wind in a
forest of reeds? What man can stand against the one who bears the Gandiva in
battle? When the armies shook and fear took hold of the heroes, who did not
leave Drona there? Which cowards fled in fear? Or which gave up their bodies and
went face to face with death, with Dhananjaya, who has beaten even beings more
than human in battle? My men will not stand the speed of the one with white
horses, or the roar of the Gandiva, like the thunder of clouds in the rains.
That chariot, which has Vishvaksena to drive it and Dhananjaya to fight in it,
cannot be beaten, I think, even by the gods and the demons.

"The Pandava is young and tender, brave and handsome, wise, skilled and clever,
with courage that holds true in battle. When wise Nakula came at you, with a
great roar that shook all the Kauravas, what heroes surrounded him? Sahadeva
came at you, angry as a poisonous snake, a conqueror hard to beat in battle,
wreaking havoc on the enemy. He keeps a noble vow, his arrows never miss, and he
is modest and never beaten. What heroes surrounded him as he went at Drona?

"He crushed the great army of the king of the Sauviras and carried off his
lovely queen, beautiful in every limb. Truth, steadiness and courage, and
perfect chastity, are always there in Yuyudhana, that bull among men. He is
strong, true in his deeds, never downcast, never beaten, Vasudeva's equal in
battle and next after Vasudeva. He has served Dhananjaya and learned from him as
from a teacher, and he is Partha's equal with weapons. Who held him back from
Drona? He is the best of the Vrishnis, a hero, brave among all bowmen, the equal
of Rama in weapons, fame and courage. Truth, steadiness and self-control,
courage and the highest chastity are all in the Satvata, as the three worlds are
in Keshava. When that great archer, so rich in virtues and hard to hold back
even for the gods, came up, what heroes surrounded him?

"The best of the Panchalas is brave and dear to the best families. He always
does the best deeds, and his power in battle is the best. He is devoted to
Dhananjaya's good, and the best at doing me harm. He is like Yama, Vaishravana,
the sun, great Indra and Varuna, and famed as a great chariot fighter. He went
up against Drona in battle, ready to give his life in the turmoil. What heroes
surrounded him? Dhrishtaketu, who alone left the Chedis and took the side of the
Pandavas, who held him back from Drona as he came? Who held back brave Ketuman
from Drona, who killed Prince Sudarshana at the mountain gate of the far west?

"Shikhandi, Yajnasena's son, that tiger among men, was born a woman. He knows
what is good and what is bad, and his spirit never flags in battle. He was the
cause of great Devavrata's death in battle. What heroes surrounded him as he
went at Drona?

"Abhimanyu has every virtue in greater measure than Dhananjaya. In him are
weapons, truth and lasting chastity. He is Vasudeva's equal in courage,
Dhananjaya's equal in strength, the sun's equal in splendour and Brihaspati's
equal in wisdom. What heroes surrounded that great man as he went at Drona, like
Death with his jaws gaping? When young Subhadra's son, bright as the dawn, the
killer of enemy heroes, rushed at Drona, what were your thoughts?

"When the sons of Draupadi, tigers among men, rushed at Drona in battle like
rivers to the ocean, what heroes held them back? And the proud sons of
Dhrishtadyumna, Kshatranjaya, Kshatradeva and Kshatradharman, gave up play as
boys and lived twelve years with Bhishma for the sake of weapons, keeping a high
vow. Who held those heroes back from Drona? Who held back from Drona the great
archer Chekitana, whom the Vrishnis thought better than a hundred in battle? Who
held back Anadhrishti, Vriddhakshema's son, of undaunted spirit, who carried off
the daughter of the Kalingas in battle? The five Kekaya brothers are righteous
and true in courage, the colour of cochineal beetles, with red armour, weapons
and banners. They are the sons of the Pandavas' mother's sister, and they want
the Pandavas to win. What heroes surrounded them when they came to kill Drona?

"Yuyutsu is the best of archers, brave, true to his word and very strong. Kings
fought him for six months at Varanavata, furious and wanting to kill that lord
of warriors, and could not beat him. Who held back that tiger among men from
Drona? The great archer Dhrishtadyumna keeps the Parthas' counsel. He is bent on
harming Duryodhana and was created to kill Drona. Who surrounded him as he went
at Drona, burning up the warriors in the battle and tearing them apart on every
side? At Varanasi he struck from his chariot, with a broad arrow, the great
chariot fighter, the son of the king of Kashi, who was fighting over women. Who
held back Kshatradeva, Shikhandi's son, from Drona, the best of all who know
weapons, brought up as if in Drupada's lap?

"The son of Ushinara wrapped this whole earth round like a hide with his great
line of chariots. He was a great chariot fighter who killed the chief of his
enemies. He performed ten horse sacrifices with fine food and drink and generous
gifts, and sacrifices of every kind without hindrance, and he protected his
people as if they were his sons. The hero gave as many cows at his sacrifice as
there are grains of sand in the stream of the Ganga. 'No men before or after
have done this,' the gods cried out when he had done this hard deed. 'We see no
one else in the three worlds, among all that stands and moves, born or to be
born, who can carry the burden but Shaibya, the son of Ushinara. Men living in
the world will not reach where he has gone.' Who held back from Drona his
grandson Shaibya as he came at Drona like Death with his jaws gaping? What
heroes surrounded the chariot ranks of Virata, the Matsya, the killer of his
enemies, as he sought Drona in the battle?

"Ghatotkacha was born at once to Wolf-belly, of great strength and courage, a
terrible rakshasa, a master of magic. I am greatly afraid of him. He longs for
the Parthas' victory, and he is a thorn in the side of my sons. Who held back
strong-armed Ghatotkacha from Drona?

"These and many others are ready to give up their lives in battle for the
Pandavas, Sanjaya. What is there in battle they cannot win? The tiger among men,
the bearer of the Sharnga bow, is their refuge, and he wants what is good for
the Parthas. How can they be beaten? He is the great teacher of the worlds, the
eternal lord of the worlds, Narayana, their protector in battle, divine, of
divine soul, the Lord. The wise tell of his divine deeds. I will tell them over
myself, with devotion, to steady my own heart.

"Listen to the divine deeds of Vasudeva, Sanjaya, which Govinda did as no other
man has ever done. While the great one was growing up in the clan of cowherds,
still a boy, the strength of his arms became famous in the three worlds,
Sanjaya. He killed the king of horses who lived in the forest by the Yamuna,
equal to Uchchaihshravas in strength and to the wind in speed. As a boy he
killed with his bare arms the danava of terrible deeds who took the form of a
bull and rose up like death to the cattle. The lotus-eyed one killed Pralamba,
Naraka, Jambha and the great demon Pitha, and Muru, who was like a mountain. And
by Krishna's courage alone, mighty Kamsa, whom Jarasandha protected, was brought
down in battle with his followers. Sunaman was the brave lord of a whole
akshauhini, the brother of Kamsa, who stood in the middle of the Bhoja king's
army, the mighty king of the Shurasenas. Krishna, the killer of enemies, with
Baladeva at his side, burned him up in battle with his army. The brahmin seer
named Durvasas, a man of fierce temper, was served by Krishna and his wife, and
gave him boons.

"At a bridegroom-choice the hero, the lotus-eyed one, beat the kings of the
earth and carried off the daughter of the king of Gandhara. The kings could not
bear it, and yet they were yoked to his wedding chariot like horses of good
breed, their skin torn by the goad. By a stratagem Janardana had strong-armed
Jarasandha, the lord of a whole akshauhini, killed by another. The strong one
killed the mighty king of Chedi, the commander of the kings, like a beast, when
he quarrelled over the guest-gift. Madhava attacked Saubha, the demons' city,
standing in the sky, guarded by Shalva and so hard to reach, and brought it down
in the belly of the sea. He beat in battle the Angas, the Vangas and the
Kalingas, the Magadhas, the men of Kashi and Kosala, the Vatsas, the Gargas, the
Karushas and the Pundras. The lotus-eyed one beat the men of Avanti and of the
south, the mountain peoples and the Dasherakas, the Kashmiris, the Aurasakas,
the Pishachas and the Samandaras. He beat the Kambojas, the Vatadhanas, the
Cholas and the Pandyas, Sanjaya, the Trigartas, the Malavas and the Daradas, so
hard to beat. He beat the bands of Ashvakas and Shakas who came from every
direction, and the Yavanas with their followers.

"Long ago he went into the home of the sea monsters, full of water creatures,
and beat Varuna in battle under the waters. He killed Panchajana in battle, who
lived in the depths of the underworld, and Hrishikesha won the divine conch
Panchajanya. In the Khandava forest, with Partha, the mighty one pleased the
fire and won the fiery weapon, the invincible discus. The hero mounted Vinata's
son, frightened Amaravati and brought the Parijata tree from great Indra's
house. And Shakra bore it, knowing his courage. We have never heard of any king
here whom Krishna did not beat.

"And that great wonder the lotus-eyed one worked in my assembly hall, Sanjaya,
who else here could do it? When I came to Krishna, the Lord, in devotion, and
saw him, all of it became as clear to me as if I saw it with my own eyes. There
is no reaching the end of the deeds of Hrishikesha, Sanjaya, full as they are of
courage and wisdom.

"And there are Gada, Samba, Pradyumna and Viduratha, Agavaha, Aniruddha,
Charudeshna and Sarana, Ulmuka and Nishatha, Jhalli and mighty Babhru, Prithu,
Viprithu, Samika and Arimejaya. These strong Vrishni heroes, strikers all, might
somehow join the Pandava army in the battle, if the Vrishni hero, great Keshava,
called them. Then, I think, everything would be in doubt. Where Janardana is,
there is Rama, the hero with the strength of ten thousand elephants, like the
peak of Kailasa, wearing the forest garland and carrying the plough.

"The twice-born call Vasudeva the father of all. Will he fight for the sake of
the Pandavas, Sanjaya? When Keshava arms himself for the Pandavas, my son, there
will be no one who can stand against him. If all the Kurus beat all the
Pandavas, the Varshneya would take up his great weapon for their sake. Then the
strong-armed tiger among men would kill all the kings in battle, and the
Kauravas, and give the earth to Kunti. What chariot could stand against the
chariot that has Hrishikesha to drive it and Dhananjaya to fight in it? By no
means at all can I see victory for the Kurus. So tell me everything, how the
battle went.

"Arjuna is Keshava's self, and Krishna is the self of the diademed one. In
Arjuna there is always victory, and in Krishna lasting fame. The virtues in
Keshava are above all others, beyond measure. Duryodhana in his folly does not
know Krishna, Madhava, here. Deluded by fate, with the noose of death before
him, he does not know Krishna of the Dasharhas, nor Arjuna the Pandava. They are
the gods of old, the great Nara and Narayana, one soul in two, seen by men on
earth. The famous pair could destroy this army by thought alone, if they wished,
but because they are men they do not wish it.

"The killing of Bhishma, my son, and of great Drona is like the overturning of
an age, like the bewildering of the worlds. Not by chastity, not by study of the
Veda, not by rites, not by weapons, does anyone escape from death. Bhishma and
Drona, heroes honoured by the world, masters of weapons, mad for battle, have
been killed. When I hear it, Sanjaya, why do I still live? The good fortune we
envied Yudhishthira long ago, we give up to him today, with the killing of
Bhishma and Drona. And this destruction of the Kurus has come about because of
me. When things are ripe for slaughter, charioteer, even blades of grass become
thunderbolts. Yudhishthira has won a lordship in the world like no other, since
by his anger the great archers Bhishma and Drona have been brought down. Dharma
has come to men by their nature, not adharma. Cruel Time is passing on towards
the destruction of all. Men of spirit think of their plans one way, my son, and
by fate they go another way. That is what I believe. So now that this
unavoidable thing has come, this great hardship that cannot be crossed and
cannot be thought of, tell me everything as it happened."

**Divergences.**

* **Krishna's boyhood deeds.** The CE list names the king of horses in the Yamuna forest, the demon in the form of a bull, Pralamba, Naraka, Jambha, Pitha, Muru and Kamsa (7.10.2–6). The lines that name Putana, the cart, the twin Arjuna trees, Govardhana, Chanura and Mushtika are rejected star passages (\*81, \*82) and are not narrated. See ledger L-33.
* **"Fought over women."** 7.9.56 says Dhrishtadyumna struck the prince of Kashi from his chariot at Varanasi as he fought "greedy for women" (*strīṣu gṛdhyantam*), that is, at a bride-winning. The CE says no more.
* **Sanjaya's reply.** Dhritarashtra's questions are not answered one by one. Sanjaya begins the full story in the next chapter (7.11).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*53–\*86 and @3. \*62 adds that Drona was "like Brahma in brahmin lore and like Narayana in kshatriya lore"; @3 repeats 7.10.32–33.
