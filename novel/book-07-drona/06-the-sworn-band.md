summary: That night Drona, ashamed, tells Duryodhana that no one can take Yudhishthira while Arjuna is beside him, and that someone must draw Arjuna away. The Trigarta king Susharma and his brothers offer to do it. With thousands of chariots from many peoples they light a fire, put on grass garments, and swear a terrible oath to kill Arjuna or die: they become the samshaptakas, the sworn band. In the morning they challenge him from the south. Arjuna, who has vowed never to refuse a challenge, leaves Satyajit to guard Yudhishthira and goes. The samshaptakas cheer at the sight of him, and he smiles that men about to die should rejoice. He kills Sudhanvan and puts them to flight, and Susharma shames them back into the fight, with the Narayana cowherds. Arjuna uses Tvashtri's weapon, so that they kill each other thinking each other Arjuna. They bury him and Krishna in arrows and cheer that both are dead, until Arjuna blows the arrows away with the wind weapon and cuts them down in thousands. Meanwhile Drona attacks Yudhishthira.

# The Sworn Band

"The two armies went back to their camp, lord of the people," said Sanjaya, "and
settled down in their places, by division and by company, in the proper way.
When he had withdrawn the troops, Drona, deeply unhappy, looked at Duryodhana
and spoke to him with shame.

"'I told you before,' he said, 'that while Dhananjaya is there, not even the
gods can capture Yudhishthira in battle. You strove, and Partha did this to you
in the battle. Do not doubt my words: Krishna and the Pandava cannot be beaten.
But if the one with white horses is drawn away somehow, king, then Yudhishthira
will come into your power today. Let someone challenge him to battle and drag
him off to another part of the field. Kunti's son will never turn back until he
has beaten him. In that gap, when he is not there, king, I will break through
the army and capture the King of Dharma, while Dhrishtadyumna looks on. If he
does not leave the battle once he is without Arjuna, and sees me coming, take it
that he is captured. So I will quickly bring the son of Dharma, Yudhishthira,
into your power today, king, with his followers, beyond doubt. If the Pandava
stands his ground in battle even for a moment, that is. If he runs from the
battle, that is better than a victory.'

"When he heard Drona's words, the lord of the Trigartas, with his brothers,
king, said this. 'We have always been wronged by the bearer of the Gandiva,
king. He has done us harm when we did him none, bull of the Bharatas. We
remember all those wrongs, of every kind, and burning with the fire of anger we
cannot sleep at night. Now he has come within sight of us, with his divine
weapons. We will do everything we have set our hearts on, whatever pleases you
and brings us fame. We will draw him out of the battle and kill him. Today let
the earth be without Arjuna, or else without the Trigartas. We give you our true
promise, and it will not be false.'

"When they had said this, Bharata, Satyaratha and Satyadharman, Satyavarman,
Satyeshu and Satyakarman, the five brothers together, with ten thousand
chariots, turned back to take their oath for the battle, great king. The Malavas
and the Tundikeras came with thirty thousand chariots. Susharma, the tiger among
men, the Trigarta, the lord of Prasthala, swore with his brothers, with the
Machellakas, the Lalitthas and the Madrakas and ten thousand chariots. And
another ten thousand chosen chariots rose up from many lands and came to take
the oath.

"Then they fetched fire, and each one made his offering. They took garments of
kusha grass and bright armour. With their armour tied on, smeared with ghee,
dressed in kusha grass, wearing belts of bowstring, the heroes had given gifts
of hundreds of thousands. They had offered sacrifices and had sons, and were
worthy of the worlds of the good. They had done what they had to do and were
ready to give up their bodies, and they were yoking themselves to fame and
victory. By chastity, by learning, and by sacrifices with fitting gifts, they
had earned the worlds above, and they longed to reach them soon by fighting
well. They satisfied the brahmins and gave each of them gold coins, cows and
clothes, and spoke with one another. They kindled the fire, took their vow for
battle, and there, in that fire, they made their promise with firm resolve. They
spoke aloud, so that all beings could hear, and they bound themselves to kill
Dhananjaya.

"'There are worlds that belong to liars and to killers of brahmins,' they said.
'There are the worlds of the drunkard and of the man who goes to his teacher's
wife. There are the worlds of the man who steals a brahmin's property or the
king's food, of the one who abandons a man who comes to him for refuge, or kills
one who begs. There are the worlds of those who burn houses or kill cows, of
those who do harm, and of those who hate brahmins. There are the worlds of those
who in folly go to their wives at the wrong time, of those who share a funeral
meal and of those who betray themselves. There are the worlds of those who steal
what is left in their keeping, of those who destroy learning, of those who fight
in anger and of those who follow low men. There are the worlds of unbelievers
and of those who neglect the sacred fire, their mothers and their fathers, and
the worlds of all who do evil. May we go to those worlds if we turn back from
battle without killing Dhananjaya, or if, crushed by him, we turn our backs in
fear. But if we do a deed in battle that is hard to do in this world, we will
surely reach the worlds we long for, the worlds of those who do good.'

"When they had said this, king, they went out to battle, the heroes, calling on
Arjuna from the south, the quarter the ancestors love. When the tiger among men,
Partha, the conqueror of enemy cities, was challenged by them, he spoke at once
to the King of Dharma.

"'I have taken a vow that when I am challenged I will not turn away,' he said.
'The sworn band are challenging me again and again, king. And here is Susharma
with his brothers, challenging me to battle. Please give me leave to kill him
and all his troops. I cannot bear this challenge, bull among men. I give you my
true promise: take it that the enemy are dead in battle.'

"'You have heard, my son, what Drona means to do,' said Yudhishthira. 'Act so
that it proves false. For Drona is strong and brave, a master of weapons who
never tires, and he has promised to capture me, great chariot fighter.'

"'Here is Satyajit, king,' said Arjuna. 'He will guard you in battle today. As
long as the Panchala lives, the teacher will not get his wish. But if Satyajit,
the tiger among men, is killed in battle, lord, you must not stay on the field
for any reason, even with all our men around you.'

"The king gave him leave and embraced him," said Sanjaya. "He looked at Phalguna
often, with love, and gave him his blessing. Then mighty Partha left him and
went against the Trigartas, like a hungry lion going for a herd of deer to still
its hunger. Then Duryodhana's army, full of joy now that Arjuna had gone, grew
fierce, bent on capturing the King of Dharma. Then the two armies met each other
with force, like the Ganga and the Sarayu in the rains, when their waters rise.

"The sworn band, king, stood on level ground, drawn up in an array of chariots
alone called the half-moon, full of joy. When they saw the diademed one coming,
sir, those tigers among men shouted with a great noise, in their delight. The
sound filled every quarter and the sky, and since it filled the world, there was
no echo. Dhananjaya saw how delighted they were. He smiled a little and said to
Krishna:

"'Look at these Trigarta brothers, son of Devaki, who are about to die in battle
today, rejoicing when they should weep. Or else this is a time of joy for the
Trigartas, no doubt of it, for they will reach the highest worlds, which bad men
cannot reach.'

"When strong-armed Arjuna had said this to Hrishikesha, he came up to the
Trigartas' army, drawn up in battle. Phalguna took up his conch Devadatta,
adorned with gold, and blew it with great force, filling the quarters. Terrified
by that sound, the army of the sworn band stood stock still in the battle, as if
made of stone. Their horses rolled their eyes, their ears, heads and necks
stiff, their legs rigid, and they passed urine and blood.

"Then they came to their senses, steadied their army, and all at once shot
arrows fledged with heron feathers at the son of Pandu. With his swift arrows
Arjuna, whose courage was quick, cut down those fifteen thousand arrows before
they reached him. Then they pierced Arjuna again with sharp arrows, ten each,
and Partha pierced them with three each. Each one of them pierced Partha with
five, king, and the hero pierced each of them back with two. In even greater
fury they filled Arjuna and Keshava with sharp arrows, as rain fills a pond.
Thousands of arrows fell on Arjuna, like swarms of bees on a forest of flowering
trees.

"Then Subahu pierced Savyasachin hard in the diadem with thirty strong arrows,
their heads of hardened iron. The diademed one, with those straight arrows with
golden feathers standing in his diadem, crowned with gold, looked like a
sacrificial post raised high. With a broad arrow the Pandava cut Subahu's
hand-guard in the battle, and covered him again with showers of arrows. Then
Susharma and Suratha, Sudharma, Sudhanvan and Subahu shot the diademed one with
ten arrows each. He of the monkey banner pierced them all back, one by one, with
arrows, and cut down their golden banners with broad arrows. He cut Sudhanvan's
bow, killed his horses with arrows, and struck his head, with its helmet, from
his body. When the hero fell, his followers were terrified, and they fled in
fear towards Duryodhana's army. Then Vasava's son in his fury struck that great
army with unbroken nets of arrows, as the sun strikes the darkness with its
rays.

"When that army was broken and fled on every side, and Savyasachin was in a
fury, fear seized the Trigartas. As Partha struck them with his smooth-jointed
arrows, they stood dazed where they were, like frightened deer. Then the king of
the Trigartas, angry, said to those great chariot fighters, 'Enough of running,
heroes! You must not be afraid. You swore terrible oaths while the whole army
watched. What will you say when you go back to Duryodhana's army, you leaders?
How shall we not be laughed at in the world for this deed in battle? Let us all
hold together. Turn back, with all your strength.'

"When he said this, king, they shouted again and again, and the heroes blew
their conches, cheering one another. Then the hosts of the sworn band turned
back again, and so did the Narayanas, the cowherds, making death their turning
back.

"When Arjuna saw the hosts of the sworn band turned back again, he spoke to
great Vasudeva. 'Drive the horses at the hosts of the sworn band, Hrishikesha.
They will not leave the battle alive. That is what I think. See the terrible
strength of my weapons, of my arms and my bow. Today I will cut them down, as
Rudra in his anger cuts down the beasts.'

"Then Krishna smiled, greeted him with kind words, and drove him, invincible as
he was, wherever Arjuna wanted to go into the enemy's army. The chariot shone as
it was carried through the battle by its white horses, like a flying chariot
carried through the sky. Then it made circles, going out and coming back, king,
like Shakra's chariot long ago in the battle of the gods and the demons.

"Then the Narayanas, angry, with every kind of weapon in their hands, surrounded
Dhananjaya, covering him with swarms of arrows. In a moment, bull of the
Bharatas, they had made Kunti's son Dhananjaya, and Krishna with him, invisible
in the battle. Phalguna in his anger doubled his courage in the battle. He wiped
the Gandiva and took it up quickly. The Pandava knitted his brows, the sign of
anger in his face, and blew his great conch Devadatta.

"Then Arjuna loosed Tvashtri's weapon, which kills hosts of enemies. Thousands
of forms appeared from it, one by one. Bewildered by those many forms, each like
himself, they killed one another, each thinking the other Arjuna, and even
killed themselves. 'This is Arjuna! This is Govinda! These are the Yadava and
the Pandava!' they cried, and in their confusion they killed one another in the
battle. Bewildered by that supreme weapon, they destroyed one another. Covered
in blood, the warriors looked like flowering kimshuka trees in the battle.

"Then, when the weapon had burned to ashes the thousands of arrows they had
loosed, it sent those heroes to Yama's house. Then Bibhatsu laughed, and struck
the Lalitthas and the Malavas, the Machellakas, the Trigartas and the Yaudheyas
with his arrows. As the hero struck them, the kshatriyas, driven by Time, poured
showers of arrows of every kind on Partha. Then neither Arjuna, nor his chariot,
nor Keshava could be seen there, covered by that terrible shower of arrows.
Then, since they had hit their mark, they shouted to one another, 'Both Krishnas
are dead!', and in their joy they waved their clothes. The heroes beat thousands
of drums and tabors and blew conches, and roared fierce lions' roars there, sir.

"Then Krishna sweated, and in his weariness he said to Arjuna, 'Where are you,
Partha? I cannot see you. Are you alive, killer of enemies?' The Pandava, who
knew what he felt, saw that human mood in him, and with the weapon of the wind
he blew away the shower of arrows they had loosed. Then the blessed Wind carried
off the hosts of the sworn band, with their horses, elephants, chariots and
weapons, like heaps of dry leaves. As the wind carried them away, king, they
looked like birds flying up from the trees at their time, sir.

"When he had thrown them into confusion like this, Dhananjaya quickly killed
them with sharp arrows, by hundreds and thousands. With broad arrows he struck
off heads, and arms still holding their weapons, and with arrows he brought down
to the ground thighs like elephants' trunks. Dhananjaya left his enemies with
their backs cut, their feet gone, without brains, eyes or fingers, without limbs
and parts of every kind. Cutting to pieces with his arrows the chariots, fitted
out by the rules like cities of the gandharvas, he left them without horses,
chariots or elephants. Here and there, groups of chariots with their banners cut
down looked like groves of palm trees with their tops lopped off. Elephants with
their riders and weapons, with their flags, goads and arms, fell like wooded
mountains struck by Shakra's thunderbolt. Horses with their riders, with their
fly whisks, headdresses and armour, their entrails, eyes and lives spilled out,
fell to the ground, struck by Partha's arrows. Foot soldiers lay pitifully dead,
their swords like claws scattered, their armour, spears and lances cut to
pieces. With the dead and the dying, the fallen and the falling, the reeling and
the groaning, the battlefield was terrible.

"The great dust that rose was settled by showers of blood, and the earth,
crowded with hundreds of headless bodies, became hard to cross. Bibhatsu's
course through the battle was both fearful and horrible, like the playground of
Rudra as he kills the beasts at the end of time. Struck by Partha, their horses,
chariots and elephants in turmoil, they fell facing him, and went to be the
guests of Shakra. The whole ground, best of the Bharatas, was covered on every
side with those great chariot fighters, killed and turned to ghosts.

"In the meantime, while Savyasachin was busy, Drona drew up his ranks and rushed
at Yudhishthira. Strikers who had drawn up their ranks met him quickly, wanting
to save Yudhishthira, and there was a great uproar."

**Divergences.**

* **The sworn band.** *Saṃśaptaka* means "one bound by an oath". The CE gives the rite in full: fire, kusha-grass garments, belts of bowstring, gifts to brahmins, and an oath that calls down on them the worlds of every kind of sinner if they turn back (7.16.22–36).
* **The twelfth day.** The oath is sworn at night, after the eleventh day (7.16.1–2), and the fight with the sworn band opens the twelfth. The next adhyaya (7.19) goes back to that morning to tell Drona's side of it.
* **Arjuna's vow.** "When I am challenged I will not turn away" (7.16.39). Arjuna leaves Satyajit, the Panchala, to guard Yudhishthira (7.16.44–45).
* **The Narayanas.** The "Narayanas, the cowherds" who fight with the sworn band (7.17.31; 7.18.7) are Krishna's own troops, whom he gave to Duryodhana (5.7.16; ledger L-171).
* **Krishna's weariness.** Krishna sweats and asks whether Arjuna is alive, and Arjuna sees "that human mood in him" (7.18.21–22).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*124–\*141. \*128 has Arjuna promise Yudhishthira that he will see the Trigartas dead; \*134 describes the stunned army "like a painting".
