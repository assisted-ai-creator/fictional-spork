# The Death of Bhagadatta

"What did the Pandava do to Bhagadatta in his anger?" said Dhritarashtra. "Or
what did the king of Pragjyotisha do to Partha? Tell me as it happened."

"When the Dasharha and the Pandava were locked in battle with the king of
Pragjyotisha," said Sanjaya, "all creatures thought they had come close to
death. For Bhagadatta, from his elephant's shoulders, poured showers of arrows
without pause on the two Krishnas in their chariot, lord. He pierced Devaki's
son with black iron arrows with golden feathers, sharpened on stone, loosed from
his bow drawn full. Sharp as the touch of fire, the arrows Bhagadatta sent went
right through Devaki's son and into the ground. Partha cut his bow and killed
the man who handed him arrows, and fought King Bhagadatta almost as if playing
with him. Bhagadatta sent fourteen sharp lances like the rays of the sun, and
Savyasachin cut each of them in three. Then Pakashasana's son tore away the
elephant's armour with a net of arrows, and it looked like the king of mountains
without its clouds.

"Then the king of Pragjyotisha hurled an iron spear with a golden shaft at
Vasudeva, and Arjuna cut it in two. Then Arjuna cut down the king's umbrella and
banner with his arrows, and with a smile he quickly pierced the lord of the
mountains with ten arrows. Deeply pierced by Arjuna's arrows, with their fine
feathers of heron, Bhagadatta grew angry with the great Pandava. He hurled
lances at the head of the one with white horses and roared, and in the battle
they knocked Arjuna's diadem askew. Setting his diadem straight, Phalguna said
to the king, 'Take a good look at the world.'

"At this Bhagadatta in his fury took up a shining bow and poured a shower of
arrows on the Pandava and Govinda. Partha cut his bow, cut his quivers to
pieces, and quickly struck him in all his vital parts with seventy-two arrows.
Pierced as he was, he did not falter. In his anger he called up the Vaishnava
weapon, spoke a spell over his goad, and loosed it at the Pandava's chest. That
weapon, loosed by Bhagadatta, could kill anything. Keshava covered Partha and
took it on his own chest. On Keshava's chest the weapon became the garland
Vaijayanti.

"Then Arjuna, sick at heart, spoke to Keshava. 'You said, Janardana, "I will not
fight, I will only hold the horses." Lotus-eyed one, you are not keeping your
promise. If I were in trouble, or could not hold it off, then you might do this.
But you should not do it while I stand here. With my arrows and my bow I could
conquer these worlds, with gods, demons and men. You know that too.'

"Then Vasudeva gave Arjuna an answer full of meaning. 'Hear this secret, Partha,
of what happened long ago, blameless one. I am always ready, in four forms, to
save the worlds. I divide myself here and bring about the good of the worlds.
One of my forms stays on earth and practises austerities. Another watches the
world doing good and evil. Another has taken shelter in the world of men and
does the work of men. And the fourth lies in a sleep of a thousand years. When
that form of mine rises at the end of the thousand years, it gives the finest
boons at that time to those who deserve them.

"'The Earth knew that the time had come, and she asked me for a boon for Naraka.
Hear what it was. "Let my son be one whom the gods and the demons cannot kill,"
she said. "Let him have the Vaishnava weapon. Please grant me that." When I
heard the Earth's request for her son, I gave her that unfailing Vaishnava
weapon long ago. And I said, "Earth, let this weapon never fail. It will protect
Naraka, and no one will kill him. Guarded by this weapon, your son, the crusher
of enemy armies, will be invincible in all the worlds for ever." "So be it,"
said the wise goddess, and she went away with her wish granted. And Naraka was
invincible, the tormentor of his enemies. From him, Partha, that weapon of mine
passed to the king of Pragjyotisha. There is no one in all the worlds, even with
Indra and Rudra, whom it cannot kill, sir. So I have turned it aside for your
sake. Now he has lost that great weapon. Kill the great demon, Partha, your
enemy, who is hard to face in battle, Bhagadatta, the enemy of the gods, as I
once killed Naraka for the good of the worlds.'

"When great Keshava had said this, Partha quickly covered Bhagadatta with sharp
arrows. Then strong-armed Partha, calm and great of mind, drove an iron arrow
into the elephant between the bumps on its forehead. The arrow reached the
elephant, like a thunderbolt a mountain, and went in to the feathers, like a
snake into an anthill. The great elephant stiffened its limbs, sank to the
ground on its tusks, and gave up its life with a cry of pain.

"Then with a smooth-jointed arrow with a crescent head the Pandava split King
Bhagadatta's heart. With his heart split by the diademed one, King Bhagadatta
let fall his bow and arrows, and his life left him. From his head the fine goad
fell, like a petal from a lotus when its stalk is struck. With his golden
garland he fell from the elephant, which was like a mountain in its trappings of
gold. He fell as a flowering karnikara tree, broken by the force of the wind,
falls from a mountain top.

"When Indra's son had killed in battle that king, with the courage of Indra and
Indra's friend, he broke your other men who wanted victory, as a strong wind
breaks trees. When Partha had killed the king of Pragjyotisha, Indra's friend of
boundless power, always dear to Indra, he moved round keeping to the right.

"Then the two sons of the king of Gandhara, conquerors of enemy cities, the
brothers Vrishaka and Achala, came at Arjuna in battle. The two heroes met
Arjuna, one in front and one behind, and pierced him hard with sharp, swift
arrows. With his sharp arrows Partha broke to pieces the horses, charioteer,
bow, umbrella, chariot and banner of Vrishaka, Subala's son. Then Arjuna threw
the Gandharas, led by Subala's son, into confusion again with swarms of arrows
and weapons of many kinds. In his anger Dhananjaya sent to the world of death
five hundred brave Gandharas with their weapons raised. The strong-armed one got
down quickly from his chariot, whose horses had been killed, climbed into his
brother's chariot and took up another bow. Mounted in one chariot, the brothers
Vrishaka and Achala pierced Bibhatsu with a shower of arrows again and again.
Your great brothers-in-law, the kings Vrishaka and Achala, struck Partha hard,
like Vritra and Bala striking Indra. The two Gandharas, finding their mark,
struck the Pandava again, as the months of heat and rain strike the world with
sweat and water. As the two kings, tigers among men, Vrishaka and Achala, stood
in the chariot, their bodies pressed together, Arjuna killed them with one
arrow, king. The two heroes, like lions, red-eyed and strong-armed, brothers
from one womb and alike in their marks, fell lifeless from the chariot. Their
bodies, dear to their kin, fell from the chariot to the ground, having spread
their holy fame in all ten directions.

"When your sons saw their uncles, who never ran, killed in battle, lord of the
people, they wept bitterly. When Shakuni, the master of a hundred tricks, saw
his brothers killed, he made an illusion to bewilder the two Krishnas. Clubs,
iron balls and stones, shataghnis and spears, maces, iron bars, swords,
tridents, hammers and axes, kampanas, lances and claws, pestles and hatchets,
razors, arrows with razor heads, calf-tooth arrows and three-jointed arrows,
discuses, shafts, darts and weapons of every kind fell on Arjuna from every
quarter and every point between. Asses, camels and buffaloes, lions and tigers,
deer and chillikas, bears, wolves, vultures, monkeys and snakes, and rakshasas
of every kind, hungry, and birds of every kind, rushed at Arjuna in fury. Then
brave Dhananjaya, Kunti's son, who knew the divine weapons, loosed nets of
arrows and struck them all at once. Struck by the hero with his fine, strong
arrows, they screamed loudly and perished, killed on every side.

"Then darkness appeared around Arjuna's chariot, and from that darkness cruel
voices threatened Partha. Arjuna destroyed that darkness with the great weapon
of light. When it was gone, terrible floods of water appeared. To dry up that
water, Arjuna used the weapon of the sun, and the weapon dried up most of it. So
Arjuna quickly destroyed, with the power of his weapons, laughing, each of the
many illusions that Subala's son made. When his illusions had been destroyed,
frightened and struck by Arjuna's arrows, Shakuni fled on his swift horses like
a common man.

"Then Arjuna, showing his enemies his mastery of weapons, poured a flood of
arrows on the army of the Kauravas. Struck by Partha, your son's army split in
two, great king, like the Ganga striking a mountain. Some of the great chariot
fighters there went to Drona, and some to Duryodhana, king, as the diademed one
pressed them hard. Then we could not see that army, covered in darkness, and I
heard the twang of the Gandiva to the south. The sound of conches and drums, the
din of instruments and the twang of the Gandiva rose and reached the sky. Then
again, to the south, there was a fine battle between Arjuna and those wonderful
fighters, but I followed Drona.

"Arjuna scattered your sons' ranks of every kind, Bharata, as the wind scatters
clouds in the sky in its season. As he came on like Vasava, pouring down floods
of arrows, no one held back that fierce great archer, the tiger among men.
Struck by Partha, your men were badly shaken, and many killed their own side as
they fled here and there. The arrows Arjuna loosed, fledged with heron feathers,
cutting through bodies, fell like locusts and covered the ten directions. They
went through horses, chariot fighters, elephants and foot soldiers, sir, and
into the ground, like snakes into an anthill. He never loosed a second arrow at
elephant, horse or man. Each was broken by a single arrow and fell lifeless.
With men, horses and elephants killed and brought down on every side under the
shower of arrows, and with dogs, jackals and crows howling, the front of the
battle was a strange sight. Tormented by Partha, a father left his son, a friend
his best friend, and a son his father. Set only on saving themselves, men even
left their mounts behind."

**Divergences.**

* **The Vaishnava weapon.** Bhagadatta loosens it from his goad at Arjuna's chest; Krishna takes it on his own chest, where it becomes the garland Vaijayanti (7.28.16–18). Krishna tells Arjuna of his four forms and of the boon he gave the Earth for her son Naraka, from whom the weapon passed to Bhagadatta (7.28.23–35).
* **No blindfold.** A popular story says Bhagadatta was so old that his eyelids drooped, and he kept them up with a band of cloth, which Arjuna cut at Krishna's word. That is only in rejected star passages (\*224–\*225). In the CE, Arjuna kills the elephant with an iron arrow and splits Bhagadatta's heart with a crescent arrow (7.28.37–41). See ledger L-202.
* **The goad.** At 7.28.42 the CE reads *varāṅkuśaḥ*, "the fine goad", as what falls from his head. Ganguli's text has a fine cloth or turban there.
* **Shakuni's brothers.** Vrishaka and Achala, the sons of the Gandhara king and Duryodhana's uncles, are killed by one arrow as they stand in one chariot (7.29.11).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*210–\*231. \*215–\*216 tell more of Naraka's birth; \*220 has Arjuna cut the elephant's bell; \*230 has Arjuna mock Shakuni: "the Gandiva does not throw dice".
