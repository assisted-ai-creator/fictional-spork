# The Horses Drink

"As the sun turned," said Sanjaya, "the soldiers grew sluggish in the sun's rays
and the dust that covered them. Some stood and fought, others turned back again,
some broke and some won, and so that day went slowly by.

"While those armies were locked together, greedy for victory, Arjuna and
Vasudeva went on towards the Saindhava. With his sharp arrows Kunti's son made a
road there as wide as a chariot's track, and Janardana drove along it. Wherever
the great-souled Pandava's chariot went, lord of the people, your armies were
torn apart. The mighty Dasharha showed his skill as a charioteer, driving the
best, the lowest and the middle circles.

"Arjuna's arrows were marked with his name and well tempered, like the fire of
Time. They were bound with sinew, with good joints, broad and far-flying, of
bamboo and of iron, drawn well back, with heads of many kinds. In that battle
they drank the blood of living things, along with the birds. The arrows Arjuna
shot from his chariot at a krosha's distance struck down the enemy only when his
chariot had already passed that krosha.

"Hrishikesha drove with horses as swift as Tarkshya and the wind, which ran
well, and amazed the whole world. The sun's chariot does not go like that, lord
of the people, nor Indra's, nor Rudra's, nor Vaishravana's. No one else's
chariot had ever gone in battle as Arjuna's went, king, as fast as the wish of
the mind.

"Keshava, killer of enemy heroes, drove into the battle, king, and urged the
horses on quickly in the middle of the army, Bharata. When they reached the
middle of that mass of chariots, the fine horses could hardly draw the chariot,
for they were tired, hungry and thirsty. Fierce fighters had wounded them many
times with many weapons. Again and again they ran in strange circles. They went
over thousands of dead horses, elephants and chariots and men, lying like hills.

"Meanwhile, king, the two heroes, the brothers of Avanti, came against the
Pandava with their army when his horses were weary. Full of joy, they pierced
Arjuna with sixty-four arrows, Janardana with seventy, and the horses with a
hundred. Arjuna, knowing the vital places, in anger, great king, struck them in
battle with nine smooth-jointed arrows that split the vital places. Then the
two, in fury, covered Bibhatsu and Keshava with floods of arrows and roared like
lions. The man with the white horses quickly cut their fine bows with two broad
arrows in the battle, and their banners, blazing with gold. They took two other
bows, king, and struck the Pandava hard with arrows, in great anger. In great
anger Pandu's son Dhananjaya quickly cut their bows once more with two arrows.
Then with other arrows, gold-feathered and whetted on stone, he quickly killed
their horses and the foot soldiers with them, and both the drivers who guard the
wheels behind. He cut the elder brother's head from his body with a razor arrow,
and he fell dead to the earth like a tree broken by the wind.

"Seeing Vinda killed, mighty Anuvinda, of great strength, left his chariot, its
horses killed, and took up a mace. Remembering his brother's death, the great
warrior, best of mace fighters, rushed at them in battle, as if dancing with the
mace. In his anger Anuvinda struck Madhusudana on the forehead with the mace,
and could not shake him, any more than the Mainaka mountain. With six arrows
Arjuna cut through his neck, his feet, his arms and his head. Cut to pieces, he
fell like a heap of rocks.

"When they saw the two killed, king, their followers rushed in anger, scattering
arrows by the hundred. Arjuna quickly killed them with his arrows, bull of the
Bharatas, and shone like a fire that has burned a forest at the end of the cold
season. Passing their army, Dhananjaya came out with difficulty, and shone like
the sun risen after breaking through the clouds.

"When they saw him, the Kurus were afraid, and then glad again. They rained
arrows on Partha from every side, bull of the Bharatas. Seeing that he was
tired, and knowing the Saindhava was far away, they surrounded him on every side
with a great lion roar.

"Seeing their fury, the bull among men smiled, and Arjuna said slowly to the
Dasharha, 'The horses are hurt by the arrows and tired, and the Saindhava is far
away. What do you think is the best thing to do next? Tell me truly, Krishna,
for you are always the wisest. With you as their eyes, the Pandavas will beat
their enemies in battle. But hear what I think must be done next. Unyoke the
horses, Madhava, and draw the arrows out of them in peace.'

"Told this by Partha, Keshava answered him, 'I think so too, Partha, what you
have said.'

"Arjuna said, 'I will hold back all the armies, Keshava. You do what has to be
done next here, in the proper way.'

"Dhananjaya got down from the chariot, quite calm. He took up the bow Gandiva
and stood like a mountain that does not move. Seeing Dhananjaya on the ground,
Kshatriyas eager for victory rushed at him shouting, 'Here is an opening!' They
surrounded him, alone, with a great line of chariots, drawing their bows and
loosing arrows. In their anger they showed off weapons of every kind there,
covering Partha with arrows, as clouds cover the sun. The Kshatriyas rushed at
the bull among Kshatriyas with great force, those fine chariot fighters at the
lion among chariot fighters, like elephants in rut at a lion.

"There the great strength of Partha's arms was seen, as in his anger he held
back many armies on every side. The lord held off his enemies' weapons with his
own on every side, and quickly covered them all with many arrows. In the sky,
lord of the people, a blazing fire was born from the clash of the thick arrows.
There were great bowmen, panting and soaked in blood, and horses and elephants
split open and screaming. Killers of enemies and brave foes in their fury, many
and angry, seeking victory in battle, were all in one place. It was as if a heat
rose there.

"It was a sea of chariots, not to be stirred and hard to cross. Arrows were its
waves and banners its whirlpools, elephants its crocodiles and the crowding foot
soldiers its fish, and it roared with conches and drums. It could not be counted
and had no far shore, very dreadful with its dust. Turbans were its turtles,
flags were its garlands of foam, and elephants' bodies were its rocks. Then
Partha became its shore, and held it back with his arrows.

"Then, in the battle, strong-armed Janardana, quite calm, said to Arjuna, dear
to him, the best of men, 'There is no well here in the battle for the horses,
Arjuna. They want water to drink, not to bathe in.'

"'Here it is,' said Arjuna, quite calm. He struck the earth with a weapon and
made a fine pool for the horses to drink from. Then Partha made a house of
arrows, a wonder, with arrows for its beams, arrows for its pillars and arrows
for its roof, like Tvashtri, the maker of wonders. Govinda laughed, and said,
'Well done, well done!' when Partha had made that house of arrows in the great
battle.

"Great-souled Kunti's son had made the water spring up, held back the enemy army
and built the house of arrows. Then Vasudeva, of great splendour, got down
quickly from the chariot and unyoked the horses, which were pierced with
heron-feathered arrows. Seeing what had never been seen before, the hosts of
Siddhas and Charanas and the soldiers everywhere gave a great lion roar.

"The bulls among men could not hold back Kunti's son as he fought on foot, and
it was a wonder. As floods of chariots came on, with many elephants and horses,
Partha was not shaken, and in that he went beyond all men. The kings loosed
floods of arrows at the Pandava, and the righteous son of Vasava, killer of
enemy heroes, was not troubled. As the sea swallows the rivers, so mighty Partha
swallowed the nets of arrows, the maces and the lances as they came. By the
great force of his weapons and the strength of his arms, Partha swallowed the
fine arrows of all those lords of kings.

"The Kauravas praised the courage of both Partha and Vasudeva, great king, as
the greatest of wonders. 'What greater wonder will there ever be in the world,
or has there ever been, than that Partha and Govinda unyoked their horses in
battle? Those best of men put great fear into us. They showed their fierce fire,
at their ease, at the head of the battle.'

"Then Hrishikesha, the lotus-eyed, smiled as if he were among women, Bharata. In
the chamber of arrows Arjuna had made in the battle, he calmly led the horses to
roll, while all your armies watched, lord of the people. Krishna, skilled in the
care of horses, took away all their weariness and exhaustion, their trembling,
their froth and their wounds. He drew out the arrows with his hands, rubbed the
horses down, let them roll in the proper way, and gave them water to drink. When
they had drunk and bathed, eaten and rested, he yoked them to the fine chariot
again, full of joy. Shauri, of great splendour, best of all who bear weapons,
mounted the fine chariot, and with Arjuna he set off fast.

"When they saw the chariot of the best of chariot fighters yoked again, with
horses that had drunk, the best of the Kuru army lost heart once more. They
sighed, king, like snakes with broken fangs, and said, one after another,
'Shame, oh shame! Partha has gone, and Krishna. Armoured, in one chariot, while
all the Kshatriyas looked on, they made nothing of our army, as if it were a
child's toy. We shouted and we struggled, and the scorchers of enemies were not
held up. They have shown their courage and gone on, through all the kings.'

"Seeing the two go on again, other soldiers said, 'Hurry, all you Kurus, to kill
Krishna and the diadem-wearer. The Dasharha has yoked the chariot while all the
bowmen watched, and he is going to Jayadratha, making nothing of us in battle.'

"There some of the kings said to each other, king, having seen a great wonder in
battle that had never been seen before, 'All the armies, and King Dhritarashtra,
have come to ruin. Through Duryodhana's offence the Kshatriyas and the whole
earth have come to destruction, and the king does not see it.' So the Kshatriyas
spoke there, and others said, Bharata, 'The Sindhu king has gone to the house of
Yama. Let Dhritarashtra's son, who sees nothing straight and knows no way out,
do what must be done for him.'

"Then the Pandava went on faster towards the Saindhava, as the hot-rayed sun
went down, with his glad horses, their thirst quenched. Fighters could not hold
back the strong-armed best of all who bear weapons as he went, like Death the
Ender in his anger. The Pandava, scorcher of enemies, put the army to flight,
and churned it up for the Saindhava's sake, as a lion does herds of deer.
Plunging into the divisions, the Dasharha quickly urged on the horses, the
colour of cranes, and blew Panchajanya. The arrows Kunti's son shot in front of
him fell behind, for the horses, swift as the wind, carried him faster than
fast. Seeing the dreadful chariot with the monkey banner, its flags tossed by
the wind, roaring like the clouds, the chariot fighters were downcast. The sun
was thickly covered with dust on every side, and the fighters, hurt by the
arrows, could not look at the two Krishnas.

"Then the kings in anger surrounded Dhananjaya, as he sought Jayadratha's death,
and many other Kshatriyas too. Duryodhana went quickly after Partha, the bull
among men, in that great battle."

**Divergences.**

* **The pool.** In the CE Arjuna "struck the earth with a weapon" and made "a fine pool for the horses to drink from" (7.74.56). The lake full of swans, ducks, lotuses, fish and turtles, which the sages visit, is from the rejected \*538.
* **Dhritarashtra's question.** \*537, in which Dhritarashtra asks how Partha was not beaten while he stood on the ground, is rejected.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*532–\*536, \*539–\*546.

**Choices.**

* 7.74.6 *uttamādhamamadhyāni maṇḍalāni*: "the best, the lowest and the middle circles", the moves of a chariot.
* 7.74.7 *pītāḥ*: "well tempered".
* 7.74.9: the arrows shot "at a krosha's distance" strike "only when his chariot had already passed that krosha". The chariot is as fast as the arrows.
* 7.74.10 *tārkṣya*: Tarkshya, Garuda.
* 7.74.52 *rajo'bhīlam*: "very dreadful with its dust". The reading is uncertain.
* 7.74.57 *tvaṣṭā*: Tvashtri, the gods' craftsman.
* 7.75.12 *strīmadhya iva*: "as if he were among women", that is, at ease.
* 7.75.13, 15 *upāvartayat, upāvṛtya*: "led the horses to roll", "let them roll", as horses do to ease their backs.
* 7.75.14 *vamathum*: "their froth".
* 7.75.28 *nivartamāne tigmāṃśau*: "as the hot-rayed sun went down".
* 7.75.31 *balākavarṇān*: "the colour of cranes", white.
* 7.75.36: the first half of the verse, *apanīyatsu śalyeṣu dhiṣṭhitam*, is unclear, and is not rendered. G reads it as the Kuru warriors going towards Arjuna, who had stopped.
