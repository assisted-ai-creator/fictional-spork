# The Fourteenth Morning

"While Krishna and Daruka were still talking," said Sanjaya, "the night passed,
king, and then King Yudhishthira woke. Hand-clappers, Magadha bards and
honey-sweet singers recited, and minstrels and bards praised the bull among men.
Dancers danced, and singers with lovely voices sang sweetly, songs in praise of
the Kuru line. Skilled players, well taught and glad at heart, sounded drums of
every kind, kettledrums, tabors and cow-mouth horns, conches and loud-voiced war
drums, and many other instruments as well. That great noise, like the roar of
thunderclouds, touched the sky, and it woke Yudhishthira, the best of kings,
from his sleep.

"He had slept well on a costly bed. He rose and went to the bathhouse for what
had to be done. There a hundred and eight young bath attendants, dressed in
white and freshly bathed themselves, waited for him with golden jars filled to
the brim. Seated at his ease on a fine seat, wearing a light cloth, he bathed in
water mixed with sandalwood and blessed with spells. Strong, well-trained men
rubbed him down with a cleansing paste, and he was rinsed with water scented
with perfume. The strong-armed king smeared his body with yellow sandal paste.
Garlanded, in clean clothes, he stood facing east with his palms joined. Kunti's
son said the prayers he had to say, keeping to the path of good men. Then he
went humbly into the room where the sacred fire was burning. He honoured the
kindled fire with sticks and with offerings made holy by spells, and came out of
the room.

"The tiger among men went on into the second court. There he saw brahmins, bulls
among brahmins, who knew the Vedas. They were self-controlled, had bathed at the
end of their Vedic vows and at the closing baths of sacrifices, and were
worshippers of the sun. There were eight thousand of them, with a thousand
followers. With unbroken rice and flowers he had them speak their blessings, and
gave them honey and ghee and the best and luckiest fruits. Pandu's son gave each
brahmin a gold coin. He gave a hundred horses in their ornaments, and clothes,
and whatever gifts they wished. He gave brown cows in milk, with their bulls,
their horns covered with gold and their hooves with silver, and walked round the
brahmins, keeping them on his right.

"There were golden lucky signs, the svastika, the vardhamana and the
nandyavarta. There were garlands and jars of water and a blazing fire, vessels
full of unbroken rice, gold ornaments and yellow pigment. There were beautiful
girls in their finery, curds, ghee, honey and water, birds of good omen, and
whatever else is honoured. Kunti's son looked at them and touched them, and then
went to the outer court.

"As the strong-armed king stood there, his attendants brought him a splendid
seat. It was gold, and set with pearls and beryl, and made so that it looked
lucky from every side. It was spread with costly rugs and a cloth over them. It
was a rich and heavenly thing, made by Vishvakarman. When the great-souled king
had sat down, servants brought him all his costly, gleaming ornaments. Once
Kunti's son had put on his ornaments and his royal dress, his beauty was such
that it added to the grief of his enemies. They fanned him with white yak-tail
whisks, bright as moonbeams, with golden handles, and he shone like a cloud lit
with lightning. Bards praised him, minstrels hailed him and singers sang to him,
and so the delight of the Kurus sat.

"In a moment there rose a loud noise of bards, with the rumble of chariot wheels
and the drumming of horses' hooves. With the clanging of elephants' bells, the
blare of conches and the tramp of men's feet, the earth seemed to shake.

"Then a young doorkeeper came into the inner rooms. He wore earrings, a sword at
his belt and armour on his body. He knelt on the ground and bowed his head to
the lord of the world, who was worthy of all honour. Bowing his head again, he
told Dharma's great-souled son that Hrishikesha had come.

"The tiger among men said, 'Make Madhava welcome. Let a guest-offering and a
seat be given him, with the highest honour.' Then they brought the Vrishni in
and seated him on a fine seat. Yudhishthira honoured him, was honoured by him in
return, and asked him, 'Did you pass the night well, slayer of Madhu? Are your
senses all clear, Achyuta?'

"Vasudeva asked Yudhishthira the same, as was proper. Then the steward told the
king that the leading men had come. With the king's leave, he let them in:
Virata, Bhimasena, Dhrishtadyumna and Satyaki, Shikhandi, the twins, Chekitana
and the Kekayas, Yuyutsu of the Kuru line, and Uttamaujas of Panchala. These,
and many other Kshatriyas, came before the great-souled bull among Kshatriyas
and took their seats. Krishna and Yuyudhana, two great-souled heroes, mighty and
shining, sat on the same seat.

"Then, while they all listened, Yudhishthira spoke sweetly to the lotus-eyed
slayer of Madhu.

"'We rely on you alone, as the immortals rely on thousand-eyed Indra, and ask
you for victory in battle and for lasting happiness. You know how we lost our
kingdom, how our enemies cast us out, and all the troubles of every kind that we
have had, Krishna. Lord of all, you who are kind to those who love you, all our
happiness rests on you, and our whole way through the world, slayer of Madhu. My
heart is set on you, Varshneya. So act that Arjuna's vow, as he meant it, comes
true. Carry us over this great sea of grief and anger. We want to reach the
other shore today. Be our boat, Madhava. For a warrior in his chariot, even one
equal to Kartavirya, cannot do in battle what a charioteer does when he sets his
mind to it, Krishna.'

"Vasudeva said, 'In all the worlds, the world of the gods among them, there is
no bowman like Partha Dhananjaya. He is brave and a master of weapons, bold and
very strong, eager for battle and never willing to bear a wrong. In fire he is
the first of men. He is young, with a bull's shoulders and long arms, great in
strength, with the gait of a lion or a bull, a splendid man, and he will kill
your enemies. And I will act so that Kunti's son Arjuna burns up the armies of
Dhritarashtra's son like a fire that has sprung up. Today Arjuna's arrows will
send that low, evil-doing killer of Subhadra's son down the road from which no
one is seen again. Today vultures, hawks, jackals and the other eaters of men
will eat his flesh. Even if all the gods, with Indra, were his guards, he would
still be killed in the crush of battle today and go to the city of Yama. Once he
has killed the Saindhava, Jishnu will come to you today. Be free of grief and
fever, king, and put your good fortune first.'

"While they were talking," said Sanjaya, "Dhananjaya appeared. He wanted to see
the best of the Bharatas, the king, and his friends. He came into the fine hall,
bowed and stood before him. The bull among the Pandavas rose and embraced Arjuna
with love. He kissed his head, held him in his arms, spoke the highest
blessings, and said with a smile, 'Clearly, Arjuna, a great victory in battle is
certain for you. I can tell from the look on your face, and Janardana is pleased
with you.'

"Jishnu said to him, 'Bless you. By Keshava's grace I have seen a great and
wonderful thing.' Then, to give his friends heart, Dhananjaya told them of his
meeting with the Three-Eyed God, just as he had seen it. They were all amazed.
They touched the earth with their heads, bowed to the god whose sign is the
bull, and said, 'Well done, well done!'

"Then all the friends took leave of Dharma's son, armed themselves quickly and
well, and went out to battle, glad. Yuyudhana, Achyuta and Arjuna saluted the
king and went gladly out of Yudhishthira's quarters. The two whom no one could
face, Yuyudhana and Janardana, went together in one chariot to Arjuna's
quarters. There Hrishikesha, like a charioteer, harnessed the chariot of the
best of warriors, the one whose sign is the bull among monkeys. Made ready, the
fine chariot roared like the clouds and gleamed like molten gold, and it shone
like the young sun. Then the tiger among men, armed, went ahead and told Partha,
who had finished his morning rites, that the chariot was ready.

"The best of men in the world, the diadem-wearer, in golden armour, with his bow
and arrows, walked round the chariot, keeping it on his right. Old men, old in
learning and in years, who kept the rites and ruled their senses, praised him
and blessed him for victory, and he climbed into the great chariot. It had
already been blessed with spells of victory in battle. In gold, on the golden
chariot, the best of chariot fighters shone, pure and bright, like the sun
rising on Mount Meru. Yuyudhana and Janardana climbed in after Partha, as the
Ashvins went with Indra to the sacrifice of Sharyati. Govinda, the best of those
who hold the reins, took the reins, as Matali did for Indra when Indra set out
to kill Vritra. With those two beside him, Partha in his fine chariot was like
the moon with Budha and Shukra, driving away the dark. He set out, the killer of
hosts of enemies, wanting the Saindhava's death, like Indra with Varuna and
Mitra in the war over Taraka.

"As Arjuna went, minstrels and Magadha bards praised him, with the sound of
instruments and with lucky, beautiful hymns. Their blessings for victory and
wishes for a good day, and their voices joined with the instruments, gave the
heroes joy. A pure wind, carrying holy scents, blew after Partha, cheering him
and withering his enemies. Many omens of many kinds appeared, king, promising
victory to the Pandavas and the opposite to your men, sir.

"Seeing the omens promising victory, Arjuna said to the great bowman Yuyudhana,
'Yuyudhana, I see sure victory for me in today's battle. These are the signs I
see, bull of the Shinis. So I will go where the Sindhu king is. He wants to go
to Yama's world, and he is waiting for my strength. Killing the Saindhava is my
highest task. Guarding the King of Dharma is just as great a task. So today,
strong-armed one, you must guard the king. Let him be as safe with you as he is
with me. I can rely on you, or on the great warrior Pradyumna, and then I can
kill the Saindhava without looking back, bull among men. You must not look to me
at all, Satvata. Guard the king above everything, with all your heart. Where the
strong-armed Vasudeva stands, and where I stand, nothing will go wrong.'

"When Partha had said this, Satyaki, the killer of enemy heroes, said, 'So be
it,' and went to where King Yudhishthira was."

**Divergences.**

* **The allies.** The CE list at 7.59.3–4 is Virata, Bhima, Dhrishtadyumna, Satyaki, Shikhandi, the twins, Chekitana, the Kekayas, Yuyutsu and Uttamaujas. Drupada and Dhrishtaketu (\*435), and Yudhamanyu, Subahu and Draupadi's sons (\*437), are added only in rejected lines. G includes them.
* **Yudhishthira's hymn.** G's "I bow to thee, O God of the lord of the gods … Narada described thee as that ancient and best of Rishis" is from the rejected \*440 and is not narrated. So is the doorkeeper's description of Krishna as four-armed, with conch, discus and mace (\*431).
* **Satyaki's praise.** G's "I do not behold the person in the world who would be able to vanquish thee … equal to Vasudeva himself" is from the rejected \*445, and is left out.
* **The head cloth.** G's white cloth tied round the king's head to dry his hair is \*428, rejected.
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*429, \*430, \*432–\*434, \*436, \*438, \*439, \*441–\*444.

**Choices.**

* 7.58.2 *pāṇisvanikā māgadhā madhuparkikāḥ / vaitālikāś ca sūtāś ca*: "hand-clappers, Magadha bards and honey-sweet singers … minstrels and bards". The exact roles of these court performers are not certain.
* 7.58.4: the drums and horns are given in general terms: "drums of every kind, kettledrums, tabors and cow-mouth horns, conches and loud-voiced war drums".
* 7.58.10 *kaṣāyeṇa*: "a cleansing paste". *sādhivāsena jalena*: "water scented with perfume".
* 7.58.15 *sahasrānucarān saurān aṣṭau daśaśatāni ca*: read as "worshippers of the sun … eight thousand of them, with a thousand followers". G takes it as a thousand sun-worshippers and eight thousand others. The line is unclear.
* 7.58.17 *niṣkam*: "a gold coin". The hundred horses are not said to go to each brahmin, so the text does not say so here either.
* 7.58.19–20: *svastika*, *vardhamāna* and *nandyāvarta* are named as lucky signs. *rucakān*: "gold ornaments". *rocanān*: "yellow pigment".
* 7.58.22 *sarvatobhadram*: "made so that it looked lucky from every side".
* 7.58.30 *jagatpatim*: "the lord of the world", of Yudhishthira.
* 7.59.2 *kṣattā prakṛtayaḥ*: "the steward … the leading men".
* 7.59.10 *yātrā*: "our whole way through the world".
* 7.59.13 *kārtavīryasamaḥ*: "equal to Kartavirya", Arjuna Kartavirya.
* 7.59.15 *tejasā paramo nṛṇām*: "in fire he is the first of men".
* 7.60.4 *chāyā*: "the look on your face".
* 7.60.6 *tryambakena*: "the Three-Eyed God", Shiva.
* 7.60.11 *vānararṣabhalakṣaṇam*: "whose sign is the bull among monkeys".
* 7.60.20 *budhaśukrābhyām*: Budha and Shukra, the planets Mercury and Venus.
* 7.60.21 *ambupatimitrābhyām*: "Varuna and Mitra". *tārakāmaye*: "the war over Taraka".
* 7.60.26 *pradakṣiṇam*: taken as "promising", the omens being favourable. G reads it as Yuyudhana standing on Arjuna's right.
