# The Death of Abhimanyu

"The delight of Vishnu's sister, adorned with Vishnu's weapon, that great
chariot fighter shone in the battle like a second Janardana," said Sanjaya. "The
kings looked at his body, his hair tossed by the wind, the finest of weapons
raised against his enemies, a form hard for the gods themselves to look at.
Deeply alarmed, they cut that wheel to pieces. Then Krishna's son, the great
chariot fighter, took up a great mace. His enemies had left him without bow,
chariot, sword or wheel. Mace in hand, Abhimanyu rushed at Ashvatthama. When the
bull among men saw the mace raised, blazing like a thunderbolt, he stepped back
three paces from his chariot seat. With the mace Subhadra's son killed his
horses and both his flank drivers, and with his body full of arrows he looked
like a porcupine.

"Then he crushed Kalakeya, Subala's son, and killed seventy-seven of his
Gandhara followers. Again he killed ten Brahma-Vasatiya chariot fighters. He
killed seven Kekaya chariots and ten elephants, and with his mace he smashed
Duhshasana's son's chariot and horses. Then Duhshasana's son in his anger raised
his mace, sir, and rushed at Subhadra's son, crying, 'Stand! Stand!'

"With their maces raised, each wanting to kill the other, the two heroes,
cousins, struck at each other, like the Three-eyed God and Andhaka long ago.
Each struck the other with the head of his mace, and the two tamers of enemies
fell to the ground in the middle of the battle, like two banners of Indra let
fall. Then Duhshasana's son, who added to the fame of the Kurus, got up first,
and struck Subhadra's son on the head with his mace as he was getting up.
Stunned by the great force of the mace and by his labour, Subhadra's son, the
killer of enemy heroes, fell senseless to the ground.

"So, king, one was killed in battle by many. He had stirred up the whole army as
an elephant stirs up a lotus pond, and now the hero lay killed, beautiful, like
a wild elephant killed by hunters. Your men surrounded the fallen hero. He was
like a fire that has burned a forest and died down at the end of the cold
season. He was like a wind that has broken the tops of the trees and dropped,
like the sun that has set after scorching the Bharata army. He was like the moon
eclipsed, like the ocean dried up. His face was like the full moon, and his eyes
were framed by his boyish locks.

"When they saw him lying on the ground, your great chariot fighters roared like
lions again and again, in the greatest joy. Your men were overjoyed, lord of the
people, but tears fell from the eyes of the other heroes. The creatures in the
sky cried out, lord of the people, when they saw the hero fallen like the moon
dropped from the sky. 'He lies killed, one man, by six great chariot fighters of
Dhritarashtra's side, led by Drona and Karna. We do not hold this to be dharma.'

"When the hero had been killed, the earth looked very fine, like the sky with
the full moon and garlanded with hosts of stars. It was full of golden-feathered
arrows and flooded with blood. It was covered with the shining heads of heroes,
with their earrings, with bright blankets and flags, with fly whisks and
saddlecloths and fine clothes thrown down. It was covered with the bright
ornaments of chariots, horses, men and elephants, and with sharp, yellow swords
like snakes that have shed their skins. It was covered with cut bows and arrows,
spears, lances, darts and kampanas, and with weapons of every other kind. The
ground was rough with the lifeless horses and their riders, brought down by
Subhadra's son, lying as if asleep, soaked in blood. Elephants lay struck down
by arrows, like shattered mountains, with their goads, riders, armour, weapons
and banners. Fine chariots lay scattered on the ground without horses, drivers
or fighters, like churned-up lakes where the elephants have been killed. Hosts
of foot soldiers lay dead, with weapons and ornaments of every kind. The earth
was terrible to see, and it frightened the timid.

"When they saw him fallen on the ground, bright as the moon and the sun, your
men were overjoyed and the Pandavas were in pain. When Abhimanyu, a child not
yet grown, had been killed, king, the whole army fled while the King of Dharma
looked on. When he saw the army breaking, now that Subhadra's son had fallen,
Ajatashatru spoke to his heroes. 'This hero has gone to heaven, killed without
turning his back. Stand firm! Don't be afraid! We will beat our enemies in
battle.' So the splendid King of Dharma, best of fighters, full of power, drove
away the grief of the grieving by what he said.

"In the battle Arjuna's son first killed many princes like poisonous snakes, and
only then followed them. Having killed ten thousand, and the great chariot
fighter of Kosala, Krishna's son, the equal of Krishna and Arjuna, has surely
gone to Shakra's house. Having killed chariots, horses, men and elephants by the
thousand, never sated with battle, he is not to be mourned, the doer of holy
deeds.

"We, having killed the best of them, and tormented by their arrows, went back to
camp in the evening, covered in blood. Looking at the battlefield, we and the
enemy slowly withdrew, great king, worn out and dazed. Then came the strange,
ill-omened meeting of night and day, full of the howls of jackals, when the sun,
like a crown of lotuses, hung low over the mountain of sunset. Taking away the
shine of fine swords, spears, lances, chariot screens, shields and ornaments,
bringing heaven and earth together, as it were, the sun went into the fire, its
dear body.

"The earth, heaped with many elephants like mountain peaks and great clouds, as
if struck down by thunderbolts, with their banners, goads, armour and riders
brought down, seemed to groan. The ground, with its great chariots smashed,
their masters killed, their foot soldiers and gear crushed, their horses and
drivers killed, without flags or banners, looked, lord of men, like cities
destroyed by enemies. With herds of chariots and horses killed with their
riders, their harness and ornaments of every kind scattered, their tongues,
teeth, entrails and eyes thrust out, the earth looked terrible and hideous. Men
lay with their armour and ornaments thrown about, with their fine weapons, their
elephants, horses, chariots and followers dead. They had always lain on costly
beds and coverlets, and now they lay dead on the ground like men with no one to
care for them.

"Dogs, jackals and crows, cranes, eagles, wolves and hyenas, birds that drink
blood, hosts of rakshasas and the most terrible bands of pishachas were
overjoyed. They tore through skins and drank fat and blood, ate marrow and
flesh, tore off the caul, laughed and sang, dragging corpses about in their
numbers. A river was set flowing by the best of warriors then, carrying heaps of
bodies. Blood was its water, chariots its rafts and elephants its rocks, with
men's heads for stones and flesh for mud, and it was garlanded with weapons of
every kind thrown about. It was terrible, hard to cross like the Vaitarani. It
ran fiercely through the middle of the battlefield, frightening, carrying the
living and the dead. There the many kinds of pishachas, dreadful and hideous to
see, drank and ate, delighted, terrifying to living things, with dogs, jackals
and birds sharing their food. So the men, watching that battlefield, fierce to
see, like the kingdom of the lord of the dead at the fall of night, crowded with
headless bodies rising up, slowly left it. There the people saw Abhimanyu, the
great chariot fighter, like Shakra, fallen in battle, his costly ornaments torn
off and scattered, like a fire at a sacrifice when the offerings have been taken
away.

"The hero, Subhadra's son, the leader of the chariot ranks, had been killed.
They all took off their chariot armour and laid down their bows, and sat round
King Yudhishthira, thinking only of that grief, their minds on Subhadra's son.
Then King Yudhishthira, in great sorrow, wept for Abhimanyu, the great chariot
fighter, his brother's son, now that the hero was dead.

"'Wanting to please me, he broke into Drona's army, where no one could pass,'
Yudhishthira said. 'He broke the array and went in like a lion among cattle. In
battle great archers who faced him, masters of weapons, mad for battle, turned
back broken. With his arrows he turned our deadly enemy Duhshasana away in
battle at once, senseless. The hero crossed that great ocean, Drona's army, so
hard to cross, and then Krishna's son met Duhshasana's son and went to
Vaivasvata's house. How will I look at Kunti's son Arjuna, now that Subhadra's
son has been killed? Or noble Subhadra, who will never see her dear son again?
What shall we say to the two of them, Hrishikesha and Dhananjaya, that is not
empty, broken and false? It was I who did this unkind thing to Subhadra, to
Keshava and to Arjuna, wanting what pleased me, longing for victory.

"'The greedy man does not see the danger. Greed moves him out of folly. Wanting
the honey, I did not see a fall like this. The boy we should have put first at
the table, in carriages, at bed and in adornment, we put first in battle. How
could a boy, young and not yet skilled in war, be safe in a tight place, like a
good horse on rough ground? If not, we too would lie on the ground with him,
burned by the pitiless eye of Bibhatsu, blazing with anger. He is free of greed,
wise, modest, patient, handsome and strong, well formed, a giver of honour, a
hero, dear, and devoted to truth. The wise praise his mighty deeds. The mighty
one killed the Nivatakavachas and the Kalakeyas. In the blink of an eye he
killed the enemies of great Indra, the Paulomas who lived in the golden city,
with all their hosts. The lord gives safety even to enemies who ask it. We could
not save his son from danger today. A very great fear has come to the great army
of Dhritarashtra's son. Partha, angry at his son's killing, will dry up the
Kauravas. Mean, with mean helpers, sick at the ruin of his own side, Duryodhana
will surely grieve and give up his life. I see him laid low, the grandson of the
best of the gods, whose courage and manhood had no match. Neither victory, nor
kingdom, nor immortality, nor a place in the gods' world gives me any joy.'"

**Divergences.**

* **How Abhimanyu dies.** Abhimanyu and Duhshasana's son strike each other down with their maces. Duhshasana's son rises first and strikes Abhimanyu on the head "as he was getting up" (7.48.11–13). The voices in the sky say he was killed "by six great chariot fighters … led by Drona and Karna", and that "we do not hold this to be dharma" (7.48.21). See ledger L-204.
* **No consolation by Vyasa here.** In the vulgate, Vyasa now comes to Yudhishthira and tells him the story of how Death was made and the stories of sixteen great kings. In the CE that whole passage is an appendix (@8), rejected; 7.49 ends with Yudhishthira's lament, and Arjuna comes back in 7.50. See ledger L-203.
* **"Burned by Bibhatsu's eye."** Yudhishthira says that if they had not sent the boy, Arjuna's anger would have burned them (7.49.14). The verse is difficult; the novel keeps its plain sense.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*329–\*342, @7, @8. \*329 says the wheel, if thrown, would have fallen like Vishnu's discus; @7 gives Abhimanyu hunger and thirst before he falls; \*336 is a father's lament not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 7.48.1 *viṣṇoḥ svasānandikaraḥ … viṣṇvāyudhabhūṣitaḥ*: "the delight of Vishnu's sister, adorned with Vishnu's weapon", Subhadra and the wheel.
* 7.48.7 *subaladāyādaṃ kālakeyam*: "Kalakeya, Subala's son".
* 7.48.8 *brahmavasātīyān*: "Brahma-Vasatiya chariot fighters", left as the text names them.
* 7.48.10 *tryambakāntakau*: "the Three-eyed God and Andhaka". The CE reads *antaka*; Ganguli's text has "Andhaka", the demon Shiva killed, which suits the simile of two fighters. The novel follows that sense.
* 7.48.17 *kākapakṣavṛtākṣakam*: "his eyes framed by his boyish locks", the side-locks of a boy.
* 7.48.21 *naiṣa dharmo mato hi naḥ*: "We do not hold this to be dharma."
* 7.48.42 *priyāṃ tanuṃ bhānur upaiti pāvakam*: "the sun went into the fire, its dear body", the old idea that the sun enters fire at night.
* 7.48.50 *vaitaraṇīva*: "like the Vaitarani", the river of the dead.
* 7.48.52 *samutthitāruṇḍakulopasaṃkulam*: "crowded with headless bodies rising up".
* 7.49.4 *gomadhyam iva kesarī*: "like a lion among cattle".
* 7.49.11 *madhu lipsuḥ … prapātam*: "wanting the honey, I did not see a fall", a proverb of the man who climbs for honey and does not see the drop.
* 7.49.16–17: Arjuna's deeds against the Nivatakavachas, the Kalakeyas and the Paulomas of the golden city (3.165–170).
