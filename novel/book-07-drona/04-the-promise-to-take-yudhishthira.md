summary: Sanjaya begins the full story. Drona offers Duryodhana a boon, and Duryodhana asks him to capture Yudhishthira alive, so that he can beat him again at dice and send the Pandavas back to the forest. Drona sees through him, and promises only on one condition, that Arjuna is drawn away from Yudhishthira's side. The promise is proclaimed through the camp. Yudhishthira hears of it, and Arjuna swears that Drona will never take his brother while he lives. On the eleventh day Drona burns through the Pandava ranks, making a river of blood. Single fights break out along the line: Sahadeva against Shakuni, Bhima against Vivimshati, Nakula against his uncle Shalya, and many more. Abhimanyu drags the Paurava king from his chariot by the hair, fights Jayadratha with sword and shield, catches the spear Shalya throws at him and hurls it back, killing Shalya's charioteer. The Kauravas close round him, and Shalya rushes at him in anger.

# The Promise to Take Yudhishthira

"Listen, and I will tell you everything," said Sanjaya, "for I saw it with my
own eyes, how Drona fell, brought down by the Pandavas and the Srinjayas.

"When Bharadvaja's son, the great chariot fighter, had been made commander, he
spoke to your son in the middle of the whole army. 'You have honoured me today,
king, by making me commander after Apaga's son, the bull of the Kauravas. Take
the reward that suits that deed, lord of the earth. What wish of yours shall I
grant today? Choose whatever you want.'

"Then Duryodhana thought it over with Karna, Duhshasana and the others. He said
to the teacher, the best of victors, whom no one could overcome, 'If you will
grant me a boon, then capture Yudhishthira, the best of chariot fighters, alive,
and bring him here to me.'

"When the teacher of the Kurus heard your son's words, he said this, and it
cheered the whole army. 'Kunti's son the king is fortunate, since you want him
captured. You do not ask me today for a boon to kill him, invincible one. Why do
you not want him dead, tiger among men? You are surely not asking me for this
deed, Duryodhana, without a reason. Or is it that the son of Dharma has no one
who hates him, since you want him alive and are protecting your family? Or else,
best of the Bharatas, do you want to beat the Pandavas in battle, give them back
a share of the kingdom and live in brotherhood with them? Kunti's son the king
is fortunate, and his birth was a good one. Wise as he is, his name Ajatashatru,
"the one with no enemy", is true, since even you love him.'

"When Drona said this to your son, Bharata, the feeling that always ruled him
came out at once. Not even those who are as wise as Brihaspati can hide their
faces. So your son, king, delighted, said, 'If Kunti's son is killed in battle,
teacher, that is no victory for me. If Yudhishthira is killed, Partha would
surely kill us all. And not even all the immortals could kill them all in
battle. Whichever of them was left would leave none of us alive. But if that
man, who is true to his word, is brought here and beaten again at dice, then the
sons of Kunti, who follow him, will go back to the forest. This would clearly be
a victory for me that would last a long time. That is why I never want the King
of Dharma killed.'

"Drona understood the truth of things. When he knew the crooked purpose behind
it, the wise man thought it over and gave him the boon with a condition.

"'If Arjuna is not guarding Yudhishthira in battle, hero,' said Drona, 'then
think of the eldest Pandava as brought here into your power. For Partha cannot
be faced in battle by the gods and the demons, even with Indra. So I do not dare
to go against him, my son. He is my pupil, beyond doubt, and his skill with
weapons comes from me first. But he is young, famous and set on one thing. He
has won more weapons from Indra and from Rudra, and he is angry with you, king.
So I will not face him. Let him be drawn away from the battle by any means you
can. When Partha has been taken away, you have beaten the King of Dharma. If you
think that capturing him is victory, bull among men, then by this means he will
surely be captured. Once Kunti's son Dhananjaya, the tiger among men, has been
drawn away, let the king stand before me in battle even for a moment. Then I
will capture him, devoted as he is to truth and dharma, and bring him into your
power today, king, beyond doubt. But with Phalguna there, Partha Yudhishthira
cannot be captured in battle, even by the gods and the demons with Indra.'

"When Drona had promised to capture the king, on that condition, your foolish
sons thought him already taken. Your son knew that Drona leaned towards the
Pandavas. So, to make the promise firm, he had the plan spread about. Then
Duryodhana had the capture of the Pandava proclaimed in every part of the army,
tamer of enemies.

"When the soldiers heard of the plan to capture Yudhishthira, they roared like
lions and made a din with arrows and conches. But the King of Dharma, Bharata,
soon learned everything that had happened, and what Bharadvaja's son meant to
do, from men he trusted. He called together all his brothers and all his troops,
and the King of Dharma said to Dhananjaya:

"'You have heard what Drona means to do today, tiger among men. Let us plan so
that it does not come true. Drona has made his promise on a condition, tamer of
enemies, and the condition rests on you, whose arrows never miss. So fight near
me today, strong-armed one, so that Duryodhana does not get this wish of his
from Drona.'

"'Just as I must never kill my teacher,' said Arjuna, 'so I have no wish to
leave you, king. I would rather give up my life in battle, Pandava. I will never
go against the teacher, and I will never leave you. What Dhritarashtra's son
wants, to capture you in battle, he will never get in this world of the living.
The sky with its stars may fall and the earth may split into pieces, but Drona
will surely not capture you while I live. Even if the one who holds the
thunderbolt helps him in battle himself, with the gods or the demons, he will
not take you. While I live, king of kings, you need not fear Drona, the best of
those who bear weapons and of all who bear arms. I do not remember ever speaking
a lie. I do not remember defeat. I do not remember ever promising anything and
not doing it.'

"Then conches and drums, tabors and kettledrums sounded in the Pandavas' camp,
great king. The great Pandavas roared like lions, and the terrible slap of
bowstrings rose to the sky. When they heard the sound of the great Pandava's
conches, they struck up music in your ranks too. Then the arrays of your army
and theirs, Bharata, came slowly towards each other, ready to fight.

"Then the battle began, wild and hair-raising, between the Pandavas and the
Kurus, and between Drona and the Panchala. The Srinjayas strove with all their
might to break Drona's ranks, king, but they could not, for Drona guarded them.
In the same way your son's great chariot fighters, strikers all, could not break
the Pandava army, for the diademed one guarded it. The two armies stood still,
each guarding against the other, like two rows of flowering trees in a forest
asleep at night.

"Then the warrior of the golden chariot, king, broke out in his screened
chariot, shining like the sun, and ranged through the armies. The Pandavas and
the Srinjayas saw him coming at them in battle, one chariot moving so fast, and
in their terror they thought he was many. The terrible arrows he loosed flew in
every direction, great king, and terrified the Pandava's army. Drona was seen
like the sun at midday, wrapped in a hundred rays. No one among the Pandavas
could look at him in battle, sir, in his anger, any more than the danavas could
look at great Indra. Mighty Bharadvaja's son threw the army into confusion, and
then at once he scattered Dhrishtadyumna's troops with his sharp arrows. He
blocked every direction and covered the sky with his straight arrows, and
wherever Parshata was, there he crushed the Pandava army.

"Then Drona ranged over the Pandavas, making a great uproar in the Pandava army,
like a fire burning dry brush. When the Srinjayas saw the warrior of the golden
chariot in battle, burning up the ranks like fire itself risen up, they
trembled. As the quick bowman shot on and on, the twang of his bow was heard,
loud as the crash of a thunderbolt. The terrible arrows loosed by his skilled
hand crushed chariot fighters and horsemen, elephants, horses and foot soldiers.
Like a thundering cloud at the end of the hot season, driven by the wind, he
rained down a hail of stones and brought fear to the enemy. The lord ranged over
the army then, king, throwing it into turmoil, and he raised the enemy's terror
beyond anything human. His bow, set with gold, was seen again and again,
whirling in that cloud of a chariot, like lightning in the clouds.

"That hero, truthful, wise and always devoted to dharma, was terrible then. As
if he were the one who drives all at the end of an age, he set flowing a
dreadful river. It sprang from the force of his rage and was full of flocks of
flesh-eaters. It was filled on all sides by floods of troops and carried off
heroes like trees. Its water was blood, its whirlpools were chariots, and
elephants and horses made its banks. Armour was its rafts, and it was thick with
a mud of flesh. Fat, marrow and bone were its sand, and fine turbans were its
foam. It was filled by the rain cloud of battle and crowded with lances for
fish. It flowed from men, elephants and horses, and carried the flood of the
rush of arrows. Bodies were its logs, arms were its snakes, heads were its
stones, and swords were its fish. Chariots and elephants were its pools, and
ornaments of every kind its lotuses. Hundreds of great chariot fighters were its
eddies, and the dust of the earth its waves. It was easy for the very strong to
cross in battle, and hard for the timid. Heroes were its beasts of prey, and
those who trade in lives came to it. Broken umbrellas were its great swans, and
crowns were its birds. Wheels were its turtles, maces its crocodiles, and arrows
its little fish, and it was haunted by terrible flocks of cranes, vultures and
jackals. It carried hundreds of living things, killed in battle by mighty
Drona's arrows, to the world of the ancestors, best of kings. Crowded with
hundreds of bodies, with hair for its weed and grass, it was a river that raised
the fear of the timid, king, and Drona set it flowing.

"As Drona beat those ranks one after another, Bharata, the Pandavas led by
Yudhishthira rushed at him from every side. Your men, with their strong bows,
met the heroes as they charged from every side, and it was hair-raising.

"Shakuni, the man of a hundred tricks, rushed at Sahadeva and pierced him, with
his charioteer, his banner and his chariot, with sharp arrows. But Madri's son
was not much angered. He cut down his banner, bow, charioteer and horses with
his arrows and pierced the uncle with sixty. Then Subala's son took up a mace
and leapt down from his fine chariot, and with his mace, king, he knocked
Sahadeva's charioteer from the chariot. Then both mighty heroes, king, without
their chariots and with maces in their hands, played in the battle like two
mountains with their peaks.

"Drona pierced the king of Panchala with ten swift arrows. The king struck him
back with many, and Drona pierced him with more than a hundred.

"Bhimasena pierced Vivimshati with twenty sharp arrows, but the hero did not
waver, and it was a marvel. Then in a moment Vivimshati left Bhima without
horses, banner or bow, great king, and the armies cheered him. The hero could
not bear his enemy's victory in the battle, and with his mace he brought down
all his well-trained horses.

"Brave Shalya, laughing, pierced his dear sister's son Nakula with arrows, as if
he were playing with him and teasing him. Mighty Nakula brought down his horses,
his umbrella and banner, his charioteer and his bow in the battle, and blew his
conch.

"Dhrishtaketu cut down the many kinds of arrows Kripa shot at him, pierced Kripa
with seventy, and struck off his emblem with three. Kripa covered him with a
great shower of arrows, and the brahmin held him back in the battle and fought
Dhrishtaketu.

"Satyaki, as if smiling, pierced Kritavarma in the chest with an iron arrow, and
then pierced him with seventy others. The Bhoja pierced him with seventy-seven
sharp arrows, but he could not shake Shini's grandson, as a swift wind cannot
shake a mountain.

"The commander struck Susharma swiftly in his vital parts, and Susharma struck
him with a lance on the collarbone.

"Virata held back Vaikartana in the battle, with his mighty Matsyas, and it was
a marvel. Terrible was the charioteer's son's manhood there, as he held back the
army with his smooth-jointed arrows.

"King Drupada himself met Bhagadatta, and their battle, great king, was a thing
to see. Those two masters of weapons terrified all living things.

"Mighty Bhurishravas covered Yajnasena's son, the great chariot fighter, with a
great flood of arrows in the battle, king. Then Shikhandi in his anger shook
Somadatta's son with ninety arrows, lord of the people.

"The two rakshasas of terrible deeds, Hidimba's son and Alambusa, fought a
marvellous fight, each wanting to kill the other. Proud, casting a hundred
spells, they vanished by their spells and moved about unseen, to everyone's
amazement.

"Chekitana fought Anuvinda most fearfully, like the mighty Bala and Shakra in
the battle of the gods and demons.

"Lakshmana fought hard with Kshatradeva, king, as Vishnu once fought Hiranyaksha
in battle.

"Then the Paurava, with a shout, charged at Subhadra's son in a chariot rightly
fitted out, with swift horses, king. Mighty Abhimanyu, the tamer of enemies,
eager for battle, went to meet him quickly and fought a great fight with him.
The Paurava covered Subhadra's son with swarms of arrows. Arjuna's son brought
down his banner, umbrella and bow to the ground. Subhadra's son pierced the
Paurava with seven more swift arrows, and his horses and charioteer with five.
Then, cheering his army and roaring again and again like a lion, Arjuna's son
quickly took up an arrow that would be the Paurava's end. Hardikya cut his bow,
with the arrow on it, with two arrows. Subhadra's son, the killer of enemy
heroes, threw away the broken bow, drew his bright sword and took up his shield.

"With that shield, set with many stars, and his sword whirling, he moved through
the various passes like a skilled hand, showing his strength. Whirling and
wheeling, shaking and raising them up, king, his shield and sword could not be
told apart. With a shout he leapt at once onto the pole of the Paurava's
chariot, climbed into the chariot and seized the Paurava by the hair. He kicked
his charioteer to death, brought down his banner with his sword, and threw the
Paurava down, as Tarkshya throws down a snake after churning up the ocean. All
the kings saw him with his hair seized, senseless, being brought down like an ox
by a lion. When Jayadratha saw the Paurava fallen, in Arjuna's son's power,
being dragged like a man with no protector, he could not bear it. He took his
shield, with its great peacock emblem and its net of hundreds of little bells,
and his sword, and leapt from his chariot with a shout.

"When he saw the Saindhava, Krishna's son let the Paurava go, sprang quickly
from the chariot and swooped down like a hawk. With his sword Krishna's son cut
down the lances, axes and swords his enemies hurled at him, and stopped them
with his shield. The mighty one showed the armies the strength of his own arms.
Then he raised his great sword and his shield again and went straight at
Vriddhakshatra's son, the deadly enemy of his father, as a tiger goes at an
elephant. They met and struck at each other gladly, like a tiger and a lion,
with swords for teeth and claws. In the rush and the strike and the downward
blow of sword and shield, no one could see any opening between those two lions
among men. The thrust and the ring of the sword, the feint, and the blows
outside and inside the guard could not be told apart. The two great men moved
through the finest passes, outside and inside, and looked like two mountains
with wings.

"Then, as famous Subhadra's son swung his sword, Jayadratha struck at the edge
of his shield. Driven by the Sindhu king's strength, the great sword stuck
between the golden bands of that shining shield and broke. When Krishna's son
knew his sword was broken, he leapt back six paces, and in the blink of an eye
he was seen in his own chariot again. When Krishna's son was free of the fight
and in his fine chariot, all the kings together surrounded him on every side.
Then the mighty son of Arjuna raised his shield and sword and roared, looking at
Jayadratha.

"Leaving the Sindhu king, Subhadra's son, the killer of enemy heroes, burned up
that army as the sun burns the world. Shalya hurled at him in the battle a
terrible spear made all of iron, adorned with gold, blazing like a tongue of
fire. Krishna's son leapt up and caught it, as Vinata's son catches a great
snake as it falls, and sheathed his sword. When they saw his speed and the
spirit of that man of boundless power, all the kings roared like lions together.
Then with the strength of his arm Subhadra's son, the killer of enemy heroes,
hurled that same spear, its shaft set with cat's-eye gems, back at Shalya. It
reached Shalya's chariot like a snake that has shed its skin, killed Shalya's
charioteer and knocked him from the chariot.

"Then Virata and Drupada, Dhrishtaketu and Yudhishthira, Satyaki, the Kekayas
and Bhima, Dhrishtadyumna and Shikhandi, the twins and the sons of Draupadi
cried out, 'Well done! Well done!' The sound of many arrows and loud lions'
roars went up, cheering Subhadra's son, who never ran. Your sons could not bear
these signs of the enemy's victory. They all fell on him at once from every side
with sharp arrows, great king, as clouds pour rain on a mountain. Then, wanting
to please them, and angry at the fall of his charioteer, Artayani, the killer of
enemies, rushed at Subhadra's son."

**Divergences.**

* **"Capture him alive."** Duryodhana's reason is his own: if Yudhishthira were killed, Arjuna would kill them all; if he is brought back and beaten again at dice, the Pandavas will go back to the forest (7.11.15–18). Drona grants the boon only "with a condition" (*sāntaram*): that Arjuna be drawn away (7.11.19–28). The CE says that Duryodhana knew Drona leaned towards the Pandavas (*sāpekṣam*, 7.11.30), and so had the promise proclaimed.
* **The eleventh day.** This is the first day of Drona's command, the eleventh of the war. The CE does not number it; it follows from the ten days of Bhishma (6.104.38) and Drona's appointment the next morning.
* **"The uncle."** At 7.13.22 Sahadeva pierces "the uncle" (*mātulam*), Shakuni, as the Kauravas' uncle. Sahadeva's own uncle is Shalya, who fights Nakula a few lines later (7.13.29).
* **Shalya's charioteer.** The spear kills Shalya's charioteer and "knocks him from the chariot" (7.13.76). Shalya himself lives, and rushes at Abhimanyu (7.13.80).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*87–\*112. \*90 has Duryodhana foresee Vishnu giving the earth to Draupadi; \*109 has Yudhishthira and Duryodhana fight each other here.
