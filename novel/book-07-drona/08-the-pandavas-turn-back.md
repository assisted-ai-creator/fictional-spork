summary: Dhritarashtra asks for the marks of the chariots of the heroes who turned back against Drona, and Sanjaya tells him instead of their horses, hero by hero, from Bhima's antelope-coloured team and Satyaki's silver horses to Yudhishthira's golden ones. The king reflects that fate yokes a man and drags him where it will, and that Yudhishthira, once an exile in the forest, now has half the world on his side. Then Sanjaya tells how the Pandavas turned back and closed round Drona, and how Duryodhana's men met them pair by pair: Durmarshana and Bhima, Kritavarma and Satyaki, Jayadratha and Kshatradharman, Shalya and Yudhishthira, Bahlika and Drupada, and many more. Yuyutsu cuts off the arms of his brother Subahu; Bhurishravas cuts Maniman to pieces with his sword; Ghatotkacha and Alambusa fight like Shambara and Indra.

# The Pandavas Turn Back

"Tell me the marks of the chariots of all of them, Sanjaya," said Dhritarashtra,
"all those who turned back in anger against Drona, led by Bhima."

"When he saw Wolf-belly striving, with his horses the colour of the black
antelope," said Sanjaya, "the brave grandson of Shini, with his silver horses,
turned back. Handsome Kamboja horses, covered in trappings like parrots'
feathers, carried Nakula swiftly as he charged your men. Black horses like
clouds carried Sahadeva with his weapon raised, horses of terrible speed, swift
as the wind, and they carried the tiger among men. With horses as swift as the
wind, covered in the finest gold, all the ranks turned back behind Yudhishthira.

"Next to the king was King Drupada of Panchala, with a golden umbrella, guarded
by all his own men. With his horses marked on the forehead and yoked, bays that
could bear every noise of battle, the great archer came on among the kings, free
of fear. Virata followed him, with brave great chariot fighters. The Kekayas,
Shikhandi and Dhrishtaketu, each surrounded by his own troops, followed the king
of the Matsyas. The fine horses that carried the Matsya, the killer of his
enemies, were the colour of trumpet flowers, and they looked splendid. Swift
horses the colour of turmeric, with golden garlands, carried the son of King
Virata at speed. The five Kekaya brothers had horses the colour of cochineal
beetles. They shone like gold, and all of them had red banners. The heroes, all
wearing golden garlands, all skilled in battle, were seen in their armour like
clouds shedding rain. Tame horses, copper-red, the colour of unbaked clay pots,
carried Shikhandi of Panchala, of boundless power. There were twelve thousand
great chariot fighters of the Panchalas, and six thousand of them followed
Shikhandi.

"Horses dappled like antelopes, playing as they went, carried the son of
Shishupala, the lion among men, sir. Dhrishtaketu, the bull of the Chedis, very
strong and hard to beat, came on with dappled Kamboja horses. Fine horses of
Sindh, the colour of smoke from burning straw, swiftly carried young
Brihatkshatra of the Kekayas. Well-adorned horses bred in Bahli, the colour of
lotuses, with eyes like jasmine, carried Shikhandi's brave son Kshatradeva. Fine
horses the colour of herons carried young Sukumara, the great chariot fighter,
son of Abhibhu of Kashi, into battle. White horses with black necks, swift as
thought, obedient to their driver, carried Prince Prativindhya, king.

"Horses the colour of bean flowers carried Sutasoma into battle, the son whom
Partha asked for from Dhaumya. They shone like a thousand moons. He was born in
the city of the Kurus called Udayendu, amid the cries of the soma pressing, and
so he was named Sutasoma. Horses the colour of shala flowers, like the young
sun, carried Nakula's praiseworthy son Shatanika. Horses like peacocks' necks,
with golden traces, carried Draupadi's son Shrutakarman, the tiger among men.
Fine horses like the feathers of the blue jay carried Draupadi's son
Shrutakirti, a treasury of learning, Partha's equal in battle. Tawny horses
carried young Abhimanyu into battle, whom they call half as good again as
Krishna and Partha in battle. Great horses of huge size carried Yuyutsu into
battle, the one son of Dhritarashtra who had joined the Pandavas.

"Horses the colour of straw stalks, glad and well adorned, carried mighty
Vriddhakshema's son in that wild battle. Horses with black feet, with golden
breastplates, obedient to their driver, carried young Saucitti into battle.
Horses like silk, their backs strewn with gold, with golden garlands and
patient, carried Shreniman. Fine brave horses, the colour of gold, wearing
golden garlands and well adorned, carried the praiseworthy king of Kashi. Red
horses carried Satyadhriti as he came, a master of weapons, of archery and of
the Veda. Horses the colour of doves carried Dhrishtadyumna, the Panchala
commander, who had taken Drona as his share. After him came Satyadhriti,
Saucitti, mad for battle, Shreniman, Vasudana and the son of Abhibhu of Kashi.
With swift horses of the finest Kamboja breed, with golden garlands yoked to
them, they terrified the enemy army, like Yama and Vaishravana. Six thousand
Prabhadrakas of Panchala followed Dhrishtadyumna, ready to die together. Their
weapons were raised, their horses were of many colours, and their chariots and
banners were bright with gold. With their bows drawn, they shook the enemy with
swarms of arrows.

"Fine horses the colour of brown silk, with the best golden garlands, carried
Chekitana, and they never flagged. Kuntibhoja Purujit, Savyasachin's maternal
uncle, came with fine, well-trained horses the colour of the rainbow. Horses the
colour of the sky, as if spangled with stars, carried King Rochamana in battle.
Fine spotted horses with black feet and trappings of golden net carried
Jarasandha's son Sahadeva. Fine horses the colour of lotus stalks, as swift as
hawks and bright, carried Sudama. Horses the colour of a hare's blood, with
white streaks, carried the Panchala Simhasena, the son of Gopati. The tiger
among men of the Panchalas who was famous as Janamejaya had fine horses the
colour of mustard flowers. Swift, great horses the colour of beans, with golden
garlands, with backs like curds and faces like the moon, carried a Panchala at
speed. Brave, fine horses, like reed stalks, the colour of lotus filaments,
carried Dandadhara. Horses with bellies like the ruddy goose, wearing golden
garlands, carried Sukshatra, the son of the lord of Kosala. Tall, fine, dappled
horses, tame and with golden garlands, carried Satyadhriti, Kshema's son, into
battle. Shukla came on all white, his banner, armour, horses and bow all of one
colour. Horses born by the sea, like the moon, carried Chandradeva,
Samudrasena's son, whose power was like Rudra's. Horses the colour of blue water
lilies, adorned with gold, carried Shaibya Chitraratha, with his bright garland,
into battle. Fine horses the colour of pea flowers, with white and red streaks,
carried Rathasena, mad for battle. Horses the colour of parrots carried the king
they call braver than any man, the killer of the Patachcharas. Fine horses the
colour of kimshuka flowers carried Chitrayudha, with his bright garland, his
bright armour, weapons and banner. Nila came on all blue, his banner, armour,
bow and chariot horses all of one colour. Chitra came on, bright with chariot
screen, banner and bow of many forms, set with jewels, with bright horses,
banners and flags.

"Fine horses the colour of lotus leaves carried Rochamana's son Hemavarna.
Horses white-bellied like hens' eggs, born of reed and cane, carried Dandaketu.
Horses the colour of the atarushaka flower carried the followers of the Pandya,
fourteen ten-thousands of the finest chariot fighters. Horses of many colours,
with faces of many shapes, carried the hero Ghatotkacha, whose banner bore a
chariot wheel. Fine golden horses followed behind Yudhishthira, the best of
kings, who knows dharma, as he stood among his ranks. With fine horses of every
colour, heavenly and earthly, those heroes with golden banners stood ready with
Bhimasena, lord of kings, like the gods with Indra. Dhrishtadyumna outshone all
of them as they came together, and Bharadvaja's son outshone all the armies."

"These chariot fighters who turned back in battle, led by Wolf-belly, could
shake even the army of the gods," said Dhritarashtra. "A man, it seems, is yoked
by fate, and in him all things are seen, each in its own way. For a long time
Yudhishthira lived in exile in the forest, with matted hair and a deer skin, and
wandered unknown to the world. And now he has brought a great army back into
battle. What else has come to my son, but what fate has brought together? Surely
a man is born yoked to his fortune, and it drags him where it will, not where he
wants to go himself. Yudhishthira suffered through his passion for dice, and by
his fortune he has found allies again.

"'I have won half the Kekayas, and those of Kashi and Kosala,' my foolish
Duryodhana told me then, charioteer. 'The Chedis and the rest of the Vangas have
come to me. More of the earth is mine, my son, than Partha's.' In the middle of
that great host Drona, well guarded, has been killed by Parshata in battle. What
else is this but fate? How did Drona meet his death, strong-armed, among the
kings, always delighting in battle, a master of every weapon? I have come into
great trouble, and I am deeply bewildered. Now I have heard that Bhishma and
Drona are dead, I do not want to live.

"All that the chamberlain told me, my son, when he saw how I doted on my son,
has come to me, charioteer, and to Duryodhana. It would be very cruel if I were
to abandon Duryodhana and try to save what is left of my sons. But then death
would not be so hard. For the man who gives up dharma and cares only for gain is
cut off from this world and sinks into meanness. Now that its crest is crushed
and its spirit is broken, Sanjaya, I see nothing left of this kingdom. How could
anything be left, now that the two who bore the yoke have gone before us, the
two patient bulls among men on whom we always lived?

"Tell me clearly how the battle went. Who fought? Who drew back? Which cowards
ran in fear? And tell me of Dhananjaya, all that the bull among chariot fighters
did. We fear him most of all, and above all because he is our kin. Tell me,
Sanjaya, how that terrible clash went when the Pandavas turned back and met what
was left of my army, and which of my heroes held back which of theirs."

"There was great terror among us when the Pandavas turned back," said Sanjaya,
"and we saw Drona covered by them, like the sun by clouds. The thick dust they
raised covered your army, and when he was lost from our sight we thought Drona
dead. When Duryodhana saw those brave great archers bent on cruel work, he
quickly urged on his own army. 'Kings,' he said, 'hold back the Pandava army as
best you can, each with all your strength, your spirit and your courage.'

"Then your son Durmarshana, seeing Bhima from far off, went at him, scattering
arrows, wanting to save Drona's life. He covered him with arrows in the battle,
in his anger, as if he were Death, and Bhima wounded him with arrows. It was a
wild fight. Commanded by their lord, the wise and brave strikers put their fear
of death behind them and stood up to the enemy in battle.

"Kritavarma held back Shini's son as he came on, seeking Drona, a hero who
graced the battle. Shaineya in his anger held back the angry man with swarms of
arrows, and Kritavarma held back Shaineya, like one elephant in rut another.

"The Saindhava, with his fierce bow, stood ready and held back the great archer
Kshatradharman from Drona as he came on, pouring floods of arrows.
Kshatradharman cut the Sindhu king's banner and bow, and in his anger struck him
in all his vital parts with many iron arrows. The Saindhava took up another bow
with a practised hand and pierced Kshatradharman in the battle with arrows made
all of iron.

"Subahu stood ready and held back his brother Yuyutsu, the great chariot
fighter, who was striving for the Pandavas, from Drona. As Subahu shot, Yuyutsu
cut off both his arms, like iron bars, holding bow and arrows, with two sharp,
yellow razor-headed arrows.

"The king of the Madras held back Yudhishthira, the best of the Pandavas, the
righteous king, as the shore holds back the stirred-up sea. The King of Dharma
covered him with many arrows that split the vital parts. The lord of the Madras
pierced him with sixty-four arrows and roared loudly. As he roared, the best of
the Pandavas cut down his banner and his bow with two razor-headed arrows, and
the people cheered.

"In the same way King Bahlika, with his army, held back King Drupada with his
army as he came on with arrows. The fight between the two old men with their
armies was terrible, like that of two great leaders of herds, two elephants in
rut. Vinda and Anuvinda of Avanti, with their armies, went at Virata the Matsya
with his army, as Indra and Agni once went at Bali. That fight of the Matsyas
with the Kekayas was a tangle, like the battle of the gods and the demons, with
horses, chariots and elephants that knew no fear.

"Bhutakarman, the lord of the assembly, held back Nakula's son Shatanika from
Drona as he came on, shooting nets of arrows. Then Nakula's son, with three
well-sharpened broad arrows, left Bhutakarman in the battle without his arms or
his head.

"Vivimshati held back brave Sutasoma as he came on at Drona, pouring floods of
arrows. Sutasoma in his anger pierced his uncle Vivimshati with straight arrows,
but the armoured man did not turn away.

"Then Bhimaratha, with six swift, sharp iron arrows, sent Shalva to Yama's house
with his horses and his driver. Chitrasena's son, your grandson, great king,
held back Shrutakarman as he came on with horses like peacocks. Those two
grandsons of yours, both hard to overcome, each wanting to kill the other,
fought a splendid battle to win their fathers' ends.

"When Drona's son saw Prativindhya standing in front of him in the battle, he
held him back with his arrows, for his father's honour. Prativindhya in his
anger pierced him back with sharp arrows, as he stood for his father's sake,
with the lion's tail as his emblem. As a man sows seed in the sowing season,
bull among men, Drona's son covered Draupadi's son with showers of arrows.

"Lakshmana held back the killer of the Patachcharas, who was thought the bravest
man in both armies, king. He cut Lakshmana's bow and his emblem, Bharata, and
shone as he loosed nets of arrows at Lakshmana.

"Wise Vikarna, a young man, held back young Shikhandi, Yajnasena's son, as he
came on in battle. Yajnasena's son covered him with a net of arrows. Your mighty
son shook off that net of arrows and shone.

"Angada met brave Uttamaujas in battle as he went at Drona, and held him back
with calf-tooth arrows. The clash of those two lions among men was fierce, and
it pleased all the soldiers and the two of them.

"Mighty Durmukha, the great archer, held back the hero Purujit Kuntibhoja as he
went at Drona. Purujit struck Durmukha between the brows with an iron arrow, and
his face looked like a lotus on its stalk.

"Karna held back the five Kekaya brothers, with their red banners, with showers
of arrows as they went at Drona. In a fury they covered him with swarms of
arrows, and he covered them again and again with nets of arrows. Neither Karna
nor the five could be seen, hidden by arrows, horses, drivers, banners and
chariots, all heaped with each other's arrows.

"Your sons Durjaya, Jaya and Vijaya, three heroes, held back three: Nila, the
king of Kashi, and Jaya. That fight was terrible, and it delighted those who
watched, like the fight of lions, tigers and hyenas with elephants, buffaloes
and bulls.

"The two brothers Kshemadhurti and Brihanta cut the Satvata with sharp arrows as
he went at Drona. The fight between those two and him was a marvel, like that of
a lion with two great elephants in rut in the forest.

"The king of the Chedis in his anger held back from Drona the king of the
Ambashthas, alone and delighting in battle, shooting arrows at him. The
Ambashtha pierced him with a bone-splitting shaft, and he dropped his bow and
arrow and fell from his chariot to the ground.

"Kripa, Sharadvat's son, no small man, held back the Varshneya, Vriddhakshema's
son, from Drona with small arrows, in his fury. Those who watched Kripa and the
Varshneya fighting, wonderful fighters both, were so caught up in their fight
that they did not notice anything else.

"Somadatta's son, adding to Drona's glory, held back King Maniman, who never
tired, as he came on. Maniman quickly cut Somadatta's son's bow and banner, and
then brought down his flag, his driver and his umbrella from the chariot. Then
the killer of his enemies, with the sacrificial post on his banner, leapt
quickly from his chariot, and with his fine sword he cut Maniman to pieces, with
his horses, driver, banner and chariot. He mounted his own chariot, took up
another bow, drove his horses himself, king, and scattered the Pandava army.

"With pestles, hammers and discuses, with slings and axes, with dust, wind, fire
and water, with ashes, clods, grass and trees, Ghatotkacha came on, wanting to
reach Drona. He crushed, shattered, broke, killed, routed and hurled, and he
terrified the army. In his anger the rakshasa Alambusa struck the rakshasa with
every kind of weapon and every trick of fighting. The fight between those two
chiefs of the rakshasa hosts was like the one long ago between Shambara and the
king of the immortals.

"So there were hundreds of single fights, of chariots, elephants and horses and
foot soldiers, a tangle of your men and theirs, bless you. Such a battle had
never been seen before, nor heard of, as the one fought by those bent on Drona's
life or death. 'This one is terrible! This one is wonderful! This one is
fierce!' Many such fights were seen there, spread out over the field, lord."

**Divergences.**

* **Horses, not banners.** The king asks for "the marks of the chariots" (*rathacihnāni*, 7.22.1), but in the CE Sanjaya describes the heroes' horses. The description of their banners (Drona's water pot, Bhima's lion and so on) is in an appendix passage (@5), rejected by the CE, and is not narrated.
* **Simhasena.** 7.22.43 names "the Panchala Simhasena, the son of Gopati", among those who turn back. A Simhasena of Panchala was killed by Drona on the eleventh day (7.15.37). The CE does not say whether they are the same man.
* **Shishupala's son and Dhrishtaketu.** 7.22.15 names "the son of Shishupala" and 7.22.16 "Dhrishtaketu, the bull of the Chedis", one after the other. Elsewhere Dhrishtaketu is Shishupala's son; the novel keeps both lines as the text has them.
* **Yuyutsu and Subahu.** Yuyutsu, the son of Dhritarashtra on the Pandava side, cuts off the arms of his brother Subahu (7.24.13–14).
* **Maniman.** Bhurishravas, "with the sacrificial post on his banner" (*yūpaketu*), kills Maniman with his sword (7.24.51–53).
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*159–\*172 and @5. \*167 tells the Pandya king's story; \*168 tells how Yuyutsu left the Kauravas.
