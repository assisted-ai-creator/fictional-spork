summary: Dhritarashtra asks how Bhima could fight Karna, whom Yudhishthira always feared. Karna calls Bhima back as he turns towards Arjuna, and fights him gently while Bhima, remembering the old wrongs, fights in anger; Bhima cuts his bow, kills his horses and driver and drives him off. Karna comes back in another chariot, and Bhima, recalling the dice, Draupadi's torment and Karna's taunts, fights like a man tired of life. Dhritarashtra blames his own weakness and his son's greed. Bhima cuts Karna's spear from the air and leaves him chariotless again; Duryodhana sends his brother Durjaya, and Bhima kills him while Karna weeps. Karna returns once more; Bhima smashes his chariot with his mace, kills Durmukha when he brings Karna a chariot, and the two wound each other until Karna leaves the field.

# Bhima and Karna

Dhritarashtra said, "Karna and Bhima, both of them so strong, fought each other
near Arjuna's chariot. What was that battle like? Karna had been beaten by
Bhimasena in the fight before. How did Radheya, the great warrior, go at Bhima
again? And how did Bhima go out in battle to meet the son of the suta, famous as
a great warrior, the finest chariot fighter on earth? Yudhishthira, the son of
Dharma, got past Bhishma and Drona and feared no one else, except Karna the
bowman. He never sleeps for fear, always thinking of that great warrior. How did
Bhima fight that son of the suta in battle? Karna is devoted to brahmins, full
of courage, never turning back in battle. How did Bhima fight Karna, the best of
fighters?

"Those two heroes met near Arjuna's chariot. How did they fight, the son of the
suta and Vrikodara? Their brotherhood was made known to Karna before, and the
son of the suta is merciful. How did he fight Bhima, remembering Kunti's words?
Or how did the hero Bhima fight Karna in battle, remembering the old feud with
the son of the suta?

"My son Duryodhana always hopes, Suta, that Karna will beat all the Pandavas
together in battle. My foolish son's hope of victory in battle rests on him. How
did he fight Bhimasena of the terrible deeds? It was with him to lean on that my
sons, those great warriors, made this feud. How did Bhima fight that son of the
suta, my friend? How did Bhima fight the son of the suta, remembering the many
wrongs that came from him? He conquered the whole earth with a single chariot,
so great was his courage. How did Bhima fight that son of the suta in battle? He
was born with earrings, and with armour too. How did Bhima fight the son of the
suta in battle? Tell me truly how the battle between them went, and which of
them won, Sanjaya, for you tell it well."

Sanjaya said, "Bhimasena left Radheya, the best of chariot fighters, and wanted
to go to where the two heroes Krishna and Dhananjaya were. As he went, Radheya
ran after him and rained heron-feathered arrows on him, great king, as a cloud
rains on a mountain. Smiling with a face like a lotus in bloom, the strong son
of Adhiratha called to Bhima in the battle as he went. Bhimasena could not bear
Karna's call in the battle. He wheeled round in a half circle and fought the son
of the suta.

"He rained straight arrows, heavy with iron, on Karna, the best of all who bear
weapons, armoured and careful for single combat. Wanting to end the quarrel and
to kill Karna, the mighty man pressed him, and after killing him he meant to
kill all the others. The angry Pandava, tamer of enemies, who could not bear an
insult, loosed fierce rains of arrows of many kinds at him, sir. The famous son
of the suta swallowed up those rains of arrows with the craft of his weapons, as
Bhima came on with the stride of a rutting elephant. Karna, the great bowman,
honoured for his learning as was right, ranged the battle like a teacher.
Radheya, as if smiling, met Vrikodara as he fought in his fury, though he could
not bear him.

"The son of Kunti could not bear Karna's smile in battle, with heroes fighting
and looking on from every side. As Karna came close, mighty Bhimasena in his
anger pierced him between the breasts with calf-tooth arrows, as men goad a
great elephant. He split the son of the suta's charioteer, in his bright armour,
with twenty-one sharp, well-feathered arrows, well loosed. Karna, the hero,
pierced each of Bhima's horses, fast as the wind and covered with nets of gold,
with five arrows. Then a net of arrows made by Karna was seen round Bhimasena's
chariot in half the blink of an eye, king. The Pandava, with his chariot, his
banner and his charioteer, was hidden, great king, by the arrows from Karna's
bow.

"Karna shattered his strong armour with sixty-four arrows, and in anger struck
him in the side with iron arrows that split the vital parts. Vrikodara took no
notice of the swift arrows from Karna's bow. He did not lose his head, and he
closed with the son of the suta. Bhima bore the arrows from Karna's bow, like
venomous snakes, great king, and he felt no pain in the battle. Then mighty
Bhimasena pierced Karna in the battle with thirty-two sharp, keen broad arrows.
Karna, without effort, covered strong-armed Bhimasena with arrows as he sought
the Saindhava's death. Radheya fought Bhima gently in the battle, and Bhima
fought in anger, remembering the old feud.

"Bhimasena, who could not bear it, would not bear the insult. The conqueror of
his enemies quickly loosed a rain of arrows at him. The dreadful arrows sent by
Bhimasena in the battle, king, fell on every side like birds calling. With
golden feathers, great king, loosed from Bhimasena's bow, they ran at Radheya as
wolves run at a small deer. Karna, the best of chariot fighters, covered on
every side, loosed fierce rains of arrows in the battle, king. Before his arrows
could reach him, arrows like thunderbolts, bright in battle, Vrikodara cut them
with many broad arrows. Again Karna Vaikartana covered Bhimasena, the great
warrior, with a rain of arrows in the battle, Bharata.

"There, Bharata, we saw Bhima in the battle with his body so thick with arrows
that he looked like a porcupine with its quills. The hero bore in the battle the
arrows from Karna's bow, golden-feathered and washed on stone, as the sun bears
its own rays. Bhimasena, his whole body drenched in blood, shone like a palasha
tree in a forest with flowers like gold.

"But Bhima, the great bowman, would not bear what Karna was doing in the battle,
great king. Rolling his eyes in anger, he drove twenty-five iron arrows into
Karna, as poisonous snakes with hidden feet go into a white mountain. Then
Bhima, whose courage was like a god's, pierced the son of the suta again in the
great battle with six arrows and then with eight, in his vital parts. Then
mighty Bhimasena in his anger quickly cut Karna's bow and all his gear. He
quickly killed his four horses and his charioteer with arrows, and pierced Karna
in the chest with iron arrows bright as the rays of the sun. They went through
Karna and into the ground, sir, as the rays of the sun go through a cloud, king.
In great distress, with his bow cut and hurt by the arrows, Karna, who thought
so much of his manhood, went off to another chariot."

Dhritarashtra said, "All my sons' hope of victory rested on him. When he saw him
turn away in battle, what did Duryodhana say? And what did Karna do after that
in the battle, my friend?"

Sanjaya said, "Karna saw Bhimasena in the battle like a blazing fire. He took
his stand in another chariot, properly fitted out, and went at the Pandava
again, like the sea whipped up by the wind. When they saw Adhiratha's son angry,
lord of the people, your sons thought Bhimasena was already poured into the
mouth of Vaivasvata. Radheya twanged his bow and slapped his palms with a
dreadful noise, and went at Bhimasena's chariot. Then again, king, a great and
very dreadful clash began between the son of the suta and Bhima, lord of the
people. The strong-armed men were furious, each wanting to kill the other, and
they looked at each other as if they would burn each other with their eyes.
Their eyes were red with anger. The two great warriors, tamers of their enemies,
breathing hard, met in battle and tore at each other. They fought each other
like two furious tigers, like two swift hawks, like two raging sharabhas.

"Then Bhima, the tamer of his enemies, remembered the sufferings of the dice
game and of the forest, and the sorrow he had met in Virata's city. He
remembered how your sons took away the kingdoms rich in jewels, and the torments
that you and your son always gave them. He remembered that you wanted to burn
Kunti and her sons, who had done no wrong, and the torment of Krishna in the
middle of the hall by those evil men. 'Look for another husband. You have no
husbands. The sons of Pritha have fallen into hell, all of them like sesame
seeds without kernels.' That was what the Kurus said then in front of you,
Kaurava, when your sons wanted to use Krishna as a slave. He remembered the
harsh words Karna spoke in the hall in your presence as they went into exile,
dressed in black deerskins. He remembered how your son, treating the sons of
Pritha as straw, strutted about in his anger and lost his head, himself on level
ground while they were in trouble. Thinking of those sorrows, from his childhood
on, Vrikodara, the killer of his enemies, whose soul was set on dharma, grew
tired of his life.

"Then the tiger of the Bharatas drew his great bow, backed with gold and hard to
face, and went at Karna careless of his life. With nets made of bright arrows,
washed on stone, Bhima hid the light of the sun from Karna's chariot. Then
Adhiratha's son laughed, and quickly shooting sharp feathered arrows, he blew
away Bhimasena's nets of arrows. The great warrior, strong-armed and mighty,
Adhiratha's son, pierced Bhima with nine sharp arrows of great speed. Held back
by the arrows like an elephant by goads, Vrikodara ran at the son of the suta
without losing his head. As the bull of the Pandavas came at him fast and wild,
Karna went out to fight him, like a rutting elephant going to meet a rutting
elephant.

"Then Karna blew his conch, with the sound of a hundred drums, and the army
seethed with joy like the sea whipped up. Seeing the army whipped up, with its
chariots, elephants, horses and foot soldiers, Bhima came up to Karna and
covered him with arrows. Karna, covering the Pandava with arrows in the battle,
brought his fine swan-coloured horses so close that they mingled with Bhima's
horses, which were the colour of antelopes. When your sons' army saw the
antelope-coloured horses, fast as the wind, mixed with the white ones, it cried
out 'Alas!' Those horses, fast as the wind, mingled together, white and dark,
shone like clouds in the sky, great king. When they saw Karna and Vrikodara so
furious, their eyes copper-red with anger, the great warriors of your army
trembled in fear.

"The field where they fought was as dreadful as the kingdom of Yama, best of the
Bharatas, as hard to look at as the city of the king of the dead. The great
warriors watched it like a wonderful show, and could not see which of the two
would clearly win, as each held the other back. They watched the clash of those
two with their great weapons at close quarters, king, and all through your bad
counsel and your son's, lord of the people. The two killers of their enemies
covered each other with sharp arrows, and filled the sky with nets of arrows
from their rains of arrows. The two great warriors, each wanting to kill the
other with sharp arrows, were a finer sight than two rainclouds. Loosing arrows
worked with gold, the two tamers of their enemies made the sky shine, my lord,
as if with fiery meteors. The arrows loosed by them, feathered with heron and
peacock feathers, shone like lines of wild cranes in the autumn sky.

"When Krishna and Dhananjaya saw Bhima, the tamer of his enemies, locked in
battle with the son of the suta, they thought it too heavy a load for Bhima.
There, struck hard by the arrows loosed by Adhiratha's son and Bhima, horses,
men and elephants fell beyond the reach of their arrows. With some falling and
others fallen and dead in their many kinds, great king, there was a great
slaughter of your sons' men. In a moment, bull of the Bharatas, the ground was
covered with the lifeless bodies of men, horses and elephants."

Dhritarashtra said, "I think Bhimasena's courage was a great wonder, that he
fought Karna in battle, so quick in his courage. Karna could hold off even the
gods in battle, with all their weapons, together with the yakshas, the asuras
and men. How did he not get the better of the Pandava in battle, shining in his
glory as he was? Tell me that, Sanjaya. How did the battle go on between the two
of them, in that game of dice with lives for the stake? Here, I think, winning
and losing hang on it. Having Karna in battle, Suta, my son Suyodhana dares to
hope he will beat the sons of Pritha, with Govinda and the Satvatas. When I hear
that Karna has been beaten again and again in battle by Bhimasena of the
terrible deeds, I feel as if I were fainting.

"I think the Kauravas are destroyed by my son's bad policy. Karna will not beat
the sons of Pritha, great bowmen as they are, Sanjaya. In all the battles Karna
has fought with the sons of Pandu, the Pandavas have beaten Karna everywhere on
the field. The Pandavas cannot be beaten, my friend, even by the gods with
Vasava. But my foolish son Duryodhana does not understand it. He took Partha's
wealth, as if it were the wealth of the lord of wealth, and like a fool who
wants honey, he does not see the fall. A master of trickery, he took the great
men's kingdom by trickery, and thinking they are beaten, he scorns the Pandavas.
And I, too, overcome by love for my son and without mastery of myself, wronged
the great sons of Pandu, who stood firm in dharma. Yudhishthira, the son of
Pritha, who looks far ahead, always wanted peace, and my sons turned him away,
thinking him weak.

"Strong-armed Bhima kept all those many sorrows and all those wrongs in his
heart when he fought the son of the suta. So tell me, Sanjaya, how Karna and
Bhima, the best of fighters, fought in battle, each wanting to kill the other."

Sanjaya said, "Hear, king, how the battle went between Karna and Bhima, each
wanting to kill the other, like two elephants in a forest.

"King, Vaikartana, angry, attacked Bhima, angry and courageous, the tamer of his
enemies, and pierced him with thirty arrows. With swift arrows, bright- pointed
and worked with gold, Vaikartana struck Bhima, best of the Bharatas. As he shot,
Bhima cut his bow with three sharp arrows, and with a broad arrow brought his
driver down from the chariot to the ground. Vaikartana Vrisha, wanting
Bhimasena's death, took hold of a spear with a shaft worked with gold and beryl.
The mighty man seized the great spear, like another spear of Death, raised it,
aimed it and hurled it at Bhimasena as if to end his life. When Radheya had
loosed the spear, as Puramdara looses the thunderbolt, the mighty son of the
suta roared a great roar. Your sons heard that roar and were glad.

"But Bhima cut the spear in the sky with seven swift arrows, as it came from
Karna's arm with the brightness of the sun and fire. The spear was like a snake
that has shed its skin. When he had cut it, Bhima seemed to be seeking the life
of the son of the suta, sir. In his fury he loosed nine arrows at him in the
battle. They were feathered with peacock feathers, golden-shafted and washed on
stone, like the staff of Yama. Karna took another bow, backed with gold and hard
to face, and in his great splendour drew it and loosed nine arrows. The son of
Pandu cut them with nine smooth-jointed arrows. When Bhima had cut the nine
great arrows loosed by Vasushena, great king, he roared like a lion.

"The two strong men roared like two bulls among the cows, and bellowed at each
other like two tigers. Each wanted to strike the other, each looked for an
opening in the other, and they eyed each other like two great bulls in a cow
pen. Like two great elephants meeting with the points of their tusks, they
struck each other with arrows loosed from bows drawn full and wide. They
scorched each other with rains of arrows, great king, and glared at each other
with eyes wide with anger. Laughing at each other, threatening each other again
and again and blowing their conches, they fought each other.

"Then Bhima cut his bow again at the grip, sir, and sent those conch-coloured
horses to the house of Yama with his arrows. When King Duryodhana saw Karna in
such trouble, he shook as if with anger and gave an order to Durjaya. 'Go,
Durjaya, to Radheya, before the Pandava swallows him. Kill that beardless one
quickly, and give Karna strength.' Your son said, 'So be it,' and ran at
Bhimasena as he fought, scattering arrows. He struck Bhima with nine arrows, his
horses with eight, his driver with six and his banner with three, and Bhima
again with seven. Then Bhimasena, in anger, sent Durjaya to the house of Yama
with swift arrows, his vital parts split, with his horses and his driver. Your
son lay on the ground in his finery, crushed, writhing like a snake, and Karna,
weeping in his grief, walked round him with his right side towards him.

"Bhima, smiling, made his bitter enemy chariotless and heaped him with flights
of arrows, like a shataghni studded with spikes. Even so Karna, the great
warrior, the tormentor of his enemies, torn by the arrows, did not leave Bhima
in the battle, furious as he was.

"Beaten again by Bhima and left without a chariot, Karna took his stand in
another chariot and at once pierced the Pandava. Like two great elephants
meeting with the points of their tusks, they struck each other with arrows
loosed from bows drawn full and wide. Then Karna struck Bhima hard with flights
of arrows, roared a mighty roar, and pierced him again in the chest. Bhima
pierced him back with ten straight arrows, and again with twenty smooth-jointed
arrows. Karna pierced Bhima between the breasts with nine arrows, king, and
pierced his banner with one sharp arrow. Then the son of Pritha pierced him back
with sixty-three arrows, as men drive a great elephant with goads or a horse
with whips.

"Pierced deep by the famous Pandava, great king, the hero licked the corners of
his mouth, his eyes red with anger. Then, great king, he loosed an arrow at
Bhimasena that could split any body, as Indra loosed the thunderbolt at Bala.
Loosed from the bow of the son of the suta, that bright-feathered arrow went
through the son of Pritha in the battle and on into the ground, splitting it.

"Without a second thought, Bhima hurled at the son of the suta a heavy mace all
of iron, four cubits long, six-edged, with golden bands. With that mace the
Bharata in his anger killed the fine horses of Adhiratha's son, good at the
pull, as Indra killed the asuras with the thunderbolt. Then strong-armed Bhima
cut down Adhiratha's son's banner with two razor arrows, bull of the Bharatas,
and killed his driver. Karna left the chariot, its horses and driver killed and
its banner fallen, and stood there twanging his bow, Bharata, sick at heart.
There we saw a wonder in the courage of Radheya: the best of chariot fighters,
without a chariot, held back his enemy.

"When he saw Adhiratha's son, the finest of chariot fighters, without a chariot
in the battle, king, Duryodhana said to Durmukha, 'Durmukha, here is Radheya,
left without a chariot by Bhima. Bring that great warrior, that best of men, a
chariot.' Hearing Duryodhana, Bharata, Durmukha hurried to Karna and held Bhima
back with arrows. When the son of the Wind saw Durmukha in the battle, coming to
help the son of the suta, he was glad, and licked the corners of his mouth. Then
the Pandava held Karna back with arrows, great king, and quickly sent his
chariot at Durmukha. Then and there, great king, Bhima sent Durmukha to the
house of Yama with nine well-feathered, smooth-jointed arrows.

"When Durmukha was killed, Adhiratha's son took his stand in that same chariot,
king, and shone like the blazing sun. When he saw Durmukha lying there, his
vital parts split and drenched in blood, Karna's eyes filled with tears, and for
a moment he did not go forward. He walked round the dead man with his right side
towards him, and the hero breathed long, hot breaths and did not know what to
do.

"In that gap, king, Bhimasena sent fourteen iron arrows with vultures' feathers
at the son of the suta. Golden-feathered and very strong, worked with gold,
great king, they lit up the ten directions. They split his armour and drank the
son of the suta's blood, those eaters of blood, like angry snakes driven by
Time, lord of men. As they ran on into the earth, the arrows shone, half buried,
like great angry snakes going into their holes. Without a second thought Radheya
pierced him back with fourteen very fierce iron arrows adorned with gold. The
dreadful feathered arrows split Bhimasena's left arm and went into the earth, as
birds go into the Krauncha mountain. As they went into the earth the iron arrows
shone like the rays of the sun blazing as it sets. Split in the battle by iron
arrows that pierced the vital parts, Bhima poured out blood, as a mountain pours
water.

"Straining hard, Bhima pierced the son of the suta with three arrows as swift as
Suparna, and his charioteer with seven. Then famous Karna, shaken, great king,
and hurt by Bhima's strength, left the battle and fled with his swift horses.
And Bhimasena, the great warrior, stood in the battle twanging his bow worked
with gold, blazing like a fire."

**Divergences.**

* **Karna's taunt.** \*826, in which Karna calls after Bhima that it is unworthy of Kunti's son to turn his back, is rejected. The CE has him only call Bhima back, smiling (7.106.19).
* **Karna's teacher.** \*825 (Karna won the Brahma weapon from Rama Bhargava) and \*839–\*841 (Karna, pupil of Bhargava and his equal in weapons, beaten by Bhima "in play") are rejected here.
* **Duhshasana and Draupadi's hair.** \*843, which adds Duhshasana dragging Draupadi by the hair and Karna's harsh words, is rejected at this point. In the CE, "Look for another husband" is what "the Kurus said" in Dhritarashtra's presence (7.107.12–13), and Karna's harsh words are those he spoke at the exile (7.107.14).
* **Another round.** \*846, in which Bhima kills Karna's white horses and driver and breaks his chariot before 7.107.36, is rejected.
* **Karna's shame.** \*835, in which Karna "puts aside his shame" and runs from fear of Bhima, is rejected. The CE says only that he went to another chariot (7.106.54).
* Other rejected lines not narrated: \*823–\*824, \*827–\*834, \*836–\*838, \*842, \*844–\*845, \*847–\*858.
