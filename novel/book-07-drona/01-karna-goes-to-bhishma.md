summary: Sanjaya comes back from the camp to Hastinapura at night, and Dhritarashtra asks what the Kurus did when Bhishma fell. Sanjaya tells how both sides made Bhishma a bed of arrows and then went back to fight, and how the Kaurava army was like a flock without a herdsman until the kings cried out for Karna. Karna had refused to fight while Bhishma lived. Now he comes forward, arms himself and drives out. He goes first to Bhishma on his bed of arrows and asks for his blessing. Bhishma blesses him, recalls his conquests for Duryodhana, and tells him to guard the Kaurava army as Duryodhana would. Karna bows at his feet and rides into battle, and the Kurus cheer.

# Karna Goes to Bhishma

"Bhishma had no equal in courage, strength, might and daring," said Janamejaya,
"and Shikhandi of Panchala killed him. When King Dhritarashtra heard that his
father was dead, his mind was distraught with grief. What did he do then, great
seer? For his son wanted the kingdom. He meant to beat the Pandavas, great
archers as they were, with Bhishma, Drona and the other chariot fighters. Now
Bhishma, the banner of all bowmen, was dead. Tell me what the Kaurava did, best
of brahmins."

"When Dhritarashtra, lord of the people, heard that his father had been killed,"
said Vaishampayana, "the Kaurava found no peace. He was lost in thought and
grief. As the king brooded on his sorrow day and night, the son of Gavalgana,
pure of heart, came to him again. Sanjaya came from the camp to the city of the
elephant at night, and Dhritarashtra, Ambika's son, questioned him, great king.
He had heard of Bhishma's death, and he was deeply unhappy. He longed for his
sons to win, and he wept like a man in pain.

"'When they had brought down great Bhishma, whose courage was terrible, what did
the Kurus do next, my son, driven on by Time?' said Dhritarashtra. 'When that
hero, so hard to face and so mighty, had been killed, what did the Kurus do,
sunk in a sea of grief? That huge, surging army of ours would strike sharp fear
into the great Pandavas, and even into the three worlds, Sanjaya. Tell me what
the kings did when Devavrata, the bull of the Kurus, had been killed.'

"'Listen with all your mind, king, to what I tell you,' said Sanjaya. 'Hear what
your sons did when Devavrata was killed in battle. When Bhishma, whose courage
was true, had been killed, king, your men and the Pandavas each brooded apart.
They were amazed and moved when they thought of the kshatriya's duty. They
cursed their own duty, and they bowed to the great man. For Bhishma, whose power
had no measure, they made a bed with a pillow of smooth-jointed arrows, tiger
among men. They set a guard around Bhishma, spoke with one another, took leave
of Ganga's son and walked round him with their right sides towards him. Then,
their eyes red with anger, looking at one another, the kshatriyas went out to
fight again, driven on by Time. With the blare of trumpets and the loud boom of
drums, your ranks and the enemy's marched out.

"'When the day had turned, king of kings, and Jahnavi's son had fallen, the best
of the Bharatas were in the grip of rage, their minds struck down by Time. They
ignored the sound advice of great Ganga's son and marched out, carrying weapons
of every kind. Through the folly of you and your son, and through the killing of
Shantanu's son, the Kauravas and all the kings with them were in the power of
death. Without Devavrata they were deeply troubled, like goats and sheep without
a herdsman in a forest full of beasts of prey.

"'When the best of the Bharatas had fallen, the Kuru army was like the sky
without its stars, like the air without wind, like the earth when its crops have
failed, like speech without grammar. It was like the demons' army long ago when
Bali had been captured, like a lovely woman widowed, like a river run dry. It
was like a doe cut off by wolves in the forest when the leader of her herd has
been killed, like a great mountain cave that anyone can enter now that its lion
is dead. That is what the Bharata army was like, best of the Bharatas, when
Jahnavi's son had fallen. It was like a broken ship on the great ocean, battered
by winds from every side. It was hard pressed by the mighty Pandava heroes, who
had found their mark. The army was in turmoil, its horses, chariots and
elephants in disorder. Most of its men were in despair, and it was a pitiful
thing to see. The kings and the soldiers of every kind were terrified, and
without Devavrata they were like men sinking into the underworld.

"'Then the Kurus remembered Karna, for Karna was equal to Devavrata. Their
thoughts went to him, the best of all who bear arms, shining like a welcome
guest, as the thoughts of a man in trouble go to a kinsman. The kings there
cried out, "Karna! Karna!", Bharata. "Radheya, the charioteer's son, who is
ready to give up his life, is our friend." For the famous Karna had not fought
for ten days. They called on Karna, with his ministers and kinsmen, to come at
once.

"'For in front of all the kshatriyas, when the chariot fighters, strong and
brave, were being counted, Bhishma had counted strong-armed Karna as half a
chariot fighter, though he was worth twice as much. Yet he was the first in any
count of chariot fighters and great chariot fighters, esteemed by the brave. He
would dare to fight even the lord of the dead, the lord of wealth, the lord of
the waters and the king of the gods. And in that very anger, king, he had said
to Ganga's son, "As long as you live, Kaurava, I will not fight at all. If you
kill the sons of Pandu in the great battle, I will take Duryodhana's leave and
go to the forest, Kaurava. Or if the Pandavas kill you, Bhishma, and you go to
heaven, then with a single chariot I will kill all those you think are chariot
fighters." So the famous Karna had spoken, great king, and with your son's
consent he did not fight for ten days.

"'Bhishma, brave in battle and of courage beyond count, had killed the Pandava's
warriors in battle, lord of the earth. Now that the hero, true to his word and
mighty, had been killed, your sons remembered Karna, as men who want to cross a
river think of a boat. Your men and your sons, with all the kings, cried, "Oh,
Karna!" and said, "Now is the time." Our thoughts went to Karna, whose manhood
was hard to withstand and whose weapons Rama Jamadagnya had allowed him, as
men's thoughts go to a kinsman in times of danger. For he could save us in
battle from great fear, king, as Govinda always saves the gods from great fear.'

"Sanjaya praised Karna, the best of fighters, again and again. Dhritarashtra
sighed like a snake and said:

"'When your thoughts went to Karna, the son of Vikartana, did you see Radheya,
the charioteer's son, ready to give up his life? Did he, whose courage was true,
not fail in battle the frightened, suffering men who looked to him to save them?
Did that best of bowmen fill the breach that was left in the Kauravas' ranks
when Bhishma was killed? Did he fill that gap, and put fear into the enemy, and
make my sons' hope of victory bear fruit?'

"'Adhiratha's son knew that Bhishma had been killed,' said Sanjaya. 'The army of
the Kurus was like a ship broken up in a bottomless sea. As a brother would, the
charioteer's son came to carry your son's army safely across its disaster.
Karna, the tamer of enemies, first of bowmen, heard that the great chariot
fighter, Shantanu's son, the imperishable lord of men, had been brought down. He
came at once. Bhishma, the best of chariot fighters, had been killed by the
enemy, and the Kurus were sinking like a ship in the ocean. So he came quickly,
as a father comes to his sons, to carry your son's army across.

"'"Bhishma had firmness, wisdom, courage and strength," said Karna. "He had
self-control and truth, and all the virtues of a hero. He had the divine
weapons, and humility and modesty, and kind words, and none of these ever left
him. He killed the haters of brahmins and never forgot a kindness. These virtues
were as lasting in him as the mark on the moon. If that killer of enemy heroes
has been laid to rest, then I think every warrior is as good as dead. Nothing in
this world is ever certain, since all deeds pass away. Now that the man of great
vows is dead, who can be sure the sun will rise? He had the power of the Vasus,
he was born of the Vasus' strength, and now the lord of the earth has gone back
to the Vasus. So mourn for your wealth, your sons and the earth, for the Kurus
and for this army."

"'When the great, the powerful Shantanu's son, the giver of boons, the best in
the world, had been brought down, and the Bharatas were beaten, Karna sighed
deeply in distress, and his tears flowed. When they heard Radheya's words, king,
your sons and your soldiers cried out to one another in pain, and wept aloud.

"'As the great battle began again and the kings plunged into the armies, the
bull of all the great chariot fighters spoke words that gave heart to the bulls
among chariot fighters.

"'"I look at this passing world, forever running on, and I see that nothing
stands firm," said Karna. "How could the bull of the Kurus, bright as a
mountain, have been brought down in battle while all of you stood here? Now that
the great chariot fighter, Shantanu's son, has been brought down like the sun
fallen to the ground, the kings cannot withstand Dhananjaya, any more than trees
can withstand the wind that shakes the mountains. Today this Kuru army has lost
its leader. It is in pain, its spirit broken by the enemy, and it has no
protector. I must guard it in battle, as that great man guarded it. I take this
weight on myself. I see that the world does not last, and that the one who loved
battle has been brought down in battle. So how could I be afraid in battle?

"'"As I range over the field, I will send those bulls of the Kurus to Yama's
house with my straight arrows. I will live on, having spread the highest fame in
the world, or else I will lie on the battlefield, killed by the enemy.
Yudhishthira has firmness, wisdom and knows the truth of dharma. Wolf-belly has
the strength of a hundred elephants. Arjuna is the son of the best of the gods.
So that army cannot be beaten easily, even by the immortals. Where the twins
are, like Yama in strength, and Satyaki, and Devaki's son, no coward goes into
that army and comes back, any more than he comes back out of the jaws of death.
Heat that has risen high is met only with heat, and strength with strength, by
men of spirit. My mind is made up to hold back the enemy and to guard our side,
and it is as firm as a mountain."

"'Then he said to his charioteer, "Knowing their power as I do, I will go and
beat them today, charioteer. I cannot bear this betrayal of a friend. When an
army is broken, the one who helps it is a friend. I will do this, which is the
noble work of good men. I will give up my life and follow Bhishma. I will kill
all the hosts of the enemy in battle, or be killed by them and go to the world
of heroes. The women and children are weeping and wailing, and the manhood of
Dhritarashtra's son has been beaten down. I know that this is my work,
charioteer, and so I will beat the enemies of Dhritarashtra's son. I will guard
the Kurus and seek to kill the sons of Pandu. Giving up my life in this terrible
battle, I will kill all the enemy hosts, and I will give the kingdom to
Dhritarashtra's son.

"'"Fasten on my armour, the bright one of gold that shines with gems and jewels,
and my helmet that shines like the sun. Bring my bow and my arrows like
poisonous snakes. Let them fix sixteen quivers to the chariot and bring divine
bows, and swords, spears, heavy maces and my conch patterned with gold. Wipe
with soft cloths and bring this golden girth for the elephant, the one that
brings victory, and my victorious banner, blue as a water lily, with a bright
garland fastened on it and a net. Bring me fine horses the colour of white
clouds, sleek, washed with water made pure by spells, fitted with harness of
burnished gold. Bring them quickly, charioteer's son, fast ones. Bring round at
once the finest chariot, covered in a net of gold, bright with jewels that shine
like the moon and the sun, stocked with everything for battle and yoked to good
horses. Bring bright bows with strong strings, the best bowstrings, and great
quivers full of arrows, and armour for my body. Bring quickly all that is needed
for setting out: girls, and a full golden vessel. Hang a garland on me, and let
them beat the drums at once for victory.

"'"Drive on quickly, charioteer, to where the diademed one is, and Wolf-belly,
the son of Dharma and the twins. Either I will meet them in battle and kill
them, or I will be killed by my enemies and go to Bhishma. Where King
Yudhishthira stands, firm in the truth, with Bhimasena and Arjuna, Vasudeva,
Satyaki and the Srinjayas, I think that army cannot be beaten by kings. Even if
Death, who takes all, were always there to guard the diademed one in battle, I
would still meet him in battle and kill him, or go to Yama by Bhishma's road. I
tell you truly, I will go among those heroes. Those who betray their friends,
whose loyalty is weak, whose hearts are evil, are no allies of mine."

"'He mounted his fine, strong chariot, bringing success, with its pole and its
golden ornaments and its flags, yoked to fine horses as swift as the wind, and
drove out to win. Honoured by the Kurus, that great man, that bull among chariot
fighters, drawn by white horses, drove with his fierce bow to the battlefield,
where the bull of the Bharatas had met his end. Karna, of power beyond measure,
rode in his great chariot, with its screen and its banner, set with gold,
pearls, gems and diamonds and yoked to good horses, and it rumbled like a cloud.
He was like the sun. Shining like fire in his chariot, which shone like fire,
the bowman, Adhiratha's son, the great chariot fighter, stood splendid, like the
king of the gods in his own flying chariot.

"'Karna saw the great man lying on his bed of arrows. His power had no measure,
and he looked like the ocean dried up by a great storm. The great archer had
been brought down by Savyasachin's divine weapons. Your sons' hope of victory,
their shelter and their armour, was broken. He had been an island for those who
could find no shore, a ford in fathomless water for those who wanted to cross.
Now he was flooded by a stream of arrows, as by the current of the Yamuna. He
was like mighty Mount Mainaka brought down to the earth, like the sun fallen
from the sky to the ground. The felling of Bhishma in battle had stunned the
whole army, as unthinkable as Shatakratu's defeat by Vritra long ago. He was the
hump of the bull of all the armies, the mark of all bowmen. Your father, the man
of great vows, was covered with Dhananjaya's arrows.

"'When Adhiratha's son saw the hero lying on his hero's bed, that bull among
men, the one not in the middle of the Bharatas but at their head, he got down
from his chariot in distress. He bowed, joined his hands in reverence, and spoke
to him, his voice broken by tears.

"'"I am Karna. Bless you, Bharata. Speak to me today with holy and kind words,
and look at me with a kind eye. Surely no one here gets the fruit of good deeds,
since you lie on the ground, old and devoted to dharma. In filling the treasury,
in counsel, in battle arrays and in fighting, I see no other protector of the
Kurus, best of the Kurus, with the pure mind that could carry the Kurus across
their danger. You leave the warriors without a boat, and you will go to the
world of the ancestors.

"'"From today, best of the Bharatas, the angry Pandavas will destroy the Kurus,
as tigers destroy deer. Today the Kurus, who know Savyasachin's courage, will
fear the sound of the Gandiva as the demons fear the one who holds the
thunderbolt. Today the sound of arrows loosed from the Gandiva, like
thunderbolts, will terrify the Kurus and the other kings in battle. As a blazing
fire with great flames burns up the trees, hero, so the diademed one's arrows
will burn up Dhritarashtra's sons. Wherever wind and fire go together in the
forest, the two lords burn whatever they will. Partha is like a blazing fire,
without a doubt, and Krishna is like the wind, tiger among men. When they hear
the Panchajanya roar and the Gandiva twang, all the armies will be afraid,
Bharata. When the chariot with the monkey banner, the tamer of enemies, comes
on, the kings will not bear its sound, except for you, hero.

"'"What king but you is fit to fight Arjuna in battle? Wise men speak of his
divine deeds, and of his superhuman battle with the wise Three-eyed God, and of
the boon he won from him, which men who have not mastered themselves cannot win.
Today, with your guidance, I cannot bear that Pandava, who loves to fight, who
is as terrible as a poisonous snake that kills with a look. I will go to face
him, for death or for victory."

"'As he spoke on, the old grandsire of the Kurus heard him and was glad. He
spoke words that suited the place and the time.

"'"Be the support of your friends," he said. "Be to them what the ocean is to
rivers, and the sun to the lights of the sky. Be what the good are to truth,
fertile soil to seeds, and the rain cloud to living things. Let your kinsmen
live by you, as the gods live by the thousand-eyed Indra. By the strength of
your own arm, Karna, wanting to please Dhritarashtra's son, you went to Rajapura
and killed the Kambojas. At Girivraja you beat the kings led by Nagnajit, and
the Ambashthas, the Videhas and the Gandharas. Long ago, Karna, you brought the
Kiratas, harsh in battle, who live in the strongholds of the Himalaya, under
Duryodhana's rule. In battle after battle, wanting what was good for Duryodhana,
you beat many heroes, Karna, with your great might.

"'"As Duryodhana is, my son, with his kin, his family and his friends, so be the
refuge of all the Kauravas. I speak to you with blessing. Go and fight the
enemy. Lead the Kurus in battle, and bring victory to Duryodhana. You are like a
grandson to us, just as Duryodhana is. By dharma we all belong to you, as we
belong to him. The wise say that among good people a bond with the good is
better than a bond of birth. Be true to your word, and be sure in your mind that
this army is yours. Guard the army of the Kurus as Duryodhana does."

"'When he had heard these words, Vaikartana Karna bowed at his feet and went
quickly to the battlefield. He looked at the great, matchless ranks of men,
their chests broad and their weapons ready, and gave the army new strength. When
the Kurus saw the great archer Karna ready for battle, they honoured him with
shouts and the slapping of arms, with lions' roars and the sound of every kind
of bow.'"

**Divergences.**

* **The frame.** Sanjaya comes again from the camp to Hastinapura at night (7.1.6–7). The book does not yet say why; the summary that follows (7.5–7.7) shows that Drona has already fallen when he comes. See the next chapter.
* **A second visit.** Karna has already been to Bhishma at the end of Book 6 (6.117), where Bhishma tells him he is Kunti's son and gives him leave to fight. Here the CE tells the visit again, in other words: Karna asks for Bhishma's blessing, and Bhishma blesses him and tells him to guard the army. The novel keeps both, as the text does. The lines that repeat 6.117 word for word here (the empty place, the guards sent away, the one-armed embrace) are in the CE apparatus as Appendix passages (@1, @2), and are not narrated.
* **"Half a chariot fighter."** Sanjaya recalls that Bhishma counted Karna as half a chariot fighter (7.1.34; 5.165.5–6; ledger L-184), and Karna's vow not to fight while Bhishma lived (7.1.36–39).
* **The pillow.** 7.1.16 says both sides made Bhishma's bed "with a pillow of smooth-jointed arrows", a short retelling of 6.115.33–46, where Arjuna gives the pillow.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*1–\*38 (with @1–@2). \*13 and \*15–\*16 repeat the cry of "Karna!"; \*31 names Jarasandha; \*35 repeats Bhishma's words from 6.117.
