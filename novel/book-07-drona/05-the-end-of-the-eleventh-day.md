# The End of the Eleventh Day

"I have heard many wonderful single fights from you, Sanjaya," said
Dhritarashtra, "and I envy those who have eyes. People in the worlds will tell
of this battle of the Kurus and the Pandavas as a marvel, like the battle of the
gods and the demons. I can never have enough of hearing of this great battle. So
tell me of the fight between Artayani and Subhadra's son."

"When Shalya saw his charioteer brought down," said Sanjaya, "he raised a mace
made all of iron, and in his anger he leapt down from his fine chariot with a
shout. He was like the fire of Time blazing, like Death with his staff in his
hand. Bhima took up his great mace and rushed at him. Subhadra's son also took
up a great mace like a thunderbolt and called to Shalya, 'Come on! Come on!' But
Bhima held him back with an effort. When mighty Bhimasena had held Subhadra's
son back, he came up to Shalya in the battle and stood as firm as a mountain.
The king of the Madras, too, seeing mighty Bhima, went straight at him, like a
tiger at an elephant.

"Then there were blasts of trumpets and thousands of conches, lions' roars and
the great boom of drums. Hundreds of Pandavas and Kurus watched, thinking alike,
and the cry went up, 'Well done! Well done!' For no one among all the kings but
the lord of the Madras would dare to bear Bhimasena's force in battle, Bharata.
And who else in the world but Wolf-belly would dare to bear the force of the
great Madra king's mace in battle?

"Bound with bands of gold, the great mace was a joy to see, and it blazed as
Bhima swung it. As Shalya moved through the passes and the circles, each in
turn, his mace shone like a great flash of lightning. Shalya and Wolf-belly
roared like two bulls as they circled, their maces bent like horns. In the
circling, the wheeling passes and the play of the maces, there was nothing to
choose between those two lions among men.

"Struck by Bhimasena, Shalya's great mace, blazing with fire and terrible, broke
up in a shower of sparks. In the same way Bhimasena's mace, struck by his enemy,
looked like a tree covered with fireflies on an evening in the rains. The mace
the Madra king hurled in the battle, Bharata, lit up the sky and threw out
showers of fire. So did the mace Bhimasena sent at his enemy, and it scorched
the army like a great falling meteor. The two fine maces met and hissed like
snake maidens, and threw out fire. Like two great tigers with their claws, like
two great elephants with their tusks, the two men went at each other with their
maces. In a moment, struck by the heads of the maces, the two great men were
covered in blood, and they looked like two flowering kimshuka trees. The crash
of the blows of the maces of those two lions among men was heard in every
direction, like the crash of Shakra's thunderbolt.

"Struck by the Madra king's mace on the left and the right, Bhima did not waver,
like a mountain being split. In the same way the mighty lord of the Madras,
battered by the force of Bhima's mace, stood firm, like a mountain struck by
thunderbolts. The two rushed at each other with great speed, their great maces
raised high. Then they moved into the inner passes and circled again. Then they
leapt eight paces and fell on each other like two elephants, and struck each
other at once with their iron clubs. Struck hard by each other's force and by
the maces, the two heroes fell to the ground at the same moment, like two
banners of Indra.

"Then the great chariot fighter Kritavarma rushed at once to Shalya, who lay
reeling and gasping again and again. He saw him crushed by the mace, great king,
writhing like a snake and overcome by a faint. The great chariot fighter
Kritavarma lifted the lord of the Madras into his chariot with his mace and
carried him quickly away from the battle. Strong-armed Bhima, reeling like a
drunken man, was up again in the blink of an eye, and stood there, mace in hand.

"When your sons saw the lord of the Madras turned away, sir, they trembled, with
their elephants, chariots, foot soldiers and horses. Pressed hard by the
victorious Pandavas, your men fled in fear in every direction, like clouds
driven by the wind. When they had beaten the sons of Dhritarashtra, the Pandava
great chariot fighters shone in the battle, king, blazing with glory. They
roared like lions again and again, blew their conches in joy and beat their
drums, tabors and kettledrums.

"When he saw your great army scattered, mighty Vrishasena held off the Pandavas
in the battle by himself, with the magic of his weapons. The arrows Vrishasena
loosed flew in every direction, sir, piercing men, horses, chariots and
elephants. His great blazing arrows poured out by the thousand, strong-armed
one, like the rays of the sun in the hot season. Struck by him, great king,
chariot fighters and horsemen fell to the ground in a moment, like trees blown
down by the wind. He brought down herds of horses, herds of chariots and herds
of elephants on every side in the battle, king, by the hundred and the thousand.

"When they saw him ranging so fearlessly over the field, all the kings together
surrounded him on every side. Nakula's son Shatanika rode at Vrishasena and
pierced him with ten iron arrows that split the vital parts. Karna's son cut his
bow and brought down his banner. Then the sons of Draupadi, wanting to save
their brother, rode at him. With swarms of arrows they soon hid Karna's son from
sight. Chariot fighters led by Drona's son rushed at them with a shout, covering
the great chariot fighters, the sons of Draupadi, with every kind of arrow,
great king, as clouds cover mountains. The Pandavas, fearing for their sons, met
them quickly, the Panchalas, the Kekayas, the Matsyas and the Srinjayas with
their weapons raised.

"That battle between your men and the sons of Pandu was fearful, wild and
hair-raising, like the battle of the gods and the danavas. So the Kurus and the
Pandavas fought in the greatest fury, glaring at each other, each having wronged
the other. Their bodies, as they looked at each other in anger, men of boundless
power, were like those of the great birds and the snakes wanting to fight in the
sky. With Bhima, Karna, Kripa, Drona, Drona's son, Parshata and Satyaki, the
field shone as if the suns of the end of time had risen. Then the battle was
wild, as the mighty killed each other, like the gods and the mighty danavas.

"Then Yudhishthira's army, roaring like a stirred-up ocean, cut down your army,
and its great chariot fighters fled. When Drona saw that army broken and hard
pressed by the enemy, he said, 'Enough of running, heroes!' Then the one with
red horses, in a fury like an elephant with four tusks, drove into the Pandava
army and rushed at Yudhishthira. Yudhishthira pierced him with sharp arrows
fledged with heron feathers. Drona cut his bow and rushed at him fast.

"Then the young prince who guarded his wheel, who brought glory to the
Panchalas, held Drona back as he came, as the shore holds back the lord of
rivers. When they saw Drona, the bull of the twice-born, held back by the
prince, there were lions' roars and the cry of 'Well done! Well done!' Then the
prince, in his anger, pierced Drona in the chest with an arrow in the great
battle, and roared like a lion again and again. Mighty Drona held the prince off
in the battle with many thousands of arrows, skilled of hand and never tiring.
Then the best of the twice-born crushed the brave prince who guarded the wheel,
a man of noble vows who had worked hard to learn the meaning of weapons.

"Reaching the middle of the army, ranging in every direction, Bharadvaja's son,
the bull among chariot fighters, became the guardian of your army. He pierced
Shikhandi with twelve arrows and Uttamaujas with twenty, Nakula with five and
Sahadeva with seven, Yudhishthira with twelve and each of Draupadi's sons with
three, Satyaki with five and the Matsya with ten. He threw the warriors into
turmoil in the battle, rushing at the leaders one by one, and pressed on,
wanting to reach Kunti's son Yudhishthira.

"Then Yugandhara held back Bharadvaja's son, the great chariot fighter, in his
fury, king, like the ocean stirred up by the wind. Drona pierced Yudhishthira
with smooth-jointed arrows, and with a broad arrow he knocked Yugandhara from
his chariot seat.

"Then Virata and Drupada, the Kekayas, Satyaki and Shibi, Vyaghradatta of
Panchala and mighty Simhasena, these and many others, wanting to save
Yudhishthira, blocked his way, scattering many arrows. Vyaghradatta of Panchala
pierced Drona with fifty sharp arrows, king, and the people cheered. Simhasena
quickly pierced the great chariot fighter Drona, and in his delight he laughed
out loud, to frighten the man of strict vows. Then Drona opened his eyes wide,
wiped his bowstring, made a great slap of his palm on it and rushed at him. Then
the mighty one attacked and, with two broad arrows, struck off Simhasena's head
with its earrings from his body, and Vyaghradatta's too. When he had crushed
those great chariot fighters of the Pandavas with swarms of arrows, he stood
close to Yudhishthira, like Death the ender.

"Then a great cry went up in Yudhishthira's army, king, as the man of strict
vows stood near: 'The king is taken!' When they saw Drona's courage, the
soldiers there said, 'Today King Dhritarashtra's son will get what he wants.
Surely Drona will come to us now, to Dhritarashtra's son, in the battle.'

"As your men talked like this, the great chariot fighter, Kunti's son, came up
fast, making the air ring with the rumble of his chariot. In that slaughter he
had made a river whose water was blood and whose whirlpools were chariots, thick
with heaps of heroes' bones, carrying off the dead like its banks. Its great
foam was the floods of arrows, and it was full of lances for fish. The Pandava
crossed that river at speed and put the Kurus to flight. Then the diademed one
rushed at once at Drona's ranks, covering them with a great net of arrows, as if
to bewilder them. The famous son of Kunti fitted and loosed his arrows so fast
and without pause that no one could see any gap between them. Neither the
quarters nor the air, neither the sky nor the earth could be seen, great king.
Everything seemed to be made of arrows. Nothing at all could be seen on the
field then, king, in the great darkness of arrows made by the bearer of the
Gandiva.

"The sun had set, and everything was covered with dust. Neither enemy nor friend
could be made out, nor anything else. Then Drona, Duryodhana and the rest
withdrew their troops. When Bibhatsu saw that the enemy were badly frightened
and had no mind to fight, he slowly withdrew his own ranks. Then the Pandavas
and the Srinjayas, full of joy, praised Partha, and so did the Panchalas, with
pleasing words, as the seers praise the sun. So Dhananjaya, having beaten his
enemies, went back to his camp, glad, with Keshava, behind all the armies. The
son of Pandu shone in his bright chariot, set with the finest sapphires, rubies,
gold, silver, diamonds, coral and crystal, like the moon in a sky bright with
stars."

**Divergences.**

* **Both fall.** In the mace duel Shalya and Bhima fall together (7.14.29). Kritavarma carries Shalya away; Bhima is up again "in the blink of an eye" (7.14.30–33).
* **The wheel guard.** 7.15.21–25 calls him only "the prince" (*kumāra*), the guard of Yudhishthira's wheel, "who brought glory to the Panchalas". The novel keeps him unnamed. *apāmṛdnāt* (7.15.25) is "crushed"; the CE does not say here that he died.
* **The end of the eleventh day.** Drona has not taken Yudhishthira, and the armies withdraw at sunset (7.15.48–50). One CE appendix passage (@4) gives Drona a speech to his charioteer before he charges; it is not narrated.
* Rejected lines not narrated: \*113–\*123 and @4.
