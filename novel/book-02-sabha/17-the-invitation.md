# The Invitation

"You are the eldest, my son, the son of the eldest wife," said Dhritarashtra.
"Do not hate the Pandavas. The man who hates takes on misery, as if it were
death itself. Yudhishthira has no guile. His aims are yours, and his friends are
your friends, and he does not hate you. How could a man like you hate him, bull
of the Bharatas? You are his equal in birth and in courage. Why do you want your
brother's fortune in your folly, king, my son? Do not be like this. Calm
yourself, and be good.

"If you long for the glory of a sacrifice like his, bull of the Bharatas, let
your priests hold the great sacrifice of the seven threads for you. The kings
will bring you great wealth too, out of affection and respect, jewels and
ornaments. To covet what is another's is a wicked thing, my son. The man who is
content with his own and keeps to his own dharma lives in happiness. Keep out of
other men's affairs, work always at your own, and strive to guard what is yours.
These are the marks of greatness. The man who is untroubled in misfortune,
skilful, always active, watchful and disciplined, always sees good fortune. Give
wealth at the altar, enjoy the pleasures you love, and play with women without a
care, bull of the Bharatas. Be at peace."

"You know, and yet you confuse me," said Duryodhana, "like a boat tied to a
boat. Do you not care for your own interest, or do you hate me? These sons of
Dhritarashtra, who have you to rule them, are as good as gone. You always talk
of what should be done now as something for the future. When the leader is led
by others, and loses his way, how can those who follow in his footsteps keep to
the path? King, your wisdom is mature. You have waited on the old, and you have
mastered your senses. Yet when we are set on our business, you confuse us badly.

"Brihaspati said that the way of kings is different from the way of the world.
So a king must always think hard of his own interest. The living of a kshatriya,
great king, is fixed on victory, whether that is dharma or not, bull of the
Bharatas, in his own way of life. The man who wants his enemy's blazing fortune
for himself, bull of the Bharatas, should drive every quarter before him, as a
charioteer drives with his goad. Whatever means will bind an enemy, hidden or
open, that is the weapon, to those who know weapons. The weapon is not only what
cuts.

"Discontent is the root of fortune, and so I want to be discontented. The man
who strives to rise, king, is the best at policy. A man must not think of power
or wealth as his own. Others take away what was won before. That is known to be
the dharma of kings. Shakra made a pact of no harm with Namuchi, and cut off his
head. That is held to be his ancient way with an enemy. The earth swallows these
two, as a snake swallows creatures that live in holes: a king who does not
fight, and a brahmin who does not travel.

"No one is a man's enemy by birth, lord of the people. The one who shares his
living is his enemy, and no one else. The man who in his folly lets his enemy's
side grow, and does nothing, has his root cut by it, as by a disease that has
been allowed to grow. Even a small enemy, whose strength keeps growing, eats up
a tree from nearby, like an anthill at its root. Ajamidha, Bharata, do not let
your enemy's fortune please you. This policy is a burden that the strong carry
on their heads. The man who wants his wealth to grow as a body grows from birth
prospers among his kin. For courage brings growth at once.

"Until I have won the Pandavas' power, I will be in doubt. Either I will win
that fortune, or I will lie dead in battle. What use is life to me now, lord of
the people, as I am? The Pandavas grow every day, and we stand still."

"This fortune you have seen in Yudhishthira, the son of Pandu, which pains you
so," said Shakuni, "I will take it. Let your enemy be challenged to dice.
Without risk, without fighting at the head of an army, just by throwing the
dice, I will win, unhurt, for I know the game, and they do not. Know that the
stakes are my bows, Bharata, and the dice my arrows. Know that the heart of the
dice is my bowstring, and the cloth I play on my chariot."

"This man knows dice, king," said Duryodhana, "and he is ready to take the
fortune from the sons of Pandu by gaming. Let it please you, father."

"I follow the guidance of my brother, the great Vidura," said Dhritarashtra. "I
will meet him and learn his judgment on this business."

"Vidura will spoil your judgment," said Duryodhana. "He has no doubts, and he is
set on the Pandavas' good, not on mine, Kaurava. A man should not start his own
business by relying on another's strength. No two men think alike on any matter,
joy of the Kurus. The fool who avoids danger and just looks after himself sinks
where he stands, like a wet mat in the rains. Neither illness nor Yama waits for
a man to get what is good for him. As long as he is able, he should do what is
good."

"My son, I do not like a quarrel with the strong, in any way," said
Dhritarashtra. "Enmity brings change, and it is a weapon not made of iron. You
think harm is gain, prince. It ties a terrible knot of strife. Once it has
started, somehow or other, it may set loose swords and arrows."

"The ancients laid down rules for playing dice," said Duryodhana. "There is no
danger in it, and no fighting. So let Shakuni's advice please you today, and
quickly order a hall built here. The door of heaven is open to us when we play,
and it is right for those who take that road. So it will make us their equals.
Arrange a game with the Pandavas."

"I do not like what you say," said Dhritarashtra. "Do what pleases you, lord of
men. Later you will regret following these words. For words like these will not
bring dharma. Vidura foresaw all this just so, long ago, for he follows wisdom
and learning. Now the great danger that kills the seed of the kshatriyas is
coming on me, and I cannot help it."

---

When he had said this, wise King Dhritarashtra thought fate supreme and hard to
overcome. He kept to his son's word, his mind confused by fate, and gave loud
orders to his men.

"Let skilled men quickly build me the finest hall, with a thousand pillars,
bright with gold and beryl. Let it have a hundred doors and arches topped with
crystal, and let it be a krosha long and as wide."

When they heard him, clever and skilful men built it quickly, eagerly and
without a doubt. Craftsmen by the thousand, well trained, brought every kind of
material into the hall. In a short time the hall was finished. It was lovely,
full of jewels and bright with colour, furnished with bright golden seats. They
reported it to the king, well pleased.

Then wise King Dhritarashtra said to Vidura, the chief of his counsellors, "Go
to Prince Yudhishthira, and bring him here quickly at my word. Tell him, 'This
hall of mine is full of jewels and bright with colour, furnished with costly
beds and seats. Let him come with his brothers and see it, and let there be a
friendly game here.'"

King Dhritarashtra knew his son's mind, and he thought fate hard to overcome,
and so he did this. But when Vidura, the best of the wise, was told this unjust
thing, he did not welcome his brother's words.

"I do not welcome this errand, king," said Vidura. "Do not do this. I fear the
ruin of the family. When your sons are divided, there will surely be strife.
That is what I fear from the dice, king."

"The strife here will not trouble me, steward," said Dhritarashtra, "if fate
does not turn against us. This whole world moves, they say, in the power of what
the Creator has ordained. It is not free. So go today, Vidura, at my command, to
the king, and quickly bring me Kunti's son Yudhishthira, who is hard to
overcome."

---

Then Vidura, sent by force by King Dhritarashtra, set out for the wise Pandavas,
with fine horses, swift, strong and well broken. The man of great wisdom went
along the road, reached the king's city, and entered it, honoured by the
twice-born. The righteous man came to the king's palace, which was like the
house of Kubera, and went to Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma.

The great-souled king, Ajatashatru, the Ajamidha, firm in truth, received Vidura
as was proper, with honour, and then asked after Dhritarashtra and his sons.

"I see no joy in your mind, steward," said Yudhishthira. "Have you come in good
health? Are the old man's sons obedient to him? Are the people loyal?"

"The great king is well, with his sons," said Vidura. "He is surrounded by kin
like Indra, king. He is happy with his well-behaved sons, free of grief, firm of
mind, content in himself. But the king of the Kurus has sent you this message,
after first asking after your health and well-being. 'Here is a hall that looks
just like yours. Come and see your brothers' hall, my son. Meet your brothers in
it, Partha. Let there be a friendly game, and enjoy yourselves. We will be glad
to have you among us, and so will all the Kurus who have gathered here.' You
will see the gaming the great King Dhritarashtra has set up there, and the
gamblers who are seated there. That is why I have come, king. Accept it."

"There is strife in dice, steward," said Yudhishthira. "What man who knows it
would want to play? Or what do you think is right? We all keep to your word."

"I know that dice are the root of disaster," said Vidura, "and I tried to stop
it. But the king sent me to you. You have heard, wise one. Do what is best."

"Who are the other gamblers there who will play," said Yudhishthira, "besides
the sons of King Dhritarashtra? I ask you, Vidura. Tell us who they are, with
whom we will meet and play for hundreds."

"Shakuni, the king of Gandhara, lord of the people," said Vidura, "a king who
plays high, with a skilled hand, and a master of dice. Vivimshati, King
Chitrasena, Satyavrata, Purumitra and Jaya."

"Very dangerous gamblers are seated there," said Yudhishthira, "men who play
with tricks and deceit. But this world is in the power of what the Creator has
ordained, they say. I will not refuse to play with those gamblers today. It is
not that I do not want to go to the game, wise one, at King Dhritarashtra's
command. A father is always dear to his son. So I will do as you have told me,
Vidura. I do not want to play with Shakuni. But if that bold man challenges me
in the hall, I will not refuse. Once challenged, I never turn back. That is my
vow, made long ago."

When the Dharma King had said this to Vidura, he quickly gave all the orders for
the journey. At daybreak the next day he set out with his people and his
followers, and with the women, Draupadi first.

"Fate steals the wits, as a glare that falls on the eye steals its sight," he
said. "A man goes into the Creator's power, as if bound with ropes."

With these words King Yudhishthira set out with the steward. Partha, the tamer
of enemies, could not bear that challenge. The Pandava, the killer of enemy
heroes, mounted the chariot the Bahlika had given him, and went with his
brothers and his train. Blazing with royal glory, he went with brahmins before
him, called by Dhritarashtra, and by the appointed hour of Time.

He came to Hastinapura and went to Dhritarashtra's house, and the righteous
Pandava met Dhritarashtra. The lord met Drona, Bhishma, Karna and Kripa, as was
fitting, and Drona's son. The mighty, strong-armed man met Somadatta,
Duryodhana, Shalya and the son of Subala. He met the other kings who had
gathered there before him, and Jayadratha, and all the Kurus. Then, surrounded
by all his brothers, the strong-armed man entered the palace of wise King
Dhritarashtra.

There he saw Queen Gandhari, true to her husband, surrounded always by her
daughters-in-law, like Rohini among the stars. He greeted Gandhari, and she
welcomed him. Then he saw his father, the old king, whose eyes were his wisdom.
The king smelled the heads of those joys of the Kurus, king, the four Pandavas
led by Bhimasena. Then joy spread among the Kauravas, lord of the people, when
they saw those handsome tigers among men, the Pandavas.

With leave, they went into rooms full of jewels, and the women, with Draupadi at
their head, came to see them. When Dhritarashtra's daughters-in-law saw
Yajnaseni's great splendour, blazing as it were, they were not very glad.

Then those tigers among men talked with the women. They took their exercise, and
did what had to be done, and dressed. When they had all said their evening
prayers, anointed with heavenly sandal paste, glad at heart, they had the
brahmins speak blessings. They ate delicious food and went to their rooms, and
the joys of the Kurus slept while women sang to them. That holy night passed for
them in the pleasures of love. Praised by bards, and rested, they woke in good
time. When they had spent the night happily, they all said their morning prayers
and entered the lovely hall, crowded with gamblers.

---

"The hall is spread, king," said Shakuni, "and these men have been waiting to
play. Let us throw the dice, Yudhishthira, and agree the rules of the game."

"Gaming is deceit, and wicked," said Yudhishthira. "There is no kshatriya
courage in it, and no sure policy, king. Why do you praise gaming? Men do not
praise a gambler's pride in deceit. Shakuni, do not beat us by crooked means,
cruelly."

"The gambler who can follow the count," said Shakuni, "who knows the ways of
deceit, who never tires of the play of the dice, bears everything in play. So
does the man of great mind who knows gaming. It is the stake at dice that may
overcome the other side. That is all you mean by it. Let us play, king. Do not
be afraid. Set your stake, and do not delay."

"The sage Asita Devala, the best of sages," said Yudhishthira, "who always
travels through the gates of the worlds, said this. To play with gamblers by
trickery is wicked. But victory in battle by dharma is the highest and the best
play. Noble men do not speak like barbarians, and they do not act by trickery.
War without crookedness or deceit is the vow of good men. We try, as far as we
can, to support brahmins who deserve honour. So do not overplay us for that
wealth, Shakuni, and do not win it from us. I do not want pleasures or riches
won by deceit. Even a gambler's play without deceit is not honoured."

"A learned man gets the better of an unlearned man by deceit, Yudhishthira,"
said Shakuni, "and a wise man of a fool. People do not call that deceit. So if
you think I am coming at you here with deceit, turn back from the game, if you
are afraid."

"Once challenged, I will not turn back," said Yudhishthira. "That is the vow I
have made. And the ordained is strong, king. I stand in the power of fate. In
this company, with whom am I to play? Who will stake against me? Then let the
game begin."

"I will provide the jewels and the wealth, lord of the people," said Duryodhana.
"And my uncle Shakuni will play for me."

"To play one man for another seems uneven to me," said Yudhishthira. "Know that,
wise one. But if you wish it, let it begin."

As the game was being arranged, all the kings entered the hall with
Dhritarashtra at their head. Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and wise Vidura followed
them, Bharata, with little joy in their hearts. Those men of great energy, with
necks like lions, sat on many fine lion thrones, in pairs and alone. The hall
shone with those kings gathered there, king, like heaven with the great gods
gathered in it. All of them knew the Vedas, all were heroes, all shone bright.
Then the friendly game began, great king.

"This rich jewel, king, born in the ocean's whirlpool," said Yudhishthira, "the
finest of its necklace, splendid, set in the best gold, is my stake, king. What
is your stake against it? Let this be the order of play, my friend. I will win
this throw."

"I have jewels and wealth of many kinds," said Duryodhana. "I do not grudge
wealth. I will win this throw."

Then Shakuni, who knew the truth of dice, took up the dice. "Won!" said Shakuni
to Yudhishthira.

**The CE's own doublets.** Dhritarashtra's order to build the hall, his wish to ask Vidura,
Duryodhana's objection, Vidura's protest and Dhritarashtra's "It is fated" all come for the
second time here (2.51.4–26; first at 2.45.39–57). Both tellings are kept.

**Divergences.**

* **Shakuni's dice.** In the CE Shakuni simply "took up the dice" and said "Won!" (2.53.25).
  Nothing is said of loaded dice, of dice made from bones, or of dice that obey him. The line
  "Shakuni resorted to deceit" here is \*506, a rejected line, but from the next throw on the CE
  itself says it at each throw (2.54.7 and after). See ledger L-18 and L-116. Yudhishthira and
  Vidura both call the gamblers deceitful (2.52.14, 2.53.2–3), and Shakuni does not deny it
  (2.53.11–12).
* **Yudhishthira's vow.** "Once challenged, I never turn back" is in the CE (2.52.16, 2.53.13).
* The vulgate adds a longer plea by Dhritarashtra, that the Pandavas are like Duryodhana's own
  arms (\*488–\*491), which Ganguli translates. Not in the CE.
