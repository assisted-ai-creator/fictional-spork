# Jara

"Arjuna has shown the mind that suits one born in the line of Bharata, and a son
of Kunti," said Vasudeva. "We do not know when death will come, by night or by
day. And we have never heard of anyone becoming immortal by not fighting. This
is all that men must do to satisfy their hearts: to go against their enemies
with policy, by the way the rules lay down. In battle, the highest course is
good policy without a flaw. When two are equal, doubt arises, and two can never
be quite equal. If we take the path of policy and get close to the enemy's body,
how can we fail to reach his end, as a river's current reaches a tree? Let us
strike at his weak point and keep our own covered.

"The wise say one should not attack a stronger enemy with armies drawn up in
array. I think so too. We will go into the enemy's house unseen, without blame,
and come at his body, and so we will win what we want. He alone always carries
that glory, bull among men, as the inner self carries a body. When he is
destroyed, his strength is destroyed. And if we kill him in battle, and then
face the rest of them, we will go to heaven, devoted to saving our kin."

"Krishna," said Yudhishthira, "who is this Jarasandha? What is his strength and
his courage? He touched you, who are like fire, and was not burned like a moth."

"Hear, king, what Jarasandha's strength and courage are," said Krishna, "and why
we have so often let him be, though he has done us many wrongs.

"There was a king named Brihadratha, lord and master of Magadha, proud in
battle, with three armies. He was handsome, full of courage, glorious, of
unequalled might, his body always lean from the vows of sacrifice, like a second
Shatakratu. He was like the sun in splendour, like the earth in patience, like
Yama the destroyer in anger, like Vaishravana in wealth. His qualities, which
came with his birth, spread over the whole earth, best of the Bharatas, as the
sun's rays do.

"That mighty king married the twin daughters of the king of Kashi, bull of the
Bharatas, rich in beauty and wealth. That bull among men made a promise to them
both, in private: 'I will never favour one of you over the other.' With those
two dear and fitting wives, lord of men, the king shone like an elephant with
two she-elephants. Between them the lord of the earth shone like the ocean in
bodily form, between the Ganga and the Yamuna.

"But while he was lost in pleasures, his youth passed, and no son was born to
him to carry on his line. With many rites of good omen, with fire offerings and
sacrifices for a son, the best of kings could not get a son to make his family
grow.

"Then he heard of the generous Chandakaushika, son of great Gautama Kakshivat,
who was weary from austerity. The sage had come by chance and was sitting at the
foot of a tree. The king, with his two wives, pleased him with every kind of
jewel. Then the best of seers, true to his word and firm in truth, said to him,
'I am pleased with you, king. Choose a boon, you of good vows.'

"Then Brihadratha and his wives bowed to him, and in despair of ever seeing a
son, he said in a voice choked with tears, 'Blessed one, I have given up my
kingdom and set out for the forest of austerity. What use is a boon to one so
unfortunate? What use is a kingdom to one who has no children?'

"When he heard this, the sage's heart was stirred, and he sank into thought. He
sat in the shade of that same mango tree. As the sage sat there, a single mango
fell into his lap, they say, untouched by the wind, and not pecked by a parrot.
The best of sages took it, spoke a mantra over it in his heart, and gave it to
the king, a fruit beyond compare, that would bring him a son. And the great wise
sage said to the king, 'Go, king. Your wish is fulfilled. Go back, lord of men.'

"Remembering his promise, the best of kings gave the one fruit to his two wives,
bull of the Bharatas. The two lovely women cut the mango in two and ate it.
Because it had to be, and because the sage spoke the truth, the two of them
conceived from eating the fruit. When the king saw them, he was overjoyed.

"Then, wise king, when the time came, each of them gave birth to half a body.
Each half had one eye, one arm and one leg, and half a belly, half a face and
half a buttock. When they saw the two halves of a body, they both shook with
fear. The two poor women, in their dismay, took counsel together as sisters, and
in their misery they abandoned those two halves of a living creature. Their two
nurses wrapped the lumps of the miscarriage, went out through the door of the
women's quarters, threw them away, and hurried off.

"Then a rakshasi named Jara, who ate flesh and blood, tiger among men, picked up
the halves where they lay at a crossroads. Wanting to carry them easily, the
rakshasi, driven by the power of fate, pressed the two halves together. As soon
as they were brought together, bull among men, they became one body, and a hero
was born, a boy. Then the rakshasi, king, her eyes wide with wonder, could not
carry the child, who was as hard as diamond. The boy made fists of his
copper-coloured hands, put them in his mouth, and roared with great fury, like a
cloud full of rain.

"At that sound the people in the women's quarters came out at once, in alarm,
tiger among men, with the king, tamer of enemies. And the two weary women, their
breasts full of milk, who had lost hope of a son, hurried out too. When the
rakshasi saw them like that, and the king longing for a son, and the mighty
child, she thought, *I live in the land of this king who longs for a son. It is
not right for me to take his child, as a streak of cloud might take the sun.*

"She took on a human form and said to the lord of men, 'Brihadratha, this is
your son. I give him to you. Take him. He was born to your two wives by the
command of the best of brahmins. His nurses abandoned him, and I have kept him
safe.'

"Then the two lovely daughters of the king of Kashi, best of the Bharatas, ran
to the child at once and bathed him with the milk of their breasts. The king was
overjoyed when he understood it all, and he asked the rakshasi, who was no
rakshasi now, but bright as new gold, 'Who are you, with the glow of a lotus's
heart, who have given me my son? Tell me, lovely one, if you will. You seem to
me a goddess.'

"'I am a rakshasi named Jara,' she said. 'Bless you. I can take any form I wish.
I have lived happily in your house, king of kings, and been honoured there. I
have been thinking day and night how to repay you. And I saw these two halves of
your son, righteous king. When I joined them together, by fate a boy was born.
It is by your good fortune, great king. I was only the means.'

"With that, king, she vanished on the spot. The king took the boy and went into
his house. The king did all that had to be done for the child, and he ordered a
great festival for the rakshasi in Magadha. His father, who was like Prajapati,
gave him his name there: because he had been joined, *saṃdhita*, by Jara, he
became Jarasandha. The splendid son of the lord of Magadha grew, great in size
and strength, like a fire fed with offerings.

"After some time the blessed Chandakaushika, of great austerity, came to Magadha
again. Delighted at his coming, Brihadratha went out with his ministers, his
household, his wives and his son. He honoured him, Bharata, with water for his
feet, the guest offering and water to rinse his mouth, and the king offered him
his son, with his kingdom. The blessed seer accepted the king's honour, and said
to the Magadhan, king, with a glad heart:

"'I have learned all this, king, with the eye of knowledge. Now hear, king of
kings, what this son will be. No kings will match the courage of this mighty
man. Even weapons hurled by the gods will not hurt him, lord of the earth, any
more than rivers hurt a mountain. He will blaze above the heads of all
consecrated kings, and dim them all, as the sun dims the stars. Kings with great
armies and chariots will come against him and perish, like moths in a fire. He
will take the gathered glory of all kings, as the ocean takes the rivers swollen
with the rains. This mighty man will hold up the four classes well, as the rich
earth that bears every crop holds up good and bad. All kings will be subject to
his command, as all embodied beings are to the wind, the self of all beings.
This Magadhan, the strongest in all the worlds, will see with his own eyes
Rudra, the great god, Hara, who destroyed the three cities.'

"As he said this, the sage, thinking of his own business, gave King Brihadratha
leave to go, slayer of enemies. The lord of Magadha went into his city with his
kin and relations, consecrated Jarasandha, and King Brihadratha found the
greatest peace. When Jarasandha had been consecrated, King Brihadratha, followed
by his two wives, gave himself to the forest of austerity. While his father
lived in the forest of austerity with his two mothers, Bharata, Jarasandha
brought the kings under his power by his own courage. After a long time, King
Brihadratha, living in the forest of austerity, went to heaven with his wives,
having practised austerity.

"He had Hamsa and Dibhaka, whom no weapon could kill, the best of counsellors
and masters of the science of war. I told you of those two mighty men before.
Three of them were enough for the three worlds, I think. That is why, hero, we
mighty Kukuras, Andhakas and Vrishnis let him be, great king, for policy's
sake."

**Divergences.**

* **Jara, the house goddess.** The vulgate makes Jara a household goddess, painted on the walls
  of houses and worshipped there (\*185), and has her boast that she could eat Mount Meru (\*186).
  In the CE she says only that she had lived happily in the king's house and been honoured there
  (2.17.1).
* The vulgate adds that Brihadratha gave up his kingdom and went to the forest before meeting the
  sage (\*178). In the CE he says he has done so (2.16.26).

**Choices.**

* 2.16.4–7: Krishna's argument for a secret approach is kept close to the text. It is obscure in
  places.
* 2.16.12 *akṣauhiṇīnāṃ tisṛṇām*: "with three armies", three *akṣauhiṇīs*.
* 2.16.22 *kākṣīvataḥ putraṃ gautamasya*: "son of Gautama Kakshivat".
* 2.16.28 *avātam aśukādaṣṭam*: "untouched by the wind, and not pecked by a parrot". *kila*:
  "they say".
* 2.16.37 *garbhasaṃplave*: "the lumps of the miscarriage".
* 2.17.6 *jarayā saṃdhitaḥ*: the text's etymology of Jarasandha, "joined by Jara".
