# The Second Game

"When the sons of Dhritarashtra knew that the Pandavas had been given leave to
go, with their jewels and their heaps of wealth," said Janamejaya, "what did
they think then?"

When he knew, king, that wise Dhritarashtra had given the Pandavas leave to go,
Duhshasana went at once to his brother. He found Duryodhana with his ministers,
bull of the Bharatas, and said this in great distress.

"That old man is throwing away what we won with such pain. He has sent our
wealth back to the enemy. Understand that, great warriors."

Then Duryodhana, Karna and Shakuni, the son of Subala, proud men all of them,
took counsel together against the Pandavas. They went in haste to the wise King
Dhritarashtra, the son of Vichitravirya, and spoke to him smoothly.

"Have you not heard, king," said Duryodhana, "what Brihaspati said, the learned
priest of the gods, when he taught the policy of Shakra? 'Enemies must be killed
by every means, scourge of enemies, before they do you harm by war or by force.'
If we honour all the kings with the Pandavas' wealth, and then fight the
Pandavas, what have we to lose? Who would put angry snakes, full of poison and
ready to strike, round his neck and on his back, and then let them go? The
Pandavas are angry, father. They have taken up their weapons and mounted their
chariots, and like angry snakes they will leave nothing of us.

"Arjuna goes in his armour, with his two great quivers open. He takes up Gandiva
again and again, and sighs, and looks about him. We have heard that Vrikodara
has raised his heavy mace, yoked his chariot in haste, and driven out. Nakula
has taken his sword and his shield with eight moons on it. Sahadeva and the king
have shown what they mean by their looks and gestures. They have all mounted
chariots loaded with weapons, and gone out, urging on their chariot teams, to
raise an army. They will never forgive us for the wrongs we have done them. And
who among them could forgive what was done to Draupadi?

"Bless you, let us play with the Pandavas again, for exile in the forest. In
that way we can bring them under our power, bull of the Bharatas. Either they or
we, whoever is beaten at dice, will go into the great forest for twelve years,
dressed in deerskins. The thirteenth year they will spend among people without
being recognised. If they are recognised, they must live another twelve years in
the forest. Let it be either we or they, and let the game begin. Let the dice be
thrown, and let the Pandavas play this second game.

"This is the thing we most need to do, king, bull of the Bharatas. For Shakuni
here knows all the art and skill of dice. We will be firmly rooted in the
kingdom. We will win allies, and honour a great army, strong and hard to
overcome. And if they manage to keep their vow through the thirteenth year, we
will beat them, king. Let this please you, scourge of enemies."

"Bring them back quickly," said Dhritarashtra, "even if they are far along the
road. Let them come and play this second game, the Pandavas."

Then Drona, Somadatta, and Bahlika, the great chariot fighter, spoke up. So did
Vidura, Drona's son, and mighty Yuyutsu, the son of the vaishya woman. So did
Bhurishravas, the son of Shantanu, and Vikarna, the great chariot fighter. They
all said, "No dice! Let there be peace." But against the will of all those
friends, who saw what was at stake, Dhritarashtra, who loved his son, had the
Pandavas called back.

Then Gandhari, worn out with grief, spoke to King Dhritarashtra, lord of the
people, words of dharma, out of love for her sons.

"When Duryodhana was born, the wise steward said, 'This disgrace of the family
had better be sent to the other world. For as soon as he was born he howled like
a jackal, Bharata. Surely he will be the end of this family. Know that, Kurus.'
Do not follow the ideas of these ill-taught boys, my lord. Do not be the cause
of the terrible ruin of the family. Who would break a dam once it is built, or
fan a fire that has gone out? Who would anger the Parthas again, now they are at
peace, Bharata?

"You remember it, Ajamidha, but I will remind you again. Teaching does not teach
a fool, for good or for ill. An old man should never have a child's mind, king.
Let your sons be guided by you. Do not let them break away and leave you. Let
your mind be as it was, born of peace, of dharma and of good counsel. Do not let
it turn against itself. Fortune won by cruelty is destroyed. Fortune that grows
gently passes on to sons and grandsons."

Then the great king said to Gandhari, who saw dharma, "Let the end of the family
come, if it will. I cannot stop it. Let it be as they wish. Let the Pandavas
come back, and let my sons play the second game with the Pandavas."

---

Then Pratikamin, at the word of wise King Dhritarashtra, went after Partha
Yudhishthira, who was far along the road. He said to him, "The hall is spread,
king, and the dice are thrown, Yudhishthira. Your father says to you, 'Come,
Pandava, and play,' Bharata."

"Creatures meet with good and ill by the Creator's decree," said Yudhishthira.
"There is no escaping them, if I must play again. This challenge to dice comes
at the old man's command. I know it will bring ruin, but I cannot go against
it."

So saying, the Pandava turned back with his brothers. Knowing Shakuni's
trickery, Partha went to the game again. The great warriors, the bulls of the
Bharatas, entered the hall once more, and their friends' hearts ached. They sat
down as they pleased to begin the second game, driven by fate to the ruin of the
whole world.

"The old man gave you back your wealth," said Shakuni, "and that was well done.
But hear one stake of great price, bull of the Bharatas. If we are beaten at
dice by you, we will go into the great forest for twelve years, dressed in the
skins of the ruru deer. The thirteenth year we will spend among people without
being recognised. If we are recognised, we will live another twelve years in the
forest. Or if you are beaten by us, you will live twelve years in the forest
with Krishna, dressed in deerskins. When the thirteenth year is over, each side
will take back its own kingdom, as is right. On these terms, Yudhishthira, the
dice are thrown for the second game with us. Come and play, Bharata."

"Shame on his kin, who do not warn him of this great danger," said the men in
the hall. "The bulls of the Bharatas themselves do not understand what their own
minds should tell them."

The king heard the people saying many things like this. But out of shame and his
devotion to dharma, Partha went to the game again. Wise as he was, and knowing
it, he played the second game. "Perhaps this will not be the ruin of the Kurus,"
he thought.

"How could a king like me, who keeps to his own dharma," said Yudhishthira,
"turn back when he is challenged? I play against you, Shakuni."

"Cattle and horses, many milch cows, goats and sheep without end," said Shakuni,
"elephants, treasure, gold, and slave women and slave men. All that aside, this
is our one stake, Pandavas, exile in the forest. Whoever is beaten, you or we,
will live in the forest. On these terms let us play, bull of the Bharatas, with
a single throw, for exile in the forest, Bharata."

Partha accepted the stake, and the son of Subala took up the dice. "Won!" said
Shakuni to Yudhishthira.

---

The beaten Parthas made up their minds to go into exile in the forest, and one
after another they put on deerskins as their upper garments. When Duhshasana saw
those tamers of enemies dressed in deerskins, robbed of their kingdom, and
setting out for the forest, he said this.

"The wheel of the great King Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, has begun to
turn. The sons of Pandu are beaten, and have come to the worst of misfortune.
Today the gods have come our way, by smooth roads and not rough ones. For we are
senior in virtue and in years, and greater than our enemies. The Parthas have
been thrown into hell for a long time, without end. They have lost their
happiness and their kingdom for ever and ever. Drunk with their strength, they
laughed at the sons of Dhritarashtra. Now they are beaten and robbed of their
wealth, and they will go to the forest, these Pandavas.

"Let them take off their bright armour and their shining heavenly clothes. Let
them all dress in the skins of the ruru deer, since they accepted the son of
Subala's stake. They always thought, 'There are no men like us in all the
worlds.' Today, in their ruin, these Pandavas will know themselves for what they
are, like barren sesame seeds. This is the dress that comes to proud men like
these, Kaurava. May it never come to you. Look at the strong Pandavas in their
deerskins, like men who were never consecrated for a sacrifice.

"The wise Somaka Yajnasena did a bad thing when he gave the girl of Panchala to
the Pandavas. The Parthas, Yajnaseni's husbands, are eunuchs. You have seen them
in fine cloaks, and now you will see them in deerskins in the forest, without
wealth or a place to stand. What joy will you have, Yajnaseni? Choose another
husband here, whoever you want. All these Kurus gathered here are patient and
self-controlled and rich. Choose one of them as your husband, and this turn of
the times will not hurt you. The Pandavas are all like barren sesame seeds, like
deer made of leather, like the empty husks of barley. Why wait on the fallen
Pandavas? It is wasted labour to tend barren sesame."

So the cruel son of Dhritarashtra made the Parthas listen to harsh words. When
he heard them, Bhimasena, who could never bear an insult, rebuked him loudly,
holding himself back in his anger. He went straight up to him, as a lion of the
Himalaya goes up to a jackal, and said this.

"Cruel man, you talk the empty talk of wicked men. You boast among the kings of
what you won by the art of Gandhara. As you pierce our vitals here with the
arrows of your words, so I will remind you of it in battle, when I cut into your
vitals. And those who follow you, slaves of desire and greed, as your
protectors, I will send with all their kin to the house of Yama."

As he said this, dressed in deerskins, overcome with grief, bound by dharma to
his path, Duhshasana danced round him in the midst of the Kurus, shameless,
crying, "Cow! Cow!"

"Cruel, harsh, vile words are easy for you, Duhshasana," said Bhima. "Who else
would boast, when he had won wealth by deceit? May Partha Vrikodara never reach
the worlds of the good, if I do not break open your chest in battle and drink
your blood. I will kill the sons of Dhritarashtra in battle, while every archer
looks on, and soon I will be at peace. I tell you all this truly."

As the Pandavas left the hall, the foolish King Duryodhana, in his glee, mocked
Bhimasena's lion's walk, copying it with his own. "Do not think that is the end
of it," said Vrikodara to him, turning half round. "I will soon kill you and all
your followers, fool, and remind you of this, and give you my answer."

Bhima saw the insult to himself, but he held back his anger. The strong, proud
man followed the king, and as he left the assembly of the Kauravas he said this.

"I will kill Duryodhana. Dhananjaya will kill Karna. Sahadeva will kill Shakuni,
the gambler with dice. And I say this great word again, in the middle of the
hall. The gods will make it true, when our war comes. I will kill this wicked
Suyodhana with my mace in battle, and I will set my foot on his head on the
ground. And I will drink the blood of this harsh and wicked Duhshasana, this
hero in words, like a lion."

"The resolve of good men is not known by their words, Bhima," said Arjuna. "In
the fourteenth year from now, they will see what happens. The earth will drink
the blood of Duryodhana, of Karna, of wicked Shakuni, and of Duhshasana, the
fourth. At your bidding, Bhimasena, I will kill Karna in battle, the jealous
man, the talker, who sets the wicked on. Arjuna promises, to please Bhima: in
battle I will kill Karna, and those who follow Karna, with my arrows. And any
other kings who fight me, in the folly of their minds, I will send to the house
of Yama with my sharp arrows. The Himalaya would move from its place, the sun
would lose its light, the moon its coolness, before my word would fail. If
Duryodhana does not give back the kingdom with honour, in the fourteenth year
from now, this will come true."

When Partha had said this, the glorious son of Madri, mighty Sahadeva, raised
his great arm. Wanting to kill the son of Subala, his eyes red with anger,
hissing like a snake, he said this.

"You fool, you who destroy the fame of the Gandharas, the things you think are
dice are not dice. You have chosen sharp arrows in battle. I will do the deed
Bhima spoke of, against you and your kin. Do everything you have to do. I will
attack you and kill you by force in battle, with your kin, if you stand and
fight by the dharma of the kshatriyas, son of Subala."

When he heard Sahadeva's words, Nakula too, the handsomest of men, lord of the
people, said this. "This daughter of Yajnasena was made to hear harsh words in
this game, from sons of Dhritarashtra who were set on pleasing Duryodhana. Those
ill-behaved sons of Dhritarashtra, driven by Time and bent on dying, I will show
the house of Vaivasvata, most of them. At the Dharma King's command, following
the path of Draupadi, I will soon rid the earth of the sons of Dhritarashtra."

When those tigers among men, all long of arm, had sworn their many vows, they
went to Dhritarashtra.

**Divergences.**

* **The terms.** In the CE the stake of the second game is twelve years in the forest and a
  thirteenth year unrecognised among people, with another twelve years if they are recognised
  (2.66.18–19, 2.67.9–12). It is decided by a single throw (2.67.20–21).
* **The Pandavas' arming.** That the Pandavas had armed and gone to raise an army is Duryodhana's
  report, "we have heard" (2.66.13), not the narrator's.
* **The vows.** Bhima's vows to break Duryodhana's head underfoot and to drink Duhshasana's blood,
  Arjuna's to kill Karna, Sahadeva's to kill Shakuni and Nakula's are all in the CE
  (2.68.20–45). See ledger L-20. The vulgate adds Sahadeva's vow to kill Shakuni's son Uluka
  (\*591). Not in the CE.
* The vulgate adds the verse that even Rama was lured by a golden deer, when such a thing cannot
  exist (\*583). Not in the CE.
