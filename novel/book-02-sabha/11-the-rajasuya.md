# The Rajasuya

Because the Dharma King protected them, kept to the truth and destroyed their
enemies, the people were devoted to their own work. Because he took the taxes
rightly and ruled by dharma, the rain god rained as much as anyone could wish,
and the country flourished. Every undertaking went well, cattle-keeping, farming
and trade. All of it came about above all through the king's work. No false word
was heard, king, from robbers, from cheats, from one man against another, or
from the king's favourites. Drought and flood, disease and fire, none of these
were known then, while Yudhishthira kept to dharma always. Kings came to him of
their own accord, each for himself, to please him, to wait on him and to bring
the tribute that was his by nature, and for no other business. Through the
wealth that came to him by right, his great store grew so large that it could
not be used up in a hundred years.

When the king, Kunti's son, lord of the earth, knew the size of his treasury and
his granary, he set his mind on the sacrifice. All his friends said to him, each
alone and all together, "The time for your sacrifice has come, lord. Let it be
done now."

While they were saying this, Hari came, the ancient seer, whose soul is the
Veda, who can be seen by those who know. He is the best of all that moves and
all that stands still, their source and their end, the lord of past, future and
present, Keshava, the slayer of Keshin. He is the rampart of all the Vrishnis,
who gives them safety in danger, the slayer of enemies. He had left
Anakadundubhi in command of the army, and the tiger among men, Madhava, came
with a great army, bringing the Dharma King a flood of wealth of every kind.
That endless flood of wealth, that inexhaustible ocean of jewels, he brought
into the finest of cities, filling it with the roar of his chariots. As a
sunless place rejoices in the sun, and a windless place in the wind, the city of
the Bharatas rejoiced when Krishna came.

Yudhishthira met him gladly and honoured him as the rules require. He asked
after his welfare, and Krishna sat down at ease. Then the bull among men, with
his priests led by Dhaumya and Dvaipayana, and with Bhima, Arjuna and the twins,
said to Krishna:

"It is thanks to you, Krishna, that the whole earth is under my power, and it is
by your grace, Varshneya, that I have gained so much wealth. I want to use it
all as the rules require, son of Devaki, on the best of brahmins and on the
carrier of offerings, Madhava. I want to sacrifice with you, Dasharha, and with
my younger brothers, strong-armed one. Give me your leave. Or have yourself
consecrated, Govinda, strong-armed one. If you sacrifice, Dasharha, I shall be
free of sin. Or give me leave, lord, with these younger brothers of mine. With
your leave, Krishna, I may achieve this finest of sacrifices."

Krishna answered him, speaking at length of his virtues. "You, tiger among
kings, are the emperor worthy of the great sacrifice. Achieve it. When you have
achieved it, we shall have done all we need to do. Offer the sacrifice you long
for. I stand ready to serve your good. Set me to work, and I will do whatever
you say."

"My purpose bears fruit, Krishna," said Yudhishthira, "and my success is sure,
since you have come to me, Hrishikesha, as I wished."

---

With Krishna's leave, the Pandava and his brothers began to gather the means for
the Rajasuya. The Pandava, the slayer of enemies, gave orders to Sahadeva, the
best of fighters, and to all his ministers. "Let men quickly bring together, in
the right order and as is fitting, the parts of the sacrifice that the brahmins
have laid down for this rite. Bring all the equipment and all the things of good
omen, and the materials for the sacrifice that Dhaumya names. Let Indrasena,
Vishoka, and Puru, Arjuna's driver, see to the bringing of food, to please me.
Let everything that is wished for be made, best of the Kurus, with fine flavours
and scents, delightful and pleasing to the brahmins."

As soon as he had spoken, Sahadeva, the best of fighters, told the great Dharma
King that it had all been done.

Then Dvaipayana brought the priests, king, fortunate brahmins who were like the
Vedas themselves in bodily form. Satyavati's son himself acted as the Brahman
priest. The bull of the Dhananjayas, Susaman, was the singer of the chants.
Yajnavalkya, the most learned in Brahman, was the chief Adhvaryu. Paila, son of
Vasu, was the Hotri, with Dhaumya. Their pupils and sons, bull of the Bharatas,
all masters of the Veda and its limbs, were the assistant priests. They had the
holy day proclaimed, and when they had considered the rite, they made ready the
great place of sacrifice as the treatises require. There, with their leave, the
craftsmen built large and jewelled shelters, like the houses of the gods.

Then the king, the best of kings and of the Kurus, at once told his minister
Sahadeva, "Send out swift messengers at once to invite them." Hearing the king's
word, he sent out messengers. "Invite the brahmins and the kings in the
kingdoms, and the vaishyas, and the shudras who deserve respect. Bring them
all."

At the Pandava's command they invited all the kings of the earth, and he sent
out others too. Then, at the proper time, the brahmins consecrated Kunti's son
Yudhishthira for the Rajasuya, Bharata. When he had been consecrated, the
righteous Dharma King Yudhishthira went to the place of sacrifice, surrounded by
thousands of brahmins. His brothers, his kin and his friends went with him, and
his counsellors and ministers, and kshatriyas who had come from many lands, lord
of men. The best of kings looked like Dharma in bodily form.

Brahmins came there from every country, skilled in all the sciences, masters of
the Veda and its limbs. At the Dharma King's command, thousands of craftsmen
built lodgings for them and their companions, each separate, with plenty of food
and with beds, and with the comforts of every season. There the brahmins stayed,
king, greatly honoured, telling many stories and watching the actors and
dancers. The great noise of the great brahmins, eating and talking happily, was
heard there day and night. "Give! Give! Eat! Eat!" Talk like this was heard
there all the time. The Dharma King gave them, each separately, hundreds of
thousands of cows, beds, gold and women, Bharata.

So the sacrifice of the great Pandava went on, the one hero on earth, like
Shakra's in heaven.

---

Then King Yudhishthira sent the Pandava Nakula to Hastinapura, bull of the
Bharatas. He sent him to Bhishma, to Drona, to Dhritarashtra, to Vidura and to
Kripa, and to all his cousins who were fond of Yudhishthira. Nakula, the victor
in battle, went to Hastinapura, and the Pandava invited Bhishma and
Dhritarashtra. They set out with glad hearts for the sacrifice, with brahmins in
front, men who knew sacrifice, when they heard of the Dharma King's sacrifice.
And hundreds of others came, bull among men, with contented hearts, wanting to
see the hall and the Dharma King, the Pandava.

All the kings came there from every quarter, Bharata, bringing jewels of many
kinds, and great ones. Dhritarashtra and Bhishma, and wise Vidura, and all the
brothers led by Duryodhana came. All the kings, led by the teacher, were
honoured and invited. Subala, king of Gandhara, and mighty Shakuni, Achala and
Vrishaka, and Karna, the best of chariot fighters, came. So did Rita, Shalya the
king of Madra, and the great warrior Bahlika, Somadatta the Kaurava, Bhuri,
Bhurishravas and Shala, Ashvatthama, Kripa and Drona, and Jayadratha of Sindhu.
Yajnasena came with his son, and Shalva, lord of the earth, and the glorious
Bhagadatta, king of Pragjyotisha, with all the barbarians who live in the
lowlands by the sea. The mountain kings came, and King Brihadbala, Paundraka
Vasudeva, the kings of Vanga and Kalinga, Akarsha and Kuntala, the Vanavasyas
and the Andhrakas. The Dravidas came, and the Simhalas, the king of Kashmir,
Kuntibhoja of great splendour, and mighty Suhma. The other Bahlika kings came,
all of them heroes. Virata came with his sons, and the great warrior Machella,
and kings and princes who ruled many lands. Shishupala of great courage came
with his son, Bharata, to the Pandava's sacrifice, mad for battle. Rama came,
and Aniruddha, Babhru with Sarana, Gada, Pradyumna and Samba, and mighty
Charudeshna, Ulmuka and Nishatha, and the hero Pradyumna's son. All the other
Vrishnis came, great warriors. These and many other kings from the middle
country came to the great Rajasuya sacrifice of the son of Pandu.

At the Dharma King's command they were given lodgings, king, with many rooms,
fair with ponds and trees. The son of Dharma honoured them all beyond compare,
and when they had been honoured, the kings went to the lodgings they had been
shown. These were like peaks of Kailasa, lovely, adorned with fine things,
surrounded on every side by high, well-built white walls, hung with nets of
gold, bright with floors of gems. They had easy stairs, great seats and
furnishings, and they were hung with garlands and fragrant with the finest aloe.
They were as white as a swan's feathers, and beautiful to see from a league
away. They were spacious, with doors of equal size, with every kind of comfort,
their walls inlaid with many metals, like peaks of the Himalaya.

When they had rested, the kings saw the Dharma King Yudhishthira, who gave such
rich fees to the priests, surrounded by many members of the assembly. That
assembly, crowded with kings and with great brahmins, shone then, king, like the
vault of heaven with the immortals.

---

Yudhishthira went out to meet his grandfather and his teacher, and bowed to
them. Then, king, he said to Bhishma, Drona, Kripa, Drona's son, Duryodhana and
Vivimshati, "Help me in every way at this sacrifice. This wealth of mine, and I
myself, are yours. Please yourselves as you wish, without restraint."

When he had said this to them all, the eldest Pandava, who had been consecrated,
gave them each their tasks, as was fitting. He put Duhshasana in charge of the
food. He told Ashvatthama to receive the brahmins. He set Sanjaya to return the
kings' courtesies. The two wise men, Bhishma and Drona, were to know what had
been done and what had not. The king set Kripa to watch over the gold, the
precious metals and the jewels, and to give out the fees for the priests. And he
gave other tasks to other tigers among men. Bahlika, Dhritarashtra, Somadatta
and Jayadratha, brought by Nakula, enjoyed themselves there as if they were the
masters. The steward Vidura, who knew all dharma, was the paymaster. And
Duryodhana received all the gifts.

The whole world had come together, wanting to win the highest reward, wanting to
see the hall and the Dharma King, the Pandava. No one brought a gift worth less
than a thousand. They honoured the Dharma King with many jewels. "How can the
Kaurava finish his sacrifice with my gifts of jewels?" So the kings gave wealth,
each trying to outdo the others.

The assembly of great Kaunteya shone, king. There were houses topped with
turrets, high and guarded by troops, the palaces of the kings of the world and
the lodgings of the brahmins. There were heavenly lodgings built like palaces in
the sky, bright and full of jewels and the greatest wealth. And the kings were
gathered there in their great glory. Rivalling the god Varuna in his wealth,
Yudhishthira offered the sacrifice with the six fires and with rich fees, and he
satisfied all people with every good thing they could wish for. That gathering
had plenty of food and plenty to eat, it was crowded with people who had eaten
well, and it was busy with gifts of jewels. With the offerings of butter and the
oblations, with mantras and with learning, the gods were satisfied at that
sacrifice, spread out by the great seers. As the gods were satisfied, so were
the brahmins, with the fees, the food and great wealth. People of every class
were satisfied at that sacrifice, and full of joy.

**Divergences.**

* **Who did what.** In the CE Duryodhana receives the gifts (2.32.8), which will feed his envy.
  The vulgate adds that Krishna himself washed the brahmins' feet (\*347), that Karna kept the
  treasury (\*348), and that Kunti, Madri and Gandhari received the women (\*346). None of these
  is in the CE. See ledger L-112.
* **The date.** The vulgate adds the day of the consecration, the new moon of Jyeshtha (\*337).
  Not in the CE.
