# The Tribute

"How did that game of dice come about among the brothers," said Janamejaya,
"that brought so much ruin, and that great disaster on my grandfathers, the
Pandavas? Who were the kings who sat in that hall, best of those who know
brahman? Who approved of it, and who opposed it? I want you to tell me this in
full, brahmin, best of the twice-born, for it was the root of the destruction of
the earth."

When the king asked him this, Vyasa's mighty pupil, who knew all the Vedas, told
him everything as it had happened.

"Listen to this story in full once more, best of the Bharatas," said
Vaishampayana, "if you have a mind to hear it, great king."

When Dhritarashtra, the son of Ambika, knew what Vidura thought, he spoke to
Duryodhana again, alone.

"Enough of dice, son of Gandhari. Vidura does not approve of it. That man of
great wisdom will never tell us anything that is not for our good. I think what
Vidura says is for our highest good. Do all of it, my son. I think it is for
your good. Vidura knows all the teaching, with its secrets, that the blessed
Brihaspati of generous mind, the divine seer and teacher of Vasava, gave to the
wise king of the gods. Vidura is a great sage, and I always keep to his word, my
son. Wise Vidura is thought the best of the Kurus, king, as Uddhava, of great
intelligence, is honoured among the Vrishnis.

"So enough of dice, my son. In dice one sees division, and division is the ruin
of a kingdom. So give it up, my son. You have gained, my child, the thing a
father and mother count highest for a son, the place of your father and
grandfathers. You have studied, you are skilled in the teachings, and you have
always been cherished at home. You are the eldest of the brothers, and you hold
the kingship. What good thing do you lack? You have won the finest food and
clothing, strong-armed one, that common people cannot get. Why do you grieve, my
son? You rule a great, rich kingdom, strong-armed one, the kingdom of your
fathers and grandfathers. You give commands every day and shine like the lord of
the gods in heaven. You are known for your wisdom. How has this grief taken root
in you, this grief so full of pain? You should tell me."

"The wicked man who sees only that he eats and dresses," said Duryodhana, "and
feels no resentment, is called the lowest of men. Common fortune does not please
me, king of kings, my lord. I have seen the fortune of Kunti's son, blazing as
it were, and it pains me. I have seen the whole earth obedient to Yudhishthira,
and I must be made of stone, to go on living. I tell you this out of grief.

"The Chaitrakis, the Kaukuras, the Karaskaras and the Lohajanghas look like
bondsmen, won over and obedient, in Yudhishthira's house. All the lands of the
Himalaya, the sea and the marshes, all the mines of jewels, even the peoples at
the ends of the earth, are outdone by Yudhishthira's house. Yudhishthira thought
me the eldest and the best, lord of the people, and honoured me, and set me to
receive the jewels. Of the fine and costly jewels brought there, no end could be
seen, Bharata, and no beginning. My hand gave out as I took in that wealth. When
I was tired, they waited, holding the wealth they had brought from far away.

"Maya had made a lotus pool, with jewels from Lake Bindu and a covering of
crystal. I saw it and thought it was full of water. As I lifted my clothes,
Vrikodara laughed at me, as I stood there stunned by my enemy's great wealth, a
man without jewels. If I had been able, I would have struck Vrikodara down there
and then. For to be laughed at by a rival burns me, Bharata. And again I came to
a pool just like it, full of lotuses. I took it for stone, and I fell into the
water, lord of men. Krishna and Partha laughed at me there out loud, and
Draupadi with the women, and it wounded my heart. My clothes were soaked, and
the servants, at the king's command, gave me other clothes, and that hurt me
even more.

"Hear another trick, lord of men, as I tell it. I was going out through a place
that looked like a door, but was no door. I struck my forehead on the stone
again and cut it. The handsome twins saw me there from far off, they say, and
together they held me in their arms, grieving. And Sahadeva said to me, as if in
surprise, 'This is the door, king. Go this way.' He said it again and again. I
saw jewels in that hall whose names I had never heard before, and it burns my
mind.

---

"Hear what I saw of the Pandavas' wealth, Bharata, the finest wealth, brought by
the lords of the earth from everywhere. When I saw my enemy's riches, I could
not find my footing. Learn of it, Bharata, by what it yielded and by the lands
it came from.

"The king of the Kambojas gave woollens, cloths and cat-fur, worked with gold,
and the finest cloaks and skins. He gave three hundred horses speckled like
partridges, with noses like parrots, and three hundred she-camels, fattened on
pilu, shami and ingudi.

"The Govasana brahmins and the Dasamiyas, every one of them, fortunate men, came
to please the great-souled Dharma King. They stand at the door with a tribute of
three kharvas and are kept back. They bring fine golden water pots, and only
when they have given their tribute are they let in.

"A hundred thousand slave girls from the land of the Karpasikas, dark, slender
and long-haired, decked with gold ornaments, were brought. The Shudras brought
skins of the ranku deer, fit for the best of brahmins. The people of Bharukaccha
brought a full tribute, great king, and horses bred in Gandhara.

"There are men who live on grain watered by the rain god and by the mouths of
rivers. They were born in the sea gardens and beyond the Sindhu. They are the
Vairamas, the Paradas, the Vangas and the Kitavas. They bring tribute of every
kind and jewels of every kind: goats and sheep, cows and gold, donkeys and
camels, liquor made from fruit, and blankets of every kind. They stand at the
door and are kept back.

"The king of Pragjyotisha, the hero Bhagadatta, the mighty lord of the mlecchas,
the great chariot fighter, came with the Yavanas. He brought thoroughbred
horses, swift as the wind, and a full tribute, and he stands at the door and is
kept back. Bhagadatta of Pragjyotisha gave vessels of iron and swords with hilts
of pure ivory, and then he went away.

"I saw men with two eyes, men with three eyes, men with an eye in the forehead,
come from every quarter. There were men in turbans and men without clothes, the
Bahukas, the man-eaters, and the one-footed men. I saw them kept back at the
door, giving him gold and silver in plenty as tribute. The one-footed men gave
him horses as swift as thought, red as the indragopa beetle, or the colour of
parrots, or like the rainbow, or like clouds at twilight. They were horses from
the wild, of many colours. And they gave priceless gold.

"The Chinas, the Hunas, the Shakas and the Odras, and those who live among the
mountains, the Varshneyas, the Harahunas, and the dark men of the Himalaya came.
I cannot count them all, kept back at the door, men of every kind, coming again
and again to give him tribute. They brought ten thousand black-necked, great
donkeys that could run a hundred leagues, well trained and famous everywhere.

"There was cloth from Bahli and from China, rich in size, colour and feel:
woollens and cloth of ranku hair, silk from worms and woven silk. There was
felted cloth by the thousand, the colour of lotuses, smooth cloth that was not
cotton, sheep's wool, and soft skins. There were sharp long swords, spears,
darts and battle-axes, and sharp axes made on the western coast. There were
essences and perfumes of every kind, and jewels by the thousand. They bring a
full tribute, and they stand at the door and are kept back.

"The Shakas, the Tukharas and the Kankas, hairy men and horned men, brought
horses that could go far and fast, counted by the arbuda. They brought gold by
the koti, many times over, measured by the padma. They bring tribute of every
kind, and they stand at the door and are kept back.

"The king of the eastern country gave costly seats, carriages and beds, worked
with jewels and gold and ivory. He gave chariots of every shape, worked with
gold, with trained horses and coverings of tiger skin. He gave bright blankets,
jewels by the thousand, iron arrows, half-arrows and weapons of every kind. When
he had given this great wealth, he entered the Pandava's hall of sacrifice.

"Hear me tell of the gifts of every kind, sinless one, the great heap of wealth
the kings gave him for the sacrifice.

"There are those who live along the river Shailoda, between Meru and Mandara,
and enjoy the lovely shade of the kichaka bamboos. They are the Khashas, the
Ekashanas, the Ajyohas, the Pradaras, the Dirghavenus, the Pashupas, the
Kunindas, the Tanganas and the far Tanganas. Those kings brought the gold called
ant gold, given as a gift by the ants, measured by the drona, in heaps. The
mountain people brought yak tails, black and white, some white as the moon, and
plenty of sweet honey from the Himalayan flowers. They brought garlands the
waters had carried down from the Northern Kurus, and potent herbs from north of
Kailasa. They brought other tribute too, and they stand bowing, kept back at the
door of King Ajatashatru.

"There are kings beyond the Himalaya, on the mountain where the sun rises, at
the end of the sea of Varishena, and all around Lohitya. There are Kiratas who
live on fruit and roots and dress in skins. They brought loads of sandalwood,
aloe wood and kaliyaka, heaps of skins, jewels, gold and perfumes, and ten
thousand Kirata slave girls, lord of the people. They brought lovely things,
beasts and birds from far away, and gleaming gold gathered from the mountains.
They bring a full tribute, and they stand at the door and are kept back.

"The Kayavyas, the Daradas, the Darvas, the Shuras, the Vaiyamakas, the
Audumbaras, the Durvibhagas, and the Paradas with the Bahlikas came. So did the
Kashmiris, the Kundamanas, the Paurakas and the Hamsakayanas, the Shibis, the
Trigartas and the Yaudheyas, and the princes of Madra and Kekaya. So did the
Ambashthas, the Kaukuras, the Tarkshyas, the Vastrapas with the Pahlavas, the
Vasatis with the Mauleyas, with the Kshudrakas and Malavas. So did the
Shaundikas, the Kukkuras and the Shakas, lord of the people, the Angas, the
Vangas and the Pundras, the Shanavatyas and the Gayas. These kshatriyas, well
born, in their companies, the best of men, weapons in hand, brought wealth to
Ajatashatru by the hundred.

"The Vangas, the lords of Kalinga, and the Tamraliptas with the Pundrakas
brought fine cloth, silk, cloth of leaf fibre, and cloaks. There the doorkeepers
told them, at the king's command, 'Pay your tax and bring a good tribute, and
then you will be let in at the door.' Then each of them gave a thousand
elephants, with tusks like chariot poles and girths of gold, the colour of
lotuses, hung with blankets. They were like mountains, always in rut, from all
around Lake Kamyaka, armoured, patient and of good breed. Then they entered by
the door.

"These and many other companies came from every quarter, and other great men
brought jewels here as well. The gandharva king called Chitraratha, Vasava's
follower, gave four hundred horses as swift as the wind. The gandharva Tumburu
gladly gave a hundred horses, the colour of mango leaves, with garlands of gold.
Kritin, king of the Shukaras, Kauravya, lord of the people, gave many hundreds
of fine elephants. Virata of Matsya brought as tribute two thousand elephants in
rut, with garlands of gold. King Vasudana, from Pamsurashtra, brought twenty-six
elephants, king, and two thousand horses with garlands of gold, swift and strong
and in their prime, lord of men. He gave the Pandavas a full tribute. Yajnasena
gave fourteen thousand slave girls, lord of the people, and ten thousand slaves
with their wives, and twenty-six chariots yoked with elephants, great king. And
he gave his whole kingdom to the Parthas for the sacrifice.

"The Simhalas brought beryl, the treasure of the sea, pearls and conches, and
blankets by the hundred. Their men, dark, with eyes red at the corners, dressed
in cloth worked with jewels, took these and stand at the door, kept back. The
brahmins came to please him, and the conquered kshatriyas, and the vaishyas, and
the shudras eager to serve. They all came to Yudhishthira out of love and deep
respect. All the mlecchas and all the classes, the first, the middle and the
last born, came from many lands and many peoples. It seemed the whole world had
been turned over into Yudhishthira's house. I saw the gifts of every kind, high
and low, that the kings sent to my enemies, and in my pain I wish today that I
were dead.

"I will tell you of the Pandavas' dependants, Bharata, for whom Yudhishthira
provides raw food and cooked. There are elephant riders and horsemen, three
padmas and ten thousand, an arbuda of chariots, and foot soldiers in plenty. In
one place food was being measured out, in another begun, in another cooked, in
another served, and everywhere there was the sound of blessings for the day. In
Yudhishthira's house I saw no one of any class who had not eaten, or was not
glad, or went hungry in any way.

"Eighty-eight thousand snatakas, brahmin householders, each with thirty slave
girls, are kept by Yudhishthira. They are well pleased and content, and they
pray for the destruction of his enemies. Ten thousand others, ascetics who keep
their seed drawn up, eat from golden plates in Yudhishthira's house. Yajnaseni,
without eating herself, looked to everyone, lord of the people, to see who had
eaten and who had not. She saw what was done and what was not, down to the
hunchbacks and the dwarfs. Only two, Bharata, did not pay tax to Kunti's son:
the Panchalas, because of the marriage, and the Andhakas and Vrishnis, because
of their friendship.

---

"There are noble kings, true to their word, keeping great vows, men of full
learning and eloquent, who have bathed at the end of their Vedic study. They are
steadfast and modest, righteous and famous, consecrated kings, and they wait on
him. I saw, here and there, many thousands of cows from the forest, with bronze
milking pails, brought by the kings as fees for the priests. The kings
themselves, with care, eagerly brought and held up vessels of every kind for the
consecration, Bharata.

"The Bahlika brought a chariot adorned with gold. Sudakshina yoked to it white
horses from Kamboja. Sunitha, the famous lord of the Chedis, brought its
matchless axle support, and quickly brought and raised its banner himself. The
southern king brought the armour, the Magadhan the garland and the turban. The
great archer Vasudana brought a great elephant sixty years old. The Matsya fixed
the axles, and Ekalavya brought the shoes. The king of Avanti brought water of
many kinds for the consecration. Chekitana brought the quiver, the king of Kashi
the bow, and Shalya a sword with a hilt of gold and a sling adorned with gold.

"Then Dhaumya and Vyasa, of great austerity, consecrated him. With Narada and
the sage Asita Devala at their head, the great seers stood by the consecration,
glad at heart. With Jamadagnya, other masters of the Veda came to the
great-souled king of the rich fees, with sacred words. They came as the seven
seers come to great Indra, the king of the gods in heaven. Satyaki, whose
courage is true, held the umbrella over him, and Dhananjaya and Bhimasena the
Pandava held the fans.

"The Ocean brought him the conch of Varuna that Prajapati once held for Indra in
an earlier age. It was made by Vishvakarman and set with a thousand coins of
gold. Krishna consecrated him with it, and at that I fainted. They go to the
eastern ocean and the western, and to the southern. But to the northern, my
father, no one goes but the birds. There they blew conches by the hundred for
good fortune, and at their sound my hair stood on end. The kings bowed, stripped
of their own glory, and fell down. Dhrishtadyumna, the Pandavas, Satyaki and
Keshava, the eighth, steady and brave and fond of one another, saw the kings
senseless, and me, and they laughed. Then Bibhatsu, delighted, gave five hundred
oxen with horns of gold to the chief brahmins, Bharata.

"Not Shambara's killer, nor Yauvanashva, nor Manu, nor King Prithu, son of Vena,
nor Bhagiratha had fortune so beyond measure as Kaunteya has. He has performed
the Rajasuya, like lord Harishchandra. I have seen this fortune in Partha, lord,
as it was in Harishchandra. How can you think it better for me to live, Bharata?
The yoke is tied on as if by a blind man, and everything is upside down, lord of
men. The younger grow, and the elders decline, Bharata.

"*I have seen all this, and I find no peace, though I look for it, hero of the
Kurus. That is why I have grown so thin, and pale, and full of grief.*"

**The CE's own doublets.** This is the second telling of Duryodhana's complaint (see chapter 15,
2.45). Janamejaya asks again, and Vaishampayana says "once more" (2.46.5). Several details
repeat, sometimes word for word: the snatakas (2.45.17 and 2.48.39), the brahmins at the door
(2.45.24–25 and 2.47.5–6), and the oceans no one reaches but the birds (2.45.28–29 and
2.49.16). Both tellings are kept.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi's laughter.** Here, in Duryodhana's own account, Krishna and Partha laugh, and
  "Draupadi with the women" (2.46.30). In the narrator's account in chapter 14 (2.43.6–9) she is
  not named. Nobody in either account says "a blind man's son is blind". See ledger L-17.
* **Who laughed at the door.** In the CE the twins hold Duryodhana and Sahadeva shows him the
  door (2.46.33–34). The vulgate adds the women laughing (\*457), and Bhima laughing and calling
  him "son of Dhritarashtra" (\*458). Not in the CE. Ganguli has them.
* The vulgate adds that Dhritarashtra offered Duryodhana half the Kuru kingdom (\*451), and
  Duryodhana's threat to enter the fire or the sea (\*459). Not in the CE.
* The vulgate adds more tribute: the king of the Simhalas with sandalwood (App. I No. 34),
  Dravidas, Pandyas and Cholas (\*470), the Bahlika's horses (\*476), and Krishna's fourteen
  thousand elephants (\*477). Not in the CE.
