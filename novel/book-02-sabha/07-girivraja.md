# Girivraja

"Hamsa and Dibhaka have fallen," said Vasudeva, "and Kamsa and his ministers
have been struck down. The time has come for Jarasandha's death. He cannot be
beaten in battle even by all the gods and asuras together. We understand that he
must be beaten in a fight with bare hands, life against life. In me there is
policy, in Bhima strength, and Arjuna will guard us both. We three will overcome
him, king, like three fires.

"When the three of us come upon him alone, that king will surely choose to fight
one of us. There is no doubt of it. Out of pride before the world, and because
he is so strong, he will surely choose to fight with Bhimasena. Strong-armed,
mighty Bhimasena is enough to be his death, as Death is enough for a world grown
insolent. If you know my heart, and if you trust me, give me Bhimasena and
Arjuna at once, as a pledge."

When the blessed one said this, Yudhishthira looked at Bhima and Partha,
standing there with glad faces, and answered.

"Unfallen one, unfallen one, do not speak like that, tamer of enemies. You are
the Pandavas' protector, and we depend on you. What you say, Govinda, is all
fitting. For you do not lead those from whom fortune has turned away. Jarasandha
is as good as dead, the kings are as good as free, and I have won my Rajasuya,
while I stand at your command. Act quickly, as you see fit, best of men, so that
this task is done: it is my task, and the world's. I cannot bear to live without
the three of you, like a sick man without dharma, pleasure or wealth. Partha is
nothing without Shauri, and Shauri nothing without the Pandava. I think there is
nothing in the world the two Krishnas cannot conquer. And this glorious
Vrikodara is the best of the strong. With you two beside him, what could the
famous hero not do?

"A great force well led does the finest work. They say force is blind and dull,
and must be led by the wise. Water is led to where the ground is low, and the
wise lead force to where there is a gap. So let us rely on Govinda, famous in
the world, who knows the ways of policy, and strive to get the task done. For a
task to succeed one should put first Krishna, who has wisdom, policy and
strength, and the means and ways to act. So, for the task to succeed, let Partha
Arjuna follow Krishna, the best of the Yadus, and let Bhima follow Dhananjaya.
Policy, victory and strength will succeed in this venture."

---

When he had said this, the three brothers of great energy, the Varshneya and the
two sons of Pandu, set out for Magadha. They put on the dress of brahmin
graduates, splendid men, and their friends sent them off with kind words.
Burning with anger for the sake of their kin, in the finest clothes, with bodies
like the sun, the moon and fire, they looked terrible. When he saw the two
Krishnas, unconquered in battle, with Bhima going first, all set on one purpose,
he thought Jarasandha already dead. For those two great men are lords in the
carrying out of every task, and in restraining acts of dharma, wealth and
pleasure.

They set out from the Kurus through the middle of Kurujangala, went to the
lovely Lotus Lake, and passed Kalakuta. They came in turn to the Gandaki, the
Shona, the Sadanira and the rivers of Ekaparvataka, and went on. They crossed
the lovely Sarayu and saw the eastern Kosalas. They passed Mithila, the Mala and
the river Charmanvati. All three crossed the Ganga and the Shona, facing east,
and the unfallen ones came to the land of Magadha, Kuravorashchada. Reaching the
hill Goratha, always crowded with cattle, well watered and with fine trees, they
saw the city of Magadha.

"Here, Partha, is the great, fair settlement of Magadha," said Vasudeva, "always
well watered, rich in cattle, free of sickness, full of fine houses. The great
hill Vaihara, and Varaha, Vrishabha, Rishigiri and the fair Chaityaka as the
fifth, my son, these five great-peaked hills, cool with their trees, stand close
together as if to guard Girivraja. They seem hidden by forests of lodhra, with
their branch-tips wrapped in flowers, fragrant and lovely, dear to lovers.

"Here the great Gautama, strict in his vows, fathered Kakshivat and his other
sons on a shudra woman of Ushinara. Because of that dwelling, Gautama lives in
that house and serves the Magadhan line, by the kings' favour. Long ago, Arjuna,
the mighty kings of Anga, Vanga and the rest came to Gautama's house and took
their pleasure there. Look at these lovely rows of piyal trees, Partha, and the
fair lodhras near Gautama's dwelling. Here are the snakes Arbuda and Shakravapi,
tamers of enemies, and the fine home of the snake Svastika and of the snake
Mani. Because of Mani, Magadha is never left by the clouds. Kaushika and Maniman
have shown it favour. Jarasandha thinks his success will never leave him. But
when we reach him today, we will destroy his pride."

When he had said this, the three brothers of great energy, the Varshneya and the
two sons of Pandu, set out for the city of Magadha. They came to Girivraja,
unassailable, full of content and well-fed people of all four classes, with its
festivals in full swing.

They did not go to the city gate. They went to the tall hill that the
Barhadrathas and the townspeople worship. There Brihadratha had killed a bull
that ate beans. He killed it and had three drums made, covered them with its
hide, and set them up in his city, where they would sound, sprinkled with
heavenly flowers. The three rushed at the lovely peak of the Magadhans,
Chaityaka, as if they meant to strike at Jarasandha's head, wanting to kill him.
It was a firm, broad, very great and ancient peak, always honoured with garlands
and well founded. The heroes struck it with their great arms and threw it down.
Then, seeing the city of Magadha, they went in.

At that very time the priests were honouring Jarasandha, carrying fire round the
king as he sat on an elephant. Keeping the vow of graduates, with their arms as
their only weapons and no other, they went in, Bharata, wanting to fight
Jarasandha. They saw the finest wealth of the stalls of food and garlands,
flourishing, perfect in every way, rich in everything that could be wished. When
they had seen that wealth on the street, those best of men, Krishna, Bhima and
Dhananjaya, went along the royal road, and the mighty men took garlands from the
garland makers by force. Wearing coloured clothes, all of them garlanded, their
earrings gleaming, they came to the house of wise Jarasandha, looking at it as
lions of the Himalaya look at a cowshed. The arms of those strong-armed men,
like pillars of rock, smeared with sandal and aloe, looked splendid, great king.
When the Magadhans saw them, like elephants, tall as sal trees, broad-chested,
they were amazed.

The mighty bulls among men passed through three crowded courtyards, and came
proudly to the king. Jarasandha rose to meet them as the rules require, for they
deserved water for their feet and the honey mixture, and deserved respect, and
had come to be honoured. The lord and king said to them, "Welcome." For this vow
of his was famous on earth, king: when the victor in battle heard that brahmin
graduates had come, he would go out to meet them even at midnight, Bharata. When
he saw them in their strange dress, the best of kings went up to them and was
amazed.

As soon as they saw King Jarasandha, those bulls among men, the slayers of
enemies, all said, best of the Bharatas, "Hail and good health, king." They
stood there, tiger among kings, and looked at the king and at one another. Then
Jarasandha said to the Yadava and the Pandavas, disguised as brahmins, "Please
be seated, king of kings." Then those three bulls among men sat down, blazing
with glory, like the three fires at a great sacrifice.

King Jarasandha, true to his word, spoke to them, joy of the Kurus, reproaching
them for their disguise.

"I know very well that in this world of men brahmins who keep the graduate's vow
do not wear garlands and unguents outside. You are wearing flowers, and your
arms have the marks of the bowstring. You carry a kshatriya's power, and you
claim to be brahmins. Dressed in coloured clothes like this, with garlands and
unguents outside, tell the truth: who are you? Truth becomes kings. Why have you
broken the peak of Chaityaka and come into our house by no gate, without fear of
offending a king? What is this deed of yours, that does not match your dress?
What is it you have in mind today? Speak. A brahmin's strength lies above all in
his speech. And why, when you have come to me like this, do you not accept the
honour I offered as the rules require? What is your business in coming to us?"

When he said this, the great-minded Krishna, skilled in speech, answered in a
smooth, deep voice.

"Brahmins, kshatriyas and vaishyas may all keep the graduate's vow, king. Some
of its rules are special to each, and some are shared. A kshatriya who keeps the
special rules always wins glory. Glory is sure to be found in those who wear
flowers, so we wear flowers. A kshatriya's strength is in his arms, and not so
much in his speech. That is why his speech is said to be modest, son of
Brihadratha. The Creator put the kshatriyas' strength in their arms. If you want
to see it, king, you will see it today, without a doubt. The good always enter
an enemy's house by no gate, and a friend's house by the gate. That is why we
did not use the gate. And we have come on business, from an enemy, and we do not
accept honour from him. Know that this is our everlasting vow."

---

"I cannot remember ever doing you any wrong," said Jarasandha, "and when I think
about it, I cannot see what harm I have done you. If I have done no harm, how
can you think me your enemy, when I am innocent? Tell me, brahmins, for that is
the way of good men. When dharma is harmed, the mind is troubled. Any kshatriya
who attacks an innocent man does so, without a doubt. A man who knows dharma and
keeps great vows, and acts otherwise in the world, goes the way of the crooked
and destroys his own good. In the three worlds, the kshatriya's dharma is the
best for those who live rightly. You know I am innocent, and yet you talk as if
without thinking."

"There is a man of a family," said Vasudeva, "who bears the family's burden,
great king, and it is at his bidding that we three have risen up. You have
carried off the kshatriyas of the world, king. You have done a cruel wrong, and
do you think yourself innocent? How can a king harm good kings, best of kings?
Yet you have seized kings and mean to offer them to Rudra. That sin you have
done, son of Brihadratha, would fall on us too. For we keep to dharma, and we
are able to protect it. The sacrifice of men has never been seen. How can you
want to worship the god Shankara with men? Who else but you, Jarasandha, in his
folly, would treat men of his own class as beasts for sacrifice? We follow those
in distress, and we have come here to restrain you, who destroy our kin, for the
good of our kin.

"You think there is no man among the kshatriyas but you, king. That is a very
great delusion. What kshatriya who knows his birth would not go to the heaven
beyond compare, unfading, that comes after battle? Kshatriyas who are
consecrated for the sacrifices of battle look to heaven and win the heavenly
worlds by that sacrifice. Know that, lord of Magadha. Victory is the source of
heaven, king. Great fame is the source of heaven. Austerity in battle is the
source of heaven. That path never fails. This is Indra's quality of victory,
always ready, by which Shatakratu defeated the asuras and protects the world.
Who that looks to heaven would be at war as you are, proud of your great
Magadhan armies and the strength of your numbers? Do not despise others, king.
There is courage in every man. Your power is only equal to others', lord of men.
As long as this is not understood, it will seem yours. But we can bear it. That
is why I tell you, king: put down your pride and arrogance among your equals,
Magadhan. Do not go to the house of Yama with your sons, your ministers and your
army.

"Dambhodbhava, Kartavirya, Uttara and Brihadratha scorned those who were better,
and perished with their armies. We want to free the kings from you, and we are
not brahmins in name only. I am Shauri, Hrishikesha, and these two heroes are
Pandavas. We challenge you, king. Stand firm and fight, Magadhan. Or let all the
kings go, and do not go to the house of Yama."

"I never take kings I have not conquered," said Jarasandha. "Who here has stood
against me and not been conquered? This, they say, is the kshatriya's lawful
living, Krishna: to overcome by courage, bring under his power, and do as he
pleases. I have gathered the kings for the god, Krishna. How can I let them go
today out of fear, remembering the vow of a kshatriya? I will fight army against
army drawn up in array, or one against one, or against two or three, together or
separately."

When he had said this, King Jarasandha, wanting to fight those men of terrible
deeds, ordered his son Sahadeva to be consecrated. When the battle was at hand,
bull of the Bharatas, the king thought of his two generals, Kaushika and
Chitrasena, whose names were spoken among men as Hamsa and Dibhaka, honoured in
the world.

But the lord Shauri was true to his word, the unfallen one, the best of the
self-controlled, Madhusudana, younger brother of the plough-bearer. He
remembered that King Jarasandha, the strongest of the strong, tiger among men,
with the courage of a tiger and fearsome strength on earth, had been marked out
as another's share to kill. Honouring the command of Brahma, he did not wish to
kill him himself.

**Divergences.**

* The vulgate has Jarasandha refuse to fight Krishna as a coward who ran from Mathura to the sea
  (\*241, \*242), and dismiss Arjuna as a boy (\*244, \*245). In the CE Jarasandha chooses Bhima
  without comment (2.21.3).
* The vulgate adds Krishna naming himself as Jarasandha's enemy, the Pandavas' cousin (\*239), and
  a long reply from Jarasandha about Kamsa (App. I No. 5). Not in the CE.
