# Shakuni's Plan

Shakuni, the son of Subala, had been at King Yudhishthira's great sacrifice, the
Rajasuya, with the son of Gandhari. He knew already what Duryodhana wanted, and
he liked to please him. When he had heard what Duryodhana had to say, Shakuni
went to King Dhritarashtra, the lord of the people. The wise king, whose eyes
were his wisdom, was sitting there, and Shakuni spoke to him.

"Great king, Duryodhana is pale, yellow and thin, wretched and lost in brooding.
Know that, bull of the Bharatas. You do not look into it properly. It is
something he cannot bear, and it comes from his enemy. Why do you not see your
eldest son's grief?"

"Duryodhana, what is the cause of your great distress, my son?" said
Dhritarashtra. "If it is something I may hear, tell me, joy of the Kurus.
Shakuni here tells me you are pale, yellow and thin. I think about it, and I
cannot see where your grief comes from. For all my great power is in your hands,
my son. Your brothers and your friends never do anything you dislike. You wear
fine cloaks, and you eat meat and rice. Thoroughbred horses carry you. Why are
you yellow and thin? You have costly beds and lovely women, fine houses and
pleasures as you please. Everything is yours for a word, like the gods', no
doubt of it. You are hard to overcome. Why do you grieve like a wretched man, my
son?"

"I eat and I dress like a coward," said Duryodhana, "and I carry a fierce
resentment, and wait for the times to turn. A man cannot bear it when his own
people are overpowered and in enemy hands. He wants to free them from the
troubles that enemies bring. That is the one who is called a man. Contentment
kills fortune, Bharata, and so does pride, and so do pity and fear. A man who is
wrapped in these does not win anything great.

"What I eat does me no good, when I have seen the fortune of Yudhishthira,
Kunti's son, blazing as it were. It takes the colour from my face. I see my
rivals prosper and myself decline. I cannot see that fortune in Kunti's son, and
yet I seem to see it standing over me. That is why I am pale, wretched, yellow
and thin.

"Yudhishthira keeps eighty-eight thousand snatakas, brahmin householders, each
with thirty slave girls. Ten thousand others eat the finest food every day, from
golden plates, in Yudhishthira's house. The king of the Kambojas sent him skins
of the kadali deer, black, dark and red, and priceless blankets. Chariots,
women, cows and horses in their hundreds and thousands, and three thousand
she-camels, roam there. The kings brought the son of Kunti jewels of every kind,
in plenty, lord of the earth, at that greatest of sacrifices. I have never seen
or heard of such a flood of wealth as came to the wise son of Pandu at his
sacrifice. I have seen my enemy's endless flood of wealth, king, and I find no
peace. I think about it night and day, lord.

"Brahmins and Vatadhanas, rich in cattle, in companies of hundreds, stand at the
door with a tribute of three kharvas and are kept back. They bring fine golden
water pots, and only when they have given their tribute are they let in. The
Ocean brought him, in a bronze vessel of Varuna's, honey such as the immortal
women do not keep even for Shakra. There was a sling of a thousand pieces of
gold, set with many jewels. When I saw it all, it was like a fever to me.

"They take it and go to the eastern and southern oceans, bull of the Bharatas,
and they take it to the western ocean too. But to the northern ocean, my father,
no one goes but the birds. And there was a wonder there. Listen while I tell
you. When a hundred thousand brahmins had been served, a sign had been set: a
conch was blown each time. I heard that conch sound again and again, a splendid
sound, Bharata, and it made my hair stand on end. The courtyard was crowded with
kings who had come to watch, lord of men, and the kings had brought all their
jewels. At the sacrifice of Pandu's wise son, great king, the lords of the earth
served the brahmins like vaishyas. Not the king of the gods, not Yama or Varuna,
not the lord of the guhyakas has such fortune, king, as Yudhishthira has. I have
seen that highest fortune of Pandu's son, and my heart is burning, and I cannot
find peace."

"Listen to how you may win that highest fortune you have seen in the Pandava,
you whose courage is true," said Shakuni. "I am known on earth for my skill at
dice, Bharata. I know the heart of the game, I know the stakes, and I know the
fine points of play. Kunti's son loves dice, and does not know how to play. If
he is challenged, he will surely come. Challenge him. Say, 'Let us play.'"

When Shakuni said this, King Duryodhana at once said to Dhritarashtra, "This man
knows dice, king, and he is ready to take the Pandava's fortune by gaming. You
should give him leave."

"The steward, my wise counsellor, is the one whose guidance I follow," said
Dhritarashtra. "I will meet him and learn his judgment on this business. For he
looks far ahead and puts dharma first. He will tell us what is truly good and
fitting for both sides, and what should be decided."

"If the steward comes, he will turn you back," said Duryodhana. "And if you turn
back, king of kings, I will die, no doubt of it. When I am dead, king, be happy
with Vidura. You will enjoy the whole earth. What will you do with me?"

When Dhritarashtra heard his son's pained words, spoken out of love, he took
Duryodhana's side. He said to his servants, "Let craftsmen quickly build me a
great hall, lovely and fine to look at, with a thousand pillars and a hundred
doors. Then spread it with jewels, set out dice everywhere, and when it is well
made and easy to enter, tell me quietly."

When he had made up his mind to do this to calm Duryodhana, great king, King
Dhritarashtra sent for Vidura. For he never decided anything without asking
Vidura. He knew the evils of gaming, but love for his son drew him on.

When wise Vidura heard of it, he knew that the door of Kali had opened, the
mouth of destruction, and he hurried to Dhritarashtra. The brother came to his
great elder brother, bowed his head to his feet, and said, "I do not welcome
this plan of yours, king, my lord. Act so that there is no quarrel among your
sons because of the dice."

"Steward, there will be no quarrel among my sons," said Dhritarashtra. "The gods
in heaven will be kind to us, no doubt of it. Whether it brings good or ill,
harm or help, let this friendly game go ahead. It is fated, no doubt of it. With
me here, and Bhishma, bull of the Bharatas, no wrong that fate might bring can
happen in any way. Mount a chariot drawn by horses as swift as the wind, and go
to Khandavaprastha today, and bring Yudhishthira here. My mind is made up,
Vidura, and it is not to be stopped. I tell you this. I think fate is
everything, and it is fate that brings this about."

When he was told this, wise Vidura thought, "This is not to be." In deep sorrow
he went to Bhishma, the wise son of the river.

**The CE's own doublets.** From 2.46 on the CE tells this part a second time. Janamejaya asks
again, Vaishampayana starts again, and Duryodhana complains to his father at greater length
(2.46–49). Some details of this chapter come back there: the snatakas, the brahmins at the
door, the conch, and the oceans no one reaches but the birds. Both tellings are kept (chapters 15
and 16).

**Divergences.**

* **Loaded dice.** The CE's Shakuni says only that he knows the game (2.45.37). The vulgate's
  line that he will win "by cheating" is \*448. Not in the CE. See ledger L-18.
* 2.45.27–29: the vulgate adds that Vasudeva consecrated Yudhishthira with a fine conch (\*442),
  and that rakshasas carried jars to the oceans for water (\*443). Its line that Arjuna went
  north and brought back tribute is \*444. Not in the CE, which leaves "they take it" without
  saying who or what. Ganguli translates the vulgate here.

**Choices.**

* 2.45.2 *prajñācakṣuṣam*: "whose eyes were his wisdom", the usual epithet of the blind king.
* 2.45.13: the syntax is difficult. Ganguli: "who … liveth having vanquished that foe with the
  desire of liberating his own subjects". The sense is uncertain.
* 2.45.16 *adṛśyām api … sthitāṃ paśyann ivodyatām*: "I cannot see that fortune … and yet I seem
  to see it standing over me."
* 2.45.20 *triṃśataṃ … śatāni*: "three thousand" (thirty hundred).
* 2.45.24 *vāṭadhānāḥ*: taken as the Vatadhana people. Ganguli reads "supported by grants".
  *traikharvam*: "three kharvas", a very large number, kept as a word.
* 2.45.26 *kalaśodadhiḥ*: "the Ocean". The verse is obscure.
* 2.45.27 *śaikyam*: "a sling", a hanging holder for vessels.
* 2.45.39 *apadāntaram*: "at once".
* 2.45.49: "He knew the evils of gaming, but love for his son drew him on", as the text says.
* 2.45.58 *naitad astīti cintayan*: "thought, 'This is not to be.'" Ganguli: "concluding that
  his race was doomed". The sense is uncertain.
