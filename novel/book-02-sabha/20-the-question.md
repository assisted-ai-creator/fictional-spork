# The Question

"There are harlots in the land, Yudhishthira, who belong to gamblers," said
Bhima. "Yet they do not stake even them. They have pity even on them. The
tribute the king of Kashi brought, and all the other riches, and the jewels the
other lords of the earth brought, are gone. Our mounts, our wealth, our armour
and our weapons, the kingdom, you yourself and we too: our enemies have taken it
all by trickery. Even then I did not grow angry, for you are master of
everything we have. But I think this goes too far, that Draupadi is staked. She
does not deserve this. The girl married the Pandavas, and now, because of you,
she is tormented by the Kauravas, who are mean and cruel and love deceit. For
her sake, king, this anger of mine falls on you. I will burn your arms.
Sahadeva, bring fire."

"Bhimasena, you have never spoken words like these before," said Arjuna. "Surely
our cruel enemies have destroyed your respect for dharma. Do not give our
enemies what they want. Practise the highest dharma. No one should go against
his righteous elder brother. The king was challenged by our enemies, and he
remembered the dharma of the kshatriyas, and played at the enemy's will. That
brings us great fame."

"If I did not know that he had done it in this way, Dhananjaya," said Bhimasena,
"I would have held his two arms together. I would have burned them in the
blazing fire, as if by force."

---

When he saw the Pandavas so distressed, and the princess of Panchala tormented,
Vikarna, the son of Dhritarashtra, said this.

"Answer the question Yajnaseni has asked, kings. If we do not answer it, hell
awaits us at once. Bhishma and Dhritarashtra, the two eldest of the Kurus, have
come together here and said nothing, and nor has the wise Vidura. Bharadvaja's
son, the teacher of us all, and Kripa have not answered the question either,
best of the twice-born though they are. But let the other lords of the earth,
who have gathered here from every quarter, put aside desire and anger, and speak
as they think. Think about what the good Draupadi has asked again and again,
kings, and say which side you take."

So he spoke to all the men in the hall, many times. But the lords of the earth
did not answer him a word, good or bad. When Vikarna had spoken like this to all
those kings again and again, he wrung his hands and sighed.

"Answer, lords of the earth, or do not answer at all," he said. "I will tell
you, Kauravas, what I think is right here. The best of men say there are four
vices of kings: hunting, drinking, dice, and too much love of pleasure. A man
who is given to these lives with dharma cast aside. And people do not count an
act done by a man in that state. This son of Pandu was deep in a vice when the
gamblers challenged him, and he staked Draupadi. She is blameless, and she
belongs to all the Pandavas alike. And the Pandava staked her after he had
already been won himself. And it was the son of Subala who named Krishna,
because he wanted a stake. When I weigh all this, I do not think she has been
won."

When they heard this, a great roar rose from the men in the hall. They praised
Vikarna and cursed the son of Subala. When the noise had died down, the son of
Radha, beside himself with anger, raised his fine arm and said this.

"Many perversities are seen in Vikarna. Born of the family, he will destroy it,
like the fire born of the fire-sticks. These men said nothing, even when Krishna
pressed them. I think they believe Drupada's daughter was won lawfully. You are
bursting with sheer childishness, son of Dhritarashtra, when you, a child, say
in the hall what the elders should say. You do not truly know dharma, you
younger brother of Duryodhana, you dull fool, when you say that Krishna, who was
won, was not won.

"How can you think Krishna was not won, son of Dhritarashtra, when the eldest of
the Pandavas staked everything he had in the hall? Draupadi is part of
everything he had, bull of the Bharatas. So how can you think Krishna was not
won lawfully? Draupadi was named aloud, and the Pandavas allowed it. For what
reason do you think she was not won?

"Or if you think she was brought into the hall in one garment against dharma,
hear my answer to that too. The gods ordained one husband for a woman, joy of
the Kurus. She submits to many, and so she is plainly a whore. To bring her into
the hall is nothing strange, I think, whether in one garment or naked. Whatever
wealth they have, and she, and the Pandavas themselves, the son of Subala has
won all this wealth here lawfully. Duhshasana, this Vikarna is a mere boy who
talks like a wise man. Take off the clothes of the Pandavas, and of Draupadi
too."

When they heard this, all the Pandavas threw off their upper garments, Bharata,
and sat down in the hall. Then, king, Duhshasana seized Draupadi's garment by
force, in the middle of the hall, and began to strip it off. But as Draupadi's
garment was pulled off, lord of the people, another garment of the same kind
appeared, again and again.

Then a terrible uproar went up from all the kings there, as they watched the
greatest wonder in the world. And there, in the midst of the kings, Bhima swore
an oath in a great voice, his lips quivering with anger, wringing his hands.

"Hear these words of mine, kshatriyas of the world, words no man has ever spoken
before, and no other man will ever speak. If I say this and do not do it, lords
of the earth, may I never go the way of all my forefathers. In battle I will
break open the chest of this wicked, low-born outcast of the Bharatas by force,
and drink his blood."

When they heard his words, which thrilled everyone who heard them, they honoured
him greatly and cursed the son of Dhritarashtra. When a heap of clothes had
piled up in the middle of the hall, Duhshasana, tired and ashamed, sat down.

Then a hair-raising cry of "Shame!" went up there from the men in the hall, the
gods among men, as they looked at the sons of Kunti. "The Kauravas will not
answer this question." So the people cried out there, and blamed Dhritarashtra.

---

Then Vidura, who knew all of dharma, raised his arms, quieted the men in the
hall, and said this.

"Draupadi has asked her question, and now she weeps as if she had no one to
protect her. And you in the hall do not answer the question. Dharma is being
wronged here. A man in distress comes to the hall like a blazing fire, and the
men in the hall calm him with truth and dharma. When a man in distress puts a
question of dharma to the men in the hall, they should answer it, rising above
desire and anger. Vikarna has answered the question as well as he knows how,
kings. You too should answer it as you think. A man who knows dharma, who comes
to the hall and does not answer a question, earns half the fruit of a lie. And a
man who knows dharma, who comes to the hall and answers falsely, earns the whole
fruit of a lie. That is certain.

"They tell an old story about this, the talk between Prahlada and the sage, the
son of Angiras. Prahlada was a king of the daityas, and his son was Virochana.
For the sake of a girl, Virochana went against Sudhanvan, the son of Angiras. 'I
am the better man.' 'No, I am the better man.' So they said, each wanting the
girl, and they staked their lives on it, so we have heard. When they quarrelled
over the question, they asked Prahlada. 'Which of us two is the better? Answer
the question, and do not lie.'

"Prahlada was afraid of the quarrel, and he looked at Sudhanvan. Sudhanvan,
blazing with anger like a brahmin's staff, said to him, 'If you speak falsely,
Prahlada, or if you do not speak, the thunderbolt-bearer will strike your head
with his thunderbolt. He will split it into a hundred pieces.' When Sudhanvan
said this, the daitya trembled like a leaf of the ashvattha tree. He went to ask
Kashyapa, of great power.

"'You know the dharma of the gods, of the asuras and of the brahmins,' said
Prahlada. 'Hear this hard question of dharma, wise one. What worlds await the
man who does not answer a question, or who answers it falsely? Tell me, for I
ask you.'

"'The man who knows and does not answer a question,' said Kashyapa, 'out of
desire, anger or fear, binds a thousand nooses of Varuna on himself. When a full
year has passed, one noose is loosed. So a man who knows the truth should speak
the truth straight out. When dharma, pierced by adharma, comes to the hall, and
the men in the hall do not pull out the dart, they are pierced themselves. The
chief among them takes half the sin. A quarter falls on the doers, and a quarter
on the men in the hall who do not condemn what should be condemned. But when the
one who deserves blame is blamed, the chief is free of sin, the men in the hall
are released, and the sin goes to the doer.

"'Those who speak falsely of dharma to a man who asks, Prahlada, destroy all
their sacrifices and good works, and seven generations before them and seven
after. There is the grief of a man who has been robbed, and of a man whose son
has been killed. There is the grief of a man in debt, and of a man seized by the
king. There is the grief of a woman without a husband, and of a man separated
from his caravan. There is the grief of a wife whose husband has taken another
wife, and of a man ruined by witnesses. The lords of the thirty gods say all
these griefs are equal. The man who speaks falsely suffers every one of them.
Witness comes from seeing with one's own eyes and from hearing, and so from
holding it in mind. So a witness who speaks the truth loses neither dharma nor
wealth.'

"When he had heard Kashyapa's words, Prahlada said to his son, 'Sudhanvan is
better than you, as Angiras is better than I. Sudhanvan's mother too is better
than your mother. Virochana, this Sudhanvan is now the master of your life.'

"'You have put aside your love for your son,' said Sudhanvan, 'and stood by
dharma. I give you back your son. Let him live a hundred years.'

"Now that you have heard this highest dharma, all you men in the hall, think
what must be done about Krishna's question."

When they heard Vidura's words, the kings said nothing at all. But Karna said to
Duhshasana, "Take Krishna, the slave, to the house." And Duhshasana dragged the
poor woman about in the middle of the hall. She was trembling and ashamed, and
crying out to the Pandavas.

---

"There is something I should have done first, and I have not done it," said
Draupadi. "I am bewildered, dragged here by force by this strong man. I greet my
elders in this assembly of the Kurus. Let it not be counted a fault in me that I
did not do so before."

Thrown about by him, and by grief, the poor woman, who had never known such
things, fell down and lamented in the hall.

"I, whom the kings gathered in the arena saw at my bridegroom choice, and who
was never seen anywhere else before, have today been brought into the hall. I,
whom neither the wind nor the sun ever saw before, in my own house, am seen
today in the middle of the hall, in the assembly of the Kurus. The Pandavas, who
would not let even the wind touch me before, in my own house, now let this
wicked man touch me. And these Kurus let their daughter-in-law and daughter be
tormented, though she does not deserve it. I think the times have turned.

"What could be more pitiful than this, that I, a good and virtuous woman, am
thrust into the middle of the hall today? Where is the dharma of kings? We have
heard that in the old days they did not bring righteous women into the hall.
That ancient, eternal dharma is lost among the Kauravas. How can I, the wife of
the Pandavas, the sister of Parshata, the friend of Vasudeva, a virtuous woman,
go into the hall of kings?

"I am the wife of the Dharma King, born of his own class. Tell me, Kauravas, am
I a slave or not? I will do as you say. This mean man, who destroys the fame of
the Kurus, is tormenting me hard, Kauravas, and I cannot bear it much longer.
Whether you think I was won or not won, kings, I want your answer, and I will do
as you say, Kauravas."

"I have told you already, lovely lady," said Bhishma, "that even great brahmins
in the world cannot reach the far end of dharma. What a strong man sees as
dharma in the world, others call dharma when the time for dharma comes. I cannot
decide your question for certain, because it is subtle and deep, and because the
matter is so grave. Surely the end of this family will come before long, for all
the Kurus are given over to greed and folly.

"Those who are born in good families, lovely lady, however hard misfortune
strikes them, do not stray from the paths of dharma, just as you stand firm, our
daughter-in-law. It is fitting, princess of Panchala, that you behave like this,
and look only to dharma even in your distress. These elders, Drona and the
others, who know dharma, sit with bowed heads and empty bodies, as if the life
had gone out of them. I think Yudhishthira is the authority on this question. He
himself should say whether you are won or not won."

The kings saw all this, and saw her crying out like an osprey in distress. But
out of fear of the son of Dhritarashtra they said nothing, good or bad. When the
son of Dhritarashtra saw those sons and grandsons of kings silent, he smiled a
little, and said to the daughter of the king of Panchala.

"Let this question rest with the great-hearted Bhima, with Arjuna and with
Sahadeva, and with your husband Nakula, Yajnaseni. Let them answer the question
you have raised. Let them say, among these noble men, that Yudhishthira was not
their master, princess of Panchala, for your sake. Let them all make the Dharma
King a liar, and you will be freed from slavery. Or let the great Dharma King,
who stands by dharma and is like Indra, say it himself, whether he is your
master or not your master. At his word, choose one side or the other, and
quickly. For all these Kauravas in the hall are caught up in your grief. Noble
as they are, they do not answer you properly, when they look at your luckless
husbands."

Then all the men in the hall loudly praised the words of the Kuru king. They
waved their clothes and shouted. There were cries of "Alas!" there too. And all
the kings were glad, and honoured the righteous best of the Kurus.

All the kings turned their faces towards Yudhishthira. "What will he say, he who
knows dharma?" they wondered. "What will Bibhatsu say, the Pandava never beaten
in battle? What will Bhimasena and the twins say?" They were full of curiosity.

When the noise had died down, Bhimasena raised his great round arm, smeared with
sandal paste, and said this.

"If Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, our elder, were not master of this family, we
would not have borne this. He is master of our merit and our austerities, and
master of our lives too. If he thinks he himself has been won, then we have been
won. No mortal who had touched these hairs of the princess of Panchala would
have got away from me alive, treading the earth with his feet. Look at these
arms of mine, long and round like iron bars. Once caught between them, not even
Indra of the hundred sacrifices could escape. But I am bound by the noose of
dharma, and I do not take that hard road. I am held back by my respect for him,
and restrained by Arjuna. But if the Dharma King let me loose, I would crush
these wicked sons of Dhritarashtra with the flats of my hands for swords, as a
lion crushes little deer."

Then Bhishma, Drona and Vidura said to him, "Be patient. Anything is possible
for you."

**Divergences.**

* **The garments.** The CE says only that "another garment of the same kind appeared, again and
  again" (2.61.41). It names no cause. Draupadi's prayers to Krishna, "Govinda, dweller in
  Dvaraka", are rejected passages \*542–\*550. The lines that the cloth came "by his grace"
  (\*551) or "by Dharma's protection" (\*553) are rejected too, as is the count of a hundred and
  eight garments (\*552). Krishna does not appear in the CE scene. See ledger L-19.
* **Bhima's oath.** The oath to drink Duhshasana's blood is in the CE (2.61.43–46). See ledger
  L-20. His oath about Duryodhana's thigh comes later (chapter 21, 2.63.14).
* **Karna.** Karna's words calling Draupadi a whore, and his order to strip the Pandavas and
  Draupadi, are in the CE (2.61.35–38).
* **Vikarna.** He is the one Kaurava who answers Draupadi's question (2.61.11–24).
* The vulgate adds that a quarter of the sin goes to the king and a quarter to the witness
  (\*558), and more griefs to Kashyapa's list (\*559). Not in the CE.
