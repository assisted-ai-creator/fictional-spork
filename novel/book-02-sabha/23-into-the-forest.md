# Into the Forest

"I take my leave of the Bharatas," said Yudhishthira, "and of the old
grandfather, of King Somadatta and the great King Bahlika. I take my leave of
Drona and Kripa, of the other kings and of Ashvatthama, of Vidura, Dhritarashtra
and all the sons of Dhritarashtra. I take my leave of Yuyutsu and Sanjaya, and
of all the others in the hall. I take my leave of you all, and go. I will see
you again when I come back."

They said nothing to Yudhishthira then, out of shame, good men as they were.
They only wished the wise man well in their hearts.

"The noble Pritha, a king's daughter, should not go to the forest," said Vidura.
"She is delicate and old, and has always been used to comfort. The good lady
will live here in my house, and be honoured. Know that, Parthas. May you all be
free from harm.

"Yudhishthira, bull of the Bharatas, know this from me. No one who is beaten by
adharma need be troubled by his defeat. You know the laws of dharma. Dhananjaya
knows war. Bhimasena kills enemies, and Nakula gathers wealth. Sahadeva is the
one who keeps order, Dhaumya is the best of those who know brahman, and
Draupadi, who walks in dharma, is skilled in dharma and in wealth. You are all
dear to one another, and speak kindly to one another. No enemy can divide you,
and you are content. Who here would not envy you?

"This steadiness of yours, Bharata, brings every blessing. No enemy can
withstand it, even one equal to Shakra, unfallen one. You were taught on the
Himalaya by Meru Savarni, at the town of Varanavata by Krishna Dvaipayana, on
the peak of Bhrigu by Rama, and on the Drishadvati by Shambhu. You heard the
great seer Asita too, at Anjana. Your priest Dhaumya here is often in Narada's
company. Do not lose, in the world to come, the understanding the seers have
praised.

"You surpass Pururavas, the son of Ila, in understanding, Pandava. You surpass
other kings in power, and the seers in your service of dharma. Set your mind on
victory, as Indra does. Hold back anger, as Yama does. Give, as Kubera does, and
restrain yourself, as Varuna does. Know that you have giving and gentleness from
the moon, and your life from the waters. You have patience from the earth, all
your fire from the sun's disc, and your strength from the wind. You are made of
the elements. May you be free from harm, and blessed. I will see you when you
come back. In times of trouble, in hard matters of dharma and wealth, or in
anything you have to do, act rightly, Yudhishthira, each time as it comes. You
have taken your leave here, son of Kunti. Go safely, Bharata. We will see you
come back safe, your purpose achieved."

When he was told this, Yudhishthira, the Pandava of true courage, said, "So be
it." He bowed to Bhishma and Drona, and set out.

---

As he set out, Krishna went to glorious Pritha in great grief and took her
leave, and of the other women who were there. She greeted them and embraced
them, each as was fitting, and made ready to go. Then a great wailing rose in
the women's quarters of the Pandavas. Kunti was in anguish when she saw Draupadi
going, and in a voice broken by grief she spoke with difficulty.

"Child, do not grieve, now that this great disaster has come on you. You know
the dharma of women, and you have good character and conduct. I do not need to
tell you, bright-smiling one, what to do for your husbands. You are an ornament
to both your families, with a good wife's virtues. The Kurus are lucky that you
did not burn them up, sinless one. Go on your way unharmed, and let my thoughts
of you give you strength. For good women do not lose heart at what must come.
You are guarded by dharma, which is greater than all, and you will soon find
good fortune again. Look after my son Sahadeva always, as he lives in the
forest, so that his great heart does not sink under this disaster."

"So I will," said the queen. Her eyes were streaming with tears. She wore a
single garment stained with blood, and her hair was loose. She went out.

As she went away weeping, Pritha followed her in her grief. Then she saw all her
sons, stripped of their ornaments and clothes, their bodies wrapped in the skins
of the ruru deer, their faces bowed a little with shame. Their enemies stood
round them, glad, and their friends mourned for them. Full of love, she went up
to her sons in this state, embraced them and cried out in her grief, lamenting
many things.

"You are adorned with true dharma, good conduct and firm ways. You are not mean,
you are steadfast in devotion, and always intent on the gods and on sacrifice.
How has this disaster come on you? What is this reversal of fate? Whose ill will
has brought this wrong on you, as I see it in my mind? It must be the fault of
my own fortune, that I bore you, and you have to suffer such pain and hardship,
though you have the highest virtues. How will you live in the rough forests,
robbed of your wealth? You are not lacking in courage, spirit, strength, energy
and fire, and yet you will waste away.

"If I had known that exile in the forest was surely coming to you, I would not
have come from Shatashringa to the city named after the elephant, when Pandu
died. I think your father was fortunate, rich in austerity and wisdom, who chose
heaven, as he wished, and never had to grieve for his sons. And I think Madri
was fortunate today, who knew what is beyond the senses, who knew dharma, and
was good in every way. She has reached the highest goal. She outdid me in love,
in understanding and in the way she went. Shame on this love of life of mine,
which brings me only suffering."

As Kunti lamented like this, the Pandavas comforted her and bowed to her, and
with all joy gone they went off to the forest. Vidura and the others comforted
the grieving Kunti with reasons, and slowly took her into the steward's house,
themselves in still greater grief.

King Dhritarashtra, his mind troubled with grief, sent word to the steward,
"Come quickly." So Vidura went to Dhritarashtra's house, and King Dhritarashtra
questioned him anxiously.

---

"How does the son of Kunti, the Dharma King Yudhishthira, go?" said
Dhritarashtra. "How do Bhimasena, Savyasachin and the two sons of Madri go? How
does Dhaumya go, steward, and poor Draupadi? I want to hear all of it, how each
of them moves."

"Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, goes with his face covered with his cloth,"
said Vidura. "Bhima the Pandava goes stretching out his great arms. Savyasachin
follows the king, scattering sand. Sahadeva, the son of Madri, goes with his
face smeared. Nakula, the handsomest man in the world, follows the king with his
whole body smeared with dust, his mind in turmoil. Large-eyed Krishna, so
lovely, follows the king weeping, her face covered with her hair. And Dhaumya
goes along the roads, lord of the people, with kusha grass in his hand, singing
the fearful Saman hymns of Yama and Rudra."

"The Pandavas go in all these different ways," said Dhritarashtra. "Tell me,
Vidura, why they go like this."

"Though your sons have tricked him," said Vidura, "and his kingdom and his
wealth have been taken, the wise Dharma King's mind does not waver from dharma.
The king has always been kind to the sons of Dhritarashtra, Bharata. Now,
burning with anger at the trickery, he will not open his eyes. 'I must not look
on the people with a terrible eye and burn them.' That is why the Pandava king
goes with his face covered.

"Hear from me, bull of the Bharatas, why Bhima goes as he does. 'No one is my
equal in strength of arm.' That is why Bhima goes stretching out his great arms.
He shows his arms, proud of the wealth of his arms, meaning to do to his enemies
what that wealth allows.

"Arjuna, the son of Kunti, Savyasachin, follows the king scattering sand,
pointing to the showers of arrows he will let fly. As the grains of sand fall
from him now, Bharata, one after another, so he will let fly showers of arrows
on his enemies. 'Let no one know my face today, Bharata.' That is why Sahadeva
goes with his face smeared. 'I must not steal the hearts of the women on the
road, my lord.' That is why Nakula goes with his whole body covered in dust.

"Draupadi, in a single garment, weeping, her hair loose, in her period, her wet
clothes stained with blood, said this. 'Those for whose sake I have come to
this: in the fourteenth year their wives will enter the city named after the
elephant. Their husbands will be killed, their sons killed, their dear kin
killed. Their bodies will be smeared with their kinsmen's blood, their hair
loose, in their periods, when they have offered water to the dead.'

"The steadfast priest Dhaumya has turned the kusha grass to the south-west, the
quarter of Nirriti, Bharata, and goes in front, singing the Saman hymns of Yama.
'When the Bharatas are killed in battle, the gurus of the Kurus will sing the
Samans like this.' So said Dhaumya as he went. 'Alas! Alas! Our masters are
going. See this!' So the grieving townspeople cry out on every side.

"So, with their looks and signs, the proud sons of Kunti told the resolve in
their hearts, and went to the forest. As those best of men went out of the city
named after the elephant, there was lightning without a cloud, and the earth
shook. Rahu swallowed the sun, lord of the people, out of season. A meteor
passed the city on the left and broke apart. Flesh-eating creatures, vultures,
jackals and crows, cried out from the temples and shrines, from the walls and
towers. Such are the great portents, king, as the Pandava goes to the forest,
for the ruin of the Bharatas, because of your evil counsel."

And Narada stood in the middle of the hall before the Kurus, surrounded by great
seers, and spoke a terrible word. "In the fourteenth year from now the Kauravas
will perish here, through Duryodhana's fault, by the strength of Bhima and
Arjuna." When he had said this, the best of the divine seers rose into the sky,
bearing the great glory of brahman, and quickly vanished.

---

Then Duryodhana, Karna and Shakuni, the son of Subala, looked on Drona as their
island, and offered him the kingdom. Then Drona spoke to Duryodhana, who could
never bear anything, to Duhshasana and Karna, and to all the Bharatas.

"The twice-born say that the Pandavas cannot be killed, for they are sons of the
gods. But I will do what I can for the sons of Dhritarashtra and their kings,
who have come to me for shelter with all their hearts and all their devotion. I
cannot abandon them. Fate is the root of what comes next. The sons of Pandu have
been beaten fairly, and they are going to the forest. They will live in the
forest for twelve years, Kauravas. They will keep the vow of celibacy, and in
the grip of anger and resentment the Pandavas will come back to take revenge, to
my sorrow.

"I threw Drupada down from his kingdom in a quarrel between friends. In his
anger he sacrificed for a son who would kill me, Bharata. By the austerity of
Yaja and Upayaja he won a son from the fire, Dhrishtadyumna, and from the middle
of the altar Draupadi, with her slender waist. The colour of flame, given by the
gods, with bow, armour and arrows: since I am mortal, fear of him has taken hold
of me. For that bull among men, Parshata, has gone over to their side. He is
full of life, and so I will fight your enemies. He was born, men say, to kill
me, and the whole world knows it well. Surely the turn of time has come, because
of you.

"Hurry and do what is good for you. This is not the end of it. This is happiness
for a moment, like the shadow of a palm tree in winter. Hold great sacrifices,
enjoy yourselves, and give. In the fourteenth year from now you will meet a
great slaughter. Duryodhana, when you have heard this, do as you wish. Or try
peace with the Pandavas, if you think fit."

When he heard Drona's words, Dhritarashtra said, "The teacher speaks rightly,
steward. Bring back the Pandavas. Or if they will not turn back, let the
Pandavas go with honour, with weapons, chariots and foot soldiers, and with
comforts, my sons."

---

When the Parthas, beaten at dice, had gone to the forest, great king, worry took
hold of Dhritarashtra. As King Dhritarashtra sat brooding, sighing and
distracted, Sanjaya said to him, "You have won the earth, lord of the earth,
full of riches. You have driven the Pandavas from their kingdom. Why do you
grieve, king?"

"How can those not grieve," said Dhritarashtra, "who will have to face the
enmity of the Pandavas, great warriors, fierce in battle and rich in allies?"

"This is your own doing, king," said Sanjaya. "There will be a great enmity.
There will be the destruction of the whole world, with all its kin. Though
Bhishma, Drona and Vidura tried to stop him, your son Duryodhana, the fool, sent
the suta's son Pratikamin. He shamelessly sent him to bring the Pandavas' dear
wife, Draupadi, who walks in dharma."

"When the gods give a man defeat," said Dhritarashtra, "they first take away his
understanding, and he sees everything backwards. When the mind is clouded and
ruin is at hand, bad policy looks like good, and it will not leave the heart.
Harm takes the shape of good, and good the shape of harm, and they rise up
before a man at the end, when he is ruined, and he likes it so. Time does not
raise a club and cut off anyone's head. The power of Time is only this, to make
a man see things backwards.

"This terrible, hair-raising uproar came from dragging the poor princess of
Panchala into the middle of the hall. She was not born of a womb. She is
beautiful, born of a great family, radiant, glorious, and she knows every
dharma. Who but a man playing an evil game of dice would insult her and drag her
into the middle of the hall? She was in her period, fair-hipped, and stained
with blood. The princess of Panchala in a single garment looked to the Pandavas,
and they had lost their wealth and their wits, their wife and their fortune.
They were robbed of all they wanted and brought to slavery, bound by the noose
of dharma, as if they had no power to act. Krishna stood in the assembly of the
Kurus, angry, unable to bear it, and in pain, and Duryodhana and Karna spoke
bitter words to her. With her piteous eyes she might have burned up the earth.
Will anything be left of my sons now, Sanjaya?

"When they saw Krishna brought into the hall, all the women of the Bharatas,
gathered with Gandhari, cried out fearfully. The evening fire offerings were not
made anywhere. The brahmins were angry at the dragging of Draupadi. There was a
terrible roar, and a great crash of thunder. Fearful meteors fell from the sky,
and Rahu swallowed the sun out of season, making the people terribly afraid.
Fire broke out in the chariot sheds, and banners fell apart, for the ruin of the
Bharatas. Jackals howled fearfully at Duryodhana's fire offering, and donkeys
answered them from every side.

"Then Bhishma set out with Drona, Sanjaya, and Kripa, Somadatta and Bahlika, the
great warrior. Then, urged by Vidura, I said, 'I will give Krishna a boon,
whatever she wants.' The princess of Panchala chose the Pandavas, of boundless
power, with their chariots and their bows, and I gave them leave.

"Then wise Vidura, who knew every dharma, said, 'This is the end of you,
Bharatas, that Krishna has come into the hall. This princess of Panchala, the
daughter of the king of Panchala, is the highest fortune, sent by the gods to
the Pandavas. The Parthas, who cannot bear an insult, will not forgive her
suffering, nor will the Vrishnis, the great archers, nor the Panchalas of great
power. Guarded by Vasudeva, who is true to his word, Bibhatsu will come,
protected by the Panchalas. In their midst will come the great archer Bhimasena,
of great strength, whirling his mace like Death with his staff. When they hear
the twang of wise Partha's Gandiva, and feel the force of Bhima's mace, the
kings will not be able to bear it. So I never want war with the Parthas. I have
always thought the Pandavas stronger than the Kurus. For mighty King Jarasandha,
of great splendour, was killed in a fight by Bhima with his bare arms. So let
there be peace with the Pandavas, bull of the Bharatas. Do what is fitting for
both sides, without hesitation.'

"So the steward spoke words of dharma and of good sense, son of Gavalgana, and I
did not take them, wanting what was good for my son."

**The CE's own doublets.** Dhritarashtra's account to Sanjaya (2.72.12–36) tells again what the
reader has seen: the dragging of Draupadi, the jackals and donkeys, and the boon. In his telling,
Vidura urges the boon and Draupadi chooses the Pandavas "with their chariots and their bows"
(2.72.25–26), without the two separate boons of 2.63.27–36. Both are kept.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi's hair.** In the CE Draupadi leaves weeping, in one garment stained with blood, her
  hair loose (2.70.9, 2.71.18), and foretells that the Kaurava widows will enter Hastinapura with
  their hair loose, smeared with their kinsmen's blood (2.71.19–20). She makes no vow to leave her
  hair unbound until she washes it in Duhshasana's blood. See ledger L-22.
* **Kunti stays behind.** Vidura keeps Kunti in his house (2.69.5–6). She does not go to the
  forest. The vulgate adds her cry that she will go with them (App. I No. 42). Not in the CE.
* **Dhritarashtra's order.** After Drona's warning, Dhritarashtra tells Vidura to bring the Pandavas
  back, or let them go with honour, weapons and comforts (2.71.46–47). The CE does not say what
  came of it; in the next chapter he is brooding alone.
* The vulgate adds crowds of citizens watching them go (App. I No. 41) and going back into the city like cows without their calves (App. I No. 44), the Kaurava women's weeping (\*600), and, at the end of
  the Parva, the counts of chapters and verses and the rites for hearing it (\*620–\*630). Not in
  the CE.
