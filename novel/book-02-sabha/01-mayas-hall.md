# Maya's Hall

Then Maya spoke to Partha, in Vasudeva's presence. He joined his hands and
honoured him again and again, and spoke gently.

"You have saved me, Kaunteya, from this angry Krishna and from the fire that
wanted to burn me. Tell me what I can do for you."

"You have done everything already," said Arjuna. "Go in peace, great asura. Be
my friend always, as we are yours."

"That is worthy of you, lord, as you say, bull among men," said Maya. "But I
want to do something for you, out of friendship, Bharata. For I am the
Vishvakarman of the danavas, their great artist. So I want to make something for
you, Pandava."

"You think I have saved you from mortal danger," said Arjuna. "That being so, I
cannot have you make anything for me. But I do not want your wish to come to
nothing, danava. Make something for Krishna. That will repay me."

When Maya pressed him, bull of the Bharatas, Vasudeva thought for a moment about
what he should ask. Then Krishna told him, "Build a hall for the Dharma King,
daitya, of whatever kind you think fit. Build a hall that men will look at in
wonder and never be able to copy, in all this world of men. Build that hall,
Maya, in which we may see the designs of gods, asuras and men carried out by
you."

Maya accepted the task gladly, and with joy he planned for the Pandava a hall
like a palace of the gods. Then Krishna and Partha told the Dharma King
Yudhishthira all about it, and presented Maya to him. Yudhishthira honoured him
as he deserved, and Maya accepted the honour and returned it. And here and there
the daitya told the sons of Pandu about the deeds of the old gods, lord of the
people, Bharata.

After he had rested for a while and thought it over, Vishvakarman began to build
the hall for the great Pandavas, to the wishes of the Parthas and of great
Krishna. On a holy day, when the auspicious rites had been done, the splendid,
mighty Maya satisfied thousands of the best brahmins with rice cooked in milk,
and gave them wealth of many kinds. Then he had the hall measured out on every
side, ten thousand cubits, heavenly in form and lovely, with the virtues of
every season.

---

When Janardana had stayed happily in Khandavaprastha, honoured by the loving
Parthas as he deserved, he made up his mind to leave, longing to see his father.
The large-eyed one took leave of the Dharma King and of Pritha. He whom the
world honours bowed his head at his aunt's feet, and she kissed Keshava on the
head and embraced him.

Then glorious Krishna saw his sister. Hrishikesha went to her with love, and
with tears in his eyes, and the blessed one spoke to Bhadra, Subhadra of the
gentle words, words that were meaningful, true and kind, brief, fitting and
fine. She gave him messages to take to her family, honoured him again and again,
and bowed her head to him. The Varshneya took leave of the lovely woman and
blessed her. Then Janardana saw Krishna and Dhaumya. The best of men bowed to
Dhaumya as was proper, and Janardana comforted Draupadi and took his leave.

Then the wise and strong one went to the brothers with Partha. Surrounded by the
five brothers, Krishna was like Shakra among the immortals. The bull of the
Yadus worshipped the gods and the brahmins with garlands, prayers and bows, and
with perfumes of many kinds. When he had done all he had to do, the best of
those who stand set out. He had worthy brahmins speak blessings, with pots of
curds, fruit and unhusked grain, gave them wealth, and walked round them,
keeping them on his right.

He mounted his swift golden chariot with Garuda on its banner, furnished with
mace, discus, sword, the bow Sharnga and other weapons. On a day, under a
constellation and at an hour of good omen, the lotus-eyed one set out, drawn by
Sainya and Sugriva. And King Yudhishthira climbed up after him, out of love. He
moved aside Krishna's driver Daruka, the best of drivers, and the Kuru king took
the reins himself. Arjuna too climbed up, and waved a great white fly-whisk with
a golden handle over Krishna's head, moving round him with respect. So too
Bhimasena, with the twins, followed Krishna behind, master of himself, with
priests and townspeople round him. Followed like this by the brothers, Keshava,
the slayer of enemy heroes, shone like a teacher followed by the pupils he
loves.

Govinda took leave of Partha and held him tight. He honoured Yudhishthira and
Bhimasena, and the twins embraced him hard and greeted him. When he had made the
proper arrangements with them, Madhusudana turned back the Pandavas and their
followers on foot, and Krishna went on to his own city like a second Purandara.
They followed him with their eyes as far as their sight could reach, and they
followed him with their hearts, out of love. While their hearts had not yet had
enough of seeing Keshava, Shauri, so lovely to look at, quickly vanished from
their eyes. Unwillingly, as it seemed, with their hearts gone after Govinda, the
Parthas, bulls among men, all turned and went back to their city. And Krishna in
his chariot reached Dvaraka in good time.

---

Then Maya said to Partha Arjuna, the best of victors, "I ask your leave. I will
go, and I will come back soon. North of Kailasa, towards Mount Mainaka, when all
the danavas were about to sacrifice, I made a lovely vessel of gems, by the lake
Bindusaras. It was in the hall of Vrishaparvan, who was true to his word. I will
come back with it, if it is still there, Bharata. Then I will build a hall for
the glorious Pandava, delightful, bright and adorned with every jewel. There is
also a fine mace at Bindusaras, joy of the Kurus, laid there by King Yauvanashva
when he had killed his enemies in battle. It is decorated with drops of gold,
heavy, strong, able to bear great blows. It is equal to a hundred thousand
maces, and it kills everything. It is as fit for Bhima as Gandiva is for you.
And there is Varuna's great conch, Devadatta, with its fine sound. I will give
you all of it, without a doubt."

With that the asura went off towards the north-east.

North of Kailasa, towards Mount Mainaka, is the blessed mountain Hiranyashringa,
the Golden Peak, made of great gems. There is the lovely lake called Bindusaras,
where King Bhagiratha lived for many years after he had seen the Ganga, the
Bhagirathi. There the great lord of all beings offered a hundred of the chief
sacrifices, best of the Bharatas. There the sacrificial posts were made of gems,
and the altars of gold, made for beauty and not after any model. There the
thousand-eyed husband of Shachi sacrificed and gained his perfection. There the
eternal lord of beings, of fierce splendour, who made all the worlds, is
worshipped, surrounded by thousands of beings. There Nara and Narayana, Brahma,
Yama and Sthanu as the fifth, attend a sacrifice that lasts to the end of a
thousand ages. There Vasudeva sacrificed in sessions lasting a thousand years,
with constant faith, to set an example to the good. There Keshava gave
sacrificial posts garlanded with gold, and altars of great brilliance, by the
thousand and the million.

Maya went there and took the mace and the conch, Bharata, and the crystal
material for a hall that had been Vrishaparvan's. With the rakshasas, his
Kinkaras, he took it all. When he had brought it, the asura built with it a hall
without equal, famous in the three worlds, heavenly, made of gems and lovely. He
gave the fine mace to Bhimasena, and to Partha he gave the peerless conch,
Devadatta.

That hall, great king, had trees of gold, and it stretched ten thousand cubits
on every side. Like fire, like the sun, like the moon, it blazed, and it wore a
heavenly form. It seemed to strike back the sun's bright light with its own, and
it shone with a heavenly radiance as if on fire. It stood like a mountain or a
cloud, filling the sky, long and wide, smooth, free of every stain and every
weariness. It was made of the finest materials, ringed with walls of gems, full
of jewels and full of wealth, well built by the Vishvakarman. Neither the
Dasharhas' hall Sudharma, nor Brahma's own, was so beautiful as the peerless
hall that Maya built.

Eight thousand rakshasas called Kinkaras guarded that hall and carried it, at
Maya's word. They moved through the sky, terrible and huge, mighty, with red and
tawny eyes and ears like shells, ready to strike.

In that hall Maya made a lotus pool without equal. It was spread with leaves of
lapis and lotuses with stems of gems. It was full of lotuses and water lilies,
with birds of many kinds, bright with blooming lotuses, and fair with turtles
and fish. It had good steps, clear water in every season, and it was lovely,
sprinkled with drops like pearls tossed up by the wind. It was set so thick with
gems and jewels that some kings, coming up to it and looking at it, did not know
it for what it was, and fell in.

All round that hall there were always great flowering trees of every kind, dark,
cool in their shade and lovely. There were fragrant groves, and pools
everywhere, full of geese and ducks and bright with chakravaka birds. The wind
brought the Pandavas the scent of flowers that grow in water and on land.

When he had built such a hall in fourteen months, Maya told the Dharma King that
it was finished.

**Divergences.**

* **Who asked for the hall.** In the CE, Arjuna will not accept anything for himself and sends
  Maya to Krishna, and Krishna asks for a hall for Yudhishthira (2.1.6–11). The vulgate has Maya
  offer a hall directly, and adds Arjuna's vow never to take a reward for help given (\*9, \*10).
* The vulgate adds Maya's promise to put a host of Kinkaras on Arjuna's banner (\*38, \*39). Not
  in the CE.
* The vulgate lets Abhimanyu and Dhaumya ride out with Krishna, and adds more of the farewell
  (\*26, \*27, App. I No. 1). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 2.1.5 *viśvakarmā … dānavānām*: "the Vishvakarman of the danavas". Maya compares himself to
  the gods' craftsman. At 2.1.16 and 2.3.23 the text calls Maya himself "Vishvakarman". See
  ledger L-101.
* 2.1.15 *pūrvadevacaritam*: "the deeds of the old gods", the asuras.
* 2.1.19 *daśakiṣkusahasrām*: "ten thousand cubits" (*kiṣku*, a cubit).
* 2.2.10 *tasthuṣāṃ varaḥ*: "the best of those who stand", Krishna.
* 2.2.12 *tārkṣyaketanam*: "with Garuda on its banner".
* 2.2.14 *dārukam*: Daruka, Krishna's driver.
* 2.3.5 *yauvanāśvena*: King Yauvanashva, who laid the mace there.
* 2.3.11 *na tu dṛṣṭāntataḥ kṛtāḥ*: "not after any model". The sense is uncertain.
* 2.3.16 *kiṃkaraiḥ saha rakṣobhiḥ*: the rakshasas called Kinkaras, "servants". Not the rakshasa
  Kinkara of Book 1.
* 2.3.30: the kings who fall into the pool look ahead to Duryodhana's falls in the hall
  (2.43.3–10).
