# The Wrestling Match

Then the Yadu, eloquent Krishna, the one beyond the senses, spoke to King
Jarasandha, who had made up his mind to fight. "With which of us three, king,
does your mind choose to fight? Which of us shall make ready for battle?"

When Krishna said this, King Jarasandha of Magadha, full of splendour, chose to
fight Bhimasena.

His family priest came to Jarasandha as he made ready to fight, bringing the
best medicines, and remedies for fainting and for pain. When a glorious brahmin
had spoken the blessings over him, Jarasandha, true to a kshatriya's dharma,
armed himself. He took off his crown and smoothed his hair, and rose like the
ocean bursting its shore. The wise king said to Bhima of fearsome courage,
"Bhima, I will fight you. It is better to be beaten by a better man."

With that, Jarasandha, the tamer of enemies, of great splendour, went to meet
Bhimasena, as the asura Bali went to meet Shakra. Then mighty Bhimasena, when he
had taken counsel with Krishna and been blessed, went up to Jarasandha, wanting
to fight.

Then those two tigers among men came together, with their arms for weapons,
heroes both, full of joy, each longing to defeat the other. As they struck each
other with their arms, and seized and held, there was a terrible noise, like a
thunderbolt striking a mountain. Both were full of joy, both were beyond strong,
each looking for a gap in the other, each wanting to win. They drove the people
back, and the fight went on in a lonely place, a terrible fight between two
mighty men, king, like that of Vritra and Vasava. They dragged each other
forward and back, pulled and pushed and wrenched, and struck each other with
their knees. They roared insults at each other, and hit each other with blows
like falling rocks. Broad-chested and long-armed, both skilled at wrestling,
they locked their arms together like iron bars.

The fight began on the first day of the month of Karttika. It went on without
stopping, day and night, without rest. The two great men were still locked
together on the thirteenth. But on the night of the fourteenth, the Magadhan
drew back, worn out.

When Janardana saw the king so weary, king, he spoke to Bhima of fearsome deeds,
as if to rouse him. "Kaunteya, an enemy who is worn out should not be pressed
too hard in a fight. If he is pressed with all one's strength, he may give up
his life. So you should not press the king, Kaunteya. Fight him evenly with your
arms, bull of the Bharatas."

When Krishna said this, the Pandava, the slayer of enemy heroes, understood that
this was Jarasandha's weak point, and he made up his mind to kill him. Then
Vrikodara, the best of the strong, the joy of the Kurus, gathered himself to
conquer the unconquered Jarasandha, and seized him.

---

Then Bhimasena, wanting to kill Jarasandha, set his great mind to it and said to
Krishna, the joy of the Yadus, "Krishna, tiger of the Yadus, this wicked man,
with his loincloth bound tight, is not one I should spare at the cost of my
life."

When he said this, Krishna, the tiger among men, answered Vrikodara, urging him
on, wanting Jarasandha dead. "Show us now, Bhima, on Jarasandha, the highest
power that is yours from the gods, and the strength that is yours from the
wind."

When he was told this, mighty Bhima, the tamer of enemies, lifted up the strong
Jarasandha and whirled him round, king. He whirled him round a hundred times
with his arms, bull of the Bharatas, then bent him backwards and broke him,
crushed him and roared. As he was crushed, and as the Pandava roared, there was
a tremendous noise that terrified every living thing. All the Magadhans were
terrified, and the women miscarried at the roar of Bhimasena and of Jarasandha.
"Has the Himalaya split? Is the earth breaking apart?" So the Magadhans thought,
at Bhimasena's roar.

Then the tamers of enemies left the king at the door of the palace in the night,
lifeless, as if asleep, and went out. Krishna yoked Jarasandha's chariot, with
its flags, made the two brothers mount it, and freed his kin. The kings, freed
from that great danger, came to Krishna, who deserved jewels, and gave him
jewels.

Unhurt, armed, his enemy conquered, with the kings, he mounted that heavenly
chariot and drove out of Girivraja. It was called Sodaryavan. It carried two
fighters and Krishna as driver, it struck where it was aimed, it was beautiful
to see, and all the kings together could not defeat it. With Bhima and Arjuna as
its fighters and Krishna as its driver, that finest of chariots shone, and no
archers could defeat it. Shakra and Vishnu rode it in the battle over Taraka,
and now Krishna mounted it and drove away. It was the colour of molten gold,
hung with nets of little bells, roaring like a cloud, victorious, a slayer of
enemies. It was the chariot in which Shakra killed ninety-nine danavas. When
they had it, the bulls among men were delighted.

When the Magadhans saw strong-armed Krishna in the chariot with the two
brothers, they were amazed. Yoked with heavenly horses, as swift as the wind,
and driven by Krishna, the chariot shone brightly, Bharata. On that finest of
chariots stood a banner, not made by men but by the gods, glorious, as bright as
Indra's bow, and it could be seen from a league away. Then Krishna thought of
Garuda, and Garuda came. In a moment he was there, tall as a sacrificial post at
a shrine. With the creatures that live on the banner, their mouths gaping and
roaring, Garutman, the eater of snakes, stood on that finest of chariots. Hard
for creatures to look at, he shone brighter still, like the sun at noon wrapped
in a thousand rays. It never caught on trees, it was never harmed by weapons,
that heavenly banner, king, and gods and men could see it.

Riding that heavenly chariot, which thundered like the rain god, the unfallen
one, the tiger among men, drove out with the two Pandavas. King Vasu had
received it from Vasava, from Vasu it passed to Brihadratha, and from
Brihadratha in turn to King Jarasandha.

The glorious, strong-armed, lotus-eyed one drove out of Girivraja, and stopped
on level ground outside. There all the townspeople, led by the brahmins, came to
him with honour, king, as the rules require. And the kings freed from their
bonds honoured Madhusudana, and said to him kindly, "It is no wonder in you,
strong-armed son of Devaki, with the strength of Bhima and Arjuna, to protect
dharma. We were drowning in the terrible lake that was Jarasandha, in the mud of
misery, and you have lifted us out today. We had sunk down in a cruel mountain
fortress, Vishnu, and by good fortune, best of men, you have won blazing fame by
freeing us. What shall we do, tiger among men? Tell us, bull among men. Whatever
it is, even if it is hard, count it as done by these kings."

Great-minded Hrishikesha comforted them and said, "Yudhishthira wants to perform
the Rajasuya sacrifice. He is devoted to dharma and wants to be king of the
earth. Give him your help, all of you, for the sake of the sacrifice."

Then the kings, with glad hearts, bull of the Bharatas, all said, "So be it,"
and promised to do as he said. The lords of the earth gave the Dasharha jewels,
and Govinda accepted them with reluctance, out of kindness to them.

Then Jarasandha's son Sahadeva, a great warrior, came out with his people and
ministers, with the family priest in front. Humble and meek, with many jewels
carried before him, Sahadeva came to Vasudeva, the god among men. Krishna gave
him safety, for he was stricken with fear, and consecrated Jarasandha's son
there and then. Made one with Krishna, and honoured by the two Parthas, the wise
king went back into the city of the Barhadrathas.

And lotus-eyed Krishna, blazing with the greatest glory, took many jewels and
went away with the two Parthas. The unfallen one came with the two Pandavas to
Indraprastha, met the Dharma King, and said to him with joy, "By good fortune
mighty Jarasandha has been struck down by Bhima, best of kings, and these kings
have been freed from their bonds. By good fortune Bhimasena and Dhananjaya are
well, Bharata, and have come back to their own city unhurt."

Then Yudhishthira honoured Krishna as he deserved, and he embraced Bhimasena and
Arjuna with joy. Now that Jarasandha was destroyed, and his two brothers had won
the victory, Ajatashatru rejoiced with his brothers. The Pandava met the kings
in order of age, honoured them, and gave them leave to go. With Yudhishthira's
leave, the kings, glad at heart, went quickly back to their own lands in
carriages of every kind.

So Janardana, the tiger among men, of great understanding, had the Pandavas kill
his enemy Jarasandha. When he had had Jarasandha killed by design, the tamer of
enemies took leave of the Dharma King, of Pritha and of Krishna, Bharata, of
Subhadra, of Bhimasena, of Phalguna and of the twins. He said farewell to
Dhaumya, and set out for his own city in that same fine chariot, which shone
like the morning sun, given to him by the Dharma King, filling the quarters with
its sound. Then the Pandavas, led by Yudhishthira, walked round Krishna, whose
deeds are never strained, keeping him on their right.

When the blessed Krishna, the son of Devaki, had gone, the Pandavas, having won
a great victory, gave safety to the kings. Their power grew still more by that
deed, Bharata, and the Pandavas made Draupadi's happiness grow, king. At that
time the king, famous for guarding his kingdom, did by dharma all that was
right, joining dharma, pleasure and wealth.

**Divergences.**

* **Not torn in two.** In the popular story Bhima cannot kill Jarasandha, because his two halves
  keep joining again, until Krishna splits a reed and throws the halves in opposite directions,
  and Bhima tears Jarasandha apart in the same way. In the CE Bhima whirls him a hundred times
  and breaks his back (2.22.6). The tearing in two is only in rejected lines \*256–\*258, and
  Krishna's reed only in App. I No. 8. See ledger L-110.
* **How long.** The CE's fight begins on the first day of Karttika and ends on the night of the
  fourteenth (2.21.17–18). The vulgate's "fifteen days" is \*249.
* The vulgate adds a voice from the sky telling Krishna that Jarasandha is his to kill (\*255), and
  a speech by Sahadeva asking Krishna to forgive his father (App. I No. 9). Not in the CE.

* 2.21.13 *upahvare*: "in a lonely place".
* 2.21.14: the wrestling holds are named as in the text: forward and back, pull and push, and
