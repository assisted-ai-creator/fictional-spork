# The Wager

"Come, steward," said Duryodhana, "bring Draupadi here, the dear wife the
Pandavas honour. Let her sweep the house, and quickly. Let her be our pleasure,
with the slave girls."

"Only a man like you could think of such a thing," said Vidura. "You do not see,
fool, that you are caught in a noose. You are hanging over a cliff, and you do
not know it. You are a deer, and in your great childishness you are angering
tigers. There are snakes on your head, their sacs full of deadly poison. Do not
anger them, you fool. Do not go to the house of Yama.

"Krishna has not become a slave, Bharata. For in my view she was staked by the
king when he was no longer his own master. This king, the son of Dhritarashtra,
is bearing fruit like the bamboo, which kills itself when it fruits. He is ripe
for his end, and he does not see that dice lead to enmity and great fear.

"Do not wound others, and do not speak cruelly. Do not get the better of another
man by base means. Do not use the scorching words that lead to the worlds of
evil, that make another man tremble. Harsh words fly from the mouth, and the man
they strike grieves day and night. They land nowhere but in the other man's
vitals. So a wise man does not let them loose at others.

"A goat, they say, once dug up a knife. When the knife was lost, it pawed at the
ground with its hoofs and dug up the terrible thing that would cut its own
throat. So do not dig up enmity with the sons of Pandu. Nothing evil is ever
praised. It is dog-men who always bark like this, at forest hermits and
householders, at ascetics and men of full learning.

"You do not see the terrible crooked door of hell, son of Dhritarashtra. Many of
the Kurus will follow you, and Duhshasana with them, when the game comes to its
end. Gourds may sink and stones may float, and ships may always lose their way
on the water. But the foolish king, the son of Dhritarashtra, will not listen to
my words, though they are for his good. This will surely be the end of the
Kurus, a dreadful destruction that takes everything. The wise words of friends,
meant for their good, are not heard, and only greed grows."

---

"A curse on the steward!" said the son of Dhritarashtra, drunk with pride. He
looked round for the usher Pratikamin in the hall, and spoke to him in the midst
of those noble men.

"Pratikamin, you bring Draupadi here. You have nothing to fear from the
Pandavas. This steward is a coward, and argues, and he never wants us to
prosper."

When he was told this, the usher Pratikamin, a suta, heard the king's word and
set out quickly. He went in, like a dog into a lion's den, and came to the queen
of the Pandavas.

"Yudhishthira was drunk with the madness of dice, Draupadi," said Pratikamin,
"and Duryodhana has won you. Come to Dhritarashtra's house. I am to take you to
your work, Yajnaseni."

"How can you say this, Pratikamin?" said Draupadi. "What king's son would play
with his wife as the stake? The king must have been deluded, drunk with the
madness of dice. Did he have nothing else to stake?"

"When he had nothing else left to stake," said Pratikamin, "then the Pandava
Ajatashatru played with you. First the king staked his brothers, then himself,
and then you, princess."

"Go, son of a suta," said Draupadi. "Go to the gambler in the hall and ask him,
'Bharata, whom did you lose first, yourself or me?' When you have found that
out, come back, son of a suta, and then take me."

He went to the hall and told them what Draupadi had said. "Draupadi asks you
this: 'Whose master were you when you lost us? Whom did you lose first, yourself
or me?'"

But Yudhishthira did not move, as if the life had gone out of him. He gave the
suta no answer at all, good or bad.

"Let Krishna, the princess of Panchala, come here and ask her question herself,"
said Duryodhana. "Let everyone here hear what she says, and what he says."

The suta, who did Duryodhana's bidding, went to the royal house and spoke to
Draupadi, as if in pain. "The men in the hall are summoning you, princess. I
think the ruin of the Kauravas has come. A mean man does not guard good fortune,
if you are to come to the hall, princess."

"So the Ordainer has surely ordained it," said Draupadi. "Both touches touch the
wise man and the child. He said that dharma alone is supreme in the world. If we
guard it, it will bring us peace."

When Yudhishthira heard what Duryodhana meant to do, bull of the Bharatas, he
sent a messenger to Draupadi, one she would trust. Wearing a single garment,
with its knot tied low, weeping, in her period, the princess of Panchala came to
the hall and stood before her father-in-law.

Then King Duryodhana looked at their faces and said gladly to the suta, "Bring
her right here, Pratikamin. Let the Kurus speak to her face."

The suta did his bidding, but he was afraid of the anger of Drupada's daughter.
He set aside his pride and said again to the men in the hall, "What am I to say
to Krishna?"

"Duhshasana," said Duryodhana, "this son of a suta of mine is a fool, and he is
afraid of Vrikodara. Take hold of Yajnaseni yourself and bring her here. What
can our rivals do? They are not their own masters."

When he heard his brother, the prince rose up, his eyes red with anger. He went
into the house of those great warriors, and said to Princess Draupadi, "Come,
come, Panchali! You have been won, Krishna. Look at Duryodhana, and put away
your shame. Wait on the Kurus, you with the long lotus eyes. You were won
lawfully. Come to the hall."

She rose in great misery, wiped her pale face with her hand, and ran in her
distress towards the women of the old king, the bull of the Kurus. Then
Duhshasana rushed after her in fury, roaring, and seized the wife of the lord of
men by her long, dark, wavy hair. That hair had been sprinkled at the great
sacrifice of the Rajasuya, at the final bath, with water made holy by sacred
words. Now the son of Dhritarashtra seized it by force, and scorned the
Pandavas' courage.

Duhshasana laid hold of Krishna, whose hair was so dark, and brought her near
the hall. He dragged her, who had protectors, as if she had none, as the wind
drags a stricken plantain tree. As she was dragged, her slender body bent, she
said softly, "I am in my period today, and I have only one garment on, you fool.
You cannot take me into the hall, you brute."

But he held her down by force by her dark hair, and said to Krishna, "Cry out to
Krishna and Jishnu, to Hari and Nara, to save you. I will take you all the same.
Be in your period, Yajnaseni, or in a single cloth, or naked. You have been won
at dice and made a slave. And with slave women we do as we please."

Her hair was loose, half her garment had fallen, and Duhshasana was shaking her.
Burning with shame and anger, Krishna said softly, "These men in the hall are
learned in the teachings, men who perform the rites, all of them like Indra.
They are my elders, and those who stand in the place of elders. I cannot stand
before them like this.

"You man of cruel deeds, of ignoble ways, do not strip me. Do not drag me. The
princes will not forgive you, even if the gods with Indra were on your side. The
king, the son of Dharma, stands by dharma, and dharma is subtle, and only the
wise can find it. Even in words, I would not want to lay on my husband a fault
the size of an atom, and forget his virtues. But this is ignoble, that you drag
me here in my period, among the Kuru heroes. And no one here honours me for it.
Surely they have come round to your way of thinking.

"Shame! The dharma of the Bharatas is surely lost, and so are the ways of the
kshatriyas, when all the Kurus in the hall stand and watch the bounds of Kuru
dharma broken. Drona and Bhishma have no spirit, surely, and no more has this
great king. That is why the chief elders of the Kurus do not see this terrible
wrong."

As the slender-waisted woman spoke so pitifully, she looked sideways at her
husbands, who were full of anger. With the glances she cast at them she set the
Pandavas on fire, their bodies seething with anger. The loss of their kingdom,
of their wealth and of their finest jewels did not hurt them as much as that
sideways glance of Krishna's, stirred by her anger and pain.

Duhshasana too saw Krishna looking at her wretched husbands. He shook her
violently, as she was almost fainting, and with a harsh laugh he said, "Slave!"
Karna was greatly delighted by the word, and applauded it with loud laughter.
The king of Gandhara, the son of Subala, cheered Duhshasana in the same way. But
all the others in the hall, except those two and the son of Dhritarashtra, were
deeply pained to see Krishna dragged about the hall.

"Because dharma is subtle, fortunate lady," said Bhishma, "I cannot rightly
settle this question of yours. A man who owns nothing cannot stake what belongs
to another. And yet one sees that a wife is in her husband's power. Yudhishthira
would give up the whole rich earth, but he would not give up the truth, and the
Pandava has said, 'I am won.' So I cannot decide this. Shakuni has no second
among men at dice, and the son of Kunti played with him of his own free will.
The great-souled man does not think it was deceit. So I cannot answer your
question."

"The king was challenged in the hall," said Draupadi, "by skilful, wicked,
deceitful, ignoble men who love dice, and he himself had little skill. How can
he be said to have played of his own free will? Pure of heart, the foremost of
the Kurus and the Pandavas did not see the deceit they were practising. They all
joined together and beat him, and only afterwards did he see the trickery. These
Kurus stand here in the hall, masters of their sons and their daughters-in-law.
Think over all my words, all of you, and answer my question rightly."

As she spoke so pitifully, weeping, and looking again and again at her husbands,
Duhshasana said harsh, cruel and bitter things to her. Vrikodara saw her being
dragged about, in her period, her upper garment slipping, who had done nothing
to deserve it. He looked at Yudhishthira, and his anger rose, in the greatest
pain.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi's prayer.** In the CE Duhshasana mocks her: "Cry out to Krishna and Jishnu, to Hari
  and Nara, to save you" (2.60.26). She does not pray. Her prayer to "Govinda, dweller in
  Dvaraka, Krishna, dear to the cowherd women" is \*535, a rejected passage. See ledger L-19.
* **Her question.** In the CE Draupadi sends her question by Pratikamin: "Whom did you lose first,
  yourself or me?" (2.60.7–8). She repeats it in the hall, and Bhishma cannot answer it
  (2.60.40–42). The famous verse "It is no assembly where there are no elders" is \*540 here, a
  rejected line. The CE does have the verse, but in Book 5, where Vidura says it to Dhritarashtra
  (5.35.49). See ledger L-118.
* **Her period.** The CE is plain that she was in her period and wore a single garment (2.60.15,
  25, 27, 47).
* The vulgate adds a speech in which Draupadi sends Pratikamin to ask the elders (\*531), and lines
  in which the Pandavas sit bowed and silent (\*534). Not in the CE.
