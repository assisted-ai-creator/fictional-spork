# Draupadi's Boons

"There are three, they say, who own nothing," said Karna. "A slave, a pupil, and
a woman who is not her own mistress. You are the wife of a slave, good lady, and
his property. A slave woman has no master of her own, and is a slave's property.
Go in, and serve us with the servants. That is the work left for you, to go into
the house. We are all your masters now, princess, the sons of Dhritarashtra, not
the Parthas.

"Choose another husband quickly, lovely lady, one who will not make you a slave
by gambling. A woman who chooses freely among husbands is not blamed. It is
always so in slavery. You should know that. Nakula has been beaten, and
Bhimasena, Yudhishthira, Sahadeva and Arjuna. You are a slave now, Yajnaseni. Go
in. Your husbands are beaten, and they are no longer yours. What use does Partha
think his courage and his manhood are to him, when he gambled away the daughter
of Drupada of Panchala at the stakes, in the middle of the hall?"

When he heard this, Bhimasena, who could never bear an insult, sighed deeply,
the very picture of pain. He followed the king, bound by the noose of dharma,
but he looked at him with eyes red with anger, as if to burn him.

"I am not angry with the son of the suta, king," said Bhima. "This really is the
dharma of slaves that has come upon us. But would our enemies be holding me back
now, lord of men, if you had not played for her?"

When he heard the son of Radha's words, King Duryodhana said to Yudhishthira,
who sat silent and dazed, "Bhima and Arjuna and the twins do as you command,
king. Answer the question. Do you think Krishna was not won?"

When he had said this to Kaunteya, he pushed aside his own garment. Drunk and
deluded with the pride of power, he looked at the princess of Panchala with a
smile. He smiled at the son of Radha, and as if to goad Bhima, he showed
Draupadi his left thigh as she watched. It was like the trunk of a plantain
tree, marked with every good mark, like an elephant's trunk, and heavy as a
thunderbolt.

When Vrikodara saw it, he opened his red eyes wide, and spoke to him in the
midst of the kings, as if for the whole hall to hear. "May Vrikodara never go to
the world of his fathers, if I do not break that thigh of yours with my mace in
the great battle."

As he raged, flames of fire burst from every opening of his body, as from the
hollows of a burning tree.

"See the great danger from Bhimasena," said Vidura. "Know it for what it is,
like the noose of King Varuna. Surely this great wrong among the Bharatas was
set in motion by fate long ago. This is gambling gone too far, sons of
Dhritarashtra, when you quarrel over a woman in this hall. Your welfare is in
great danger. The Kurus are hatching wicked plans.

"Kurus, understand this point of dharma quickly. If it is judged wrongly, this
assembly will be disgraced. If the gambler had staked her first, before he had
lost himself, he would have been her master. But when a man who is no longer
master of himself plays with a thing, I think it is like wealth won in a dream.
Do not listen to the son of Gandhari, Kurus, and fall away from this dharma."

"I will abide by what Bhima says, and Arjuna, and the twins too," said
Duryodhana. "If they say Yudhishthira was not their master, then you will be
freed from slavery, Yajnaseni."

"The king was our master before, when he staked us," said Arjuna, "the great son
of Kunti, the Dharma King. But once he had lost himself, whose master was he?
Decide that, all you Kurus."

---

Then, in King Dhritarashtra's house, a jackal howled loudly at the sacred fire.
Donkeys brayed back at it, king, and fierce birds on every side. Vidura, who
knew the truth of things, heard that terrible sound, and so did the daughter of
Subala. Bhishma and Drona and wise Gautama cried out loudly, "Peace! Peace!"

Then Gandhari and wise Vidura saw what that terrible portent meant, and in their
distress they told the king. Then the king said this.

"You are finished, Duryodhana, you fool. You wretch, you speak like this to a
woman, in the hall of the bulls of the Kurus, and above all to Draupadi, a
lawful wife."

When wise Dhritarashtra had said this, he looked for a way to save his kin from
disaster. He thought it over with the wisdom that saw the truth of things, and
spoke gently to Krishna, the princess of Panchala.

"Choose a boon from me, princess of Panchala, whatever you want. For of all my
daughters-in-law you are the best, a good woman devoted to dharma."

"If you will give me a boon, bull of the Bharatas," said Draupadi, "I choose
this. Let glorious Yudhishthira, who follows every dharma, be no slave. Let not
the boys, who do not know him, call my proud Prativindhya 'the slave's son' when
he comes. He has been a king's son, and cherished as no other boy ever was. If
he saw himself a slave's son, Bharata, he would die."

"I give you a second boon, good lady," said Dhritarashtra. "Choose it from me.
My heart tells me that you deserve more than one boon."

"Bhimasena and Dhananjaya, with their chariots and their bows," said Draupadi,
"and Nakula and Sahadeva: I choose them as my second boon."

"Choose a third boon from us," said Dhritarashtra. "You have not been honoured
enough with two. For you are the best of all my daughters-in-law, and you walk
in dharma."

"Greed is the ruin of dharma, my lord," said Draupadi. "I do not want it. I am
not fit to take a third boon, best of kings. They say a vaishya may have one
boon, and a kshatriya woman two, king of kings. A king may have three, and a
brahmin a hundred. These husbands of mine had fallen low, and now they have been
brought safely across. They will find good fortune, king, by their own good
deeds."

---

"Of all the women famed among men for their beauty, that we have heard of," said
Karna, "we have never heard that any of them did such a thing. When the Parthas
and the sons of Dhritarashtra were overcome with anger, Krishna Draupadi became
peace for the sons of Pandu. The sons of Pandu were sinking in water with no
boat and no footing, and the princess of Panchala became their boat, and brought
them to the shore."

When he heard this, Bhimasena, who could never bear an insult, said in great
bitterness, in the midst of the Kurus, "A woman is the refuge of the sons of
Pandu!

"Devala said that a man has three lights: his children, his deeds and his
learning. From these, creatures are born. When the body is unclean and lifeless
and empty, abandoned by his kin, these three live on after a man. But our light
has been struck down, because our wife has been violated. Dhananjaya, how can a
child be born to us from one who has been violated?"

"Great men of the Bharata line never answer a lesser man's harsh words," said
Arjuna, "whether they are spoken or not. The good remember only good deeds done
to them, not hostile ones. They know them for what they are, and they have faith
in themselves."

"I will kill all these enemies gathered here, right now," said Bhima. "Or go
outside, king of kings, and cut them down root and all, Bharata. What is the use
of arguing here, Bharata? What is the use of suffering? I will kill them here
today. You rule this earth."

When Bhimasena had said this, surrounded by his younger brothers, he looked
again and again at his iron club, like a lion among deer. Partha, whose deeds
are never strained, soothed him and fanned him, but the strong-armed hero
sweated with the burning inside him. As he raged, lord of men, fire burst from
his ears and the other openings of his body, with smoke and sparks and flame.
His face, with its knitted brows, was terrible to look at, like the face of
Death himself when the end of the age has come.

Yudhishthira held back the strong-armed man with his arm. "Not like this," he
said. "Be still, Bharata." When he had held back his strong-armed brother, whose
eyes were red with anger, he went up to his father Dhritarashtra with joined
hands.

"King, what shall we do for you?" said Yudhishthira. "Command us, for you are
our master. We always want to keep to your command, Bharata."

"Ajatashatru, bless you," said Dhritarashtra. "Go safely, and may no harm come
to you. With my leave, take your wealth with you, and rule your own kingdom. But
understand this command of an old man. I have spoken it with thought, and all of
it is for your good, and your highest good.

"You know the subtle course of dharma, my son Yudhishthira. You are disciplined,
very wise, and you have waited on the old. Where there is understanding, there
is peace. Be at peace, Bharata. The axe does not fall where there is no wood. It
falls on wood. The best of men do not remember old enmities. They see virtues,
not faults, and they do not go looking for quarrels. The lowest of men speak
harsh words in a dispute, Yudhishthira. The middling sort answer harshly when
they are spoken to harshly. But the best of men, the steadfast, never answer
harsh and hurtful words, whether they are spoken or not. The good remember only
good deeds done to them, not hostile ones. They know them for what they are, and
they have faith in themselves.

"You have behaved nobly in this gathering of good men, my son. Do not keep
Duryodhana's harshness in your heart. Look at your mother Gandhari, Bharata, and
at me, your old blind father, standing before you, who long for your virtues. I
let this game go on with my eyes open, wanting to see who our friends were, and
the strength and weakness of my sons. The Kurus need not be pitied, king, when
you are their ruler, and wise Vidura, master of every teaching, is their
counsellor. In you is dharma, in Arjuna strength, and in Bhimasena courage. In
the twins, the first of men, is faith and obedience to their elders.
Ajatashatru, bless you, go back to Khandavaprastha. Let there be brotherly love
between you and your brothers, and let your mind be set on dharma."

When he was told this, Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, the best of the Bharatas,
did all that noble custom asks, and set out with his brothers. They mounted
their chariots, which were like clouds, and with Krishna they went, glad at
heart, to Indraprastha, the finest of cities.

**The CE's own doublets.** Arjuna's words on how great men answer harsh words (2.64.8–9) come
again, almost word for word, in Dhritarashtra's counsel (2.65.8–9). Both are kept.

**Divergences.**

* **The thigh.** Duryodhana shows Draupadi his left thigh, and Bhima vows to break it with his mace
  (2.63.12–14). See ledger L-21. The vulgate adds a vow to kick Duryodhana's crown and head
  (\*568). Not in the CE.
* **The boons.** Draupadi asks for Yudhishthira's freedom for her son Prativindhya's sake
  (2.63.28–30), then for the other four with their chariots and bows (2.63.32). She refuses a
  third boon (2.63.34–36). She does not ask for her own freedom. The CE does not say in so many
  words that she is free, but she goes home with them (2.65.17).
* **Dhritarashtra's motive.** He speaks to her after the jackal's howl and the other portents, when
  Gandhari and Vidura warn him (2.63.22–26).
* The vulgate adds Karna telling Duhshasana to take Draupadi away as a slave (App. I No. 38), and a
  line where Dhritarashtra grants the first boon in so many words (\*569). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 2.63.1 *hīneśvarā*: "has no master of her own". The sense is uncertain.
* 2.63.3 *anavadyā vai patiṣu kāmavṛttiḥ*: "A woman who chooses freely among husbands is not
  blamed." Ganguli limits it to slave women. The sense is uncertain.
* 2.63.6 *dahann ivainam*: "as if to burn him". "Him" is taken as Yudhishthira, to whom Bhima then
  speaks.
* 2.63.12 *savyam ūrum*: "his left thigh".
* 2.63.15: the fire from "every opening" of his body. The text has *srotobhyaḥ*.
* 2.63.18–19: "If the gambler had staked her first … he would have been her master", and
  "like wealth won in a dream", as Ganguli.
* 2.63.23 *gautamaḥ*: Kripa. *svasti svasti*: "Peace! Peace!", words to turn away evil.
* 2.63.29 *manasvinam*: "my proud Prativindhya", her son by Yudhishthira.
* 2.63.35: the number of boons fit for each class is given as she says it.
* 2.64.5 *trīṇi jyotīṃṣi*: "three lights". The vulgate's other list, of courage, keeping one's
  word and guarding one's wife, is \*573.
* 2.64.7 *abhimṛṣṭajam*: "born … from one who has been violated". The word means "touched".
* 2.64.12 *parigham*: "his iron club".
* 2.65.5 *nādārau kramate śastram*: "The axe does not fall where there is no wood". *Śastra* is any
  blade.
* 2.65.12: "I let this game go on with my eyes open", as the text says.
