# Shishupala

When Yudhishthira saw that sea of kings, like the ocean itself, all heaving with
anger, he spoke to Bhishma. He spoke as Indra, the killer of enemies, might
speak to Brihaspati, to Bhishma, the best of the wise, the old grandfather of
the Kurus.

"Grandfather, this great sea of kings is heaving with anger. Tell me what should
be done here. Tell me today, grandfather, how the sacrifice may go on without
harm, and how all may go well for the people everywhere."

When Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, who knew dharma, had said this, Bhishma, the
grandfather of the Kurus, answered him.

"Do not be afraid, tiger of the Kurus. Can a dog kill a lion? I chose the safe
and well-guided path here long ago. When a lion is asleep, dogs gather round and
all bark together. So it is with these lords of the earth. Before the sleeping
lion of the Vrishnis, my son, these angry dogs stand barking in the lion's
presence. For as long as Achyuta sleeps like a lion and does not wake, this bull
of the Chedis, this lion among men, makes lions of them. Shishupala has little
sense, best of kings, and he means to lead all these kings, every one of them,
to the house of death, my son.

"Surely Adhokshaja wants to take back the energy of his that lives in
Shishupala, Bharata. Bless you, best of the wise, the wits of this king of the
Chedis have gone astray, son of Kunti, and so have the wits of all these kings.
For whenever this tiger among men wants to take anyone back into himself, that
man's wits go astray, as the Chedi king's have now. Madhava is the source and
the end, Yudhishthira, of all beings of the four kinds in the three worlds."

When he heard these words, the Chedi king spoke harsh words to Bhishma, Bharata.

---

"You frighten all these kings with scare after scare," said Shishupala. "Why are
you not ashamed, old as you are, you disgrace to your family? It is fitting that
you, living as one of the third sex, should talk against dharma, you, the
foremost of all the Kurus. The Kauravas, with you at their head, Bhishma, are
like a boat tied to a boat, or the blind following the blind.

"You have praised this man's deeds again, above all the killing of Putana and
the rest, and so upset us all over again. You are arrogant and a fool. You want
to praise Keshava. Why does your tongue not split into a hundred pieces,
Bhishma? Even boys would rightly mock this cowherd, and you, old in knowledge,
want to praise him!

"If he killed a bird in his childhood, what is so wonderful in that? Or the
horse and the bull, Bhishma, who knew nothing of fighting? If he kicked over a
cart, a lifeless piece of wood, Bhishma, what marvel did he do? If he held up
Govardhana for seven days, a mountain the size of an anthill, I do not think
that wonderful, Bhishma. 'He ate a great deal of food, playing on the top of a
mountain.' People heard this from you, Bhishma, and were greatly amazed. But he
killed Kamsa, a mighty man whose food he had eaten, you who know dharma. That is
no great marvel.

"You cannot have heard what good men say, Bhishma, you who do not know dharma,
you lowest of the Kuru line. I will tell you. 'Let no one bring down a weapon on
women, on cows or on brahmins, on the man whose food he has eaten, or on one who
has come to him for shelter.' So the good teach, the good men who follow dharma,
always. But in you, Bhishma, all this is plainly turned upside down. You praise
Keshava to me as if I did not know him, as old in knowledge, old in years and
greater. He killed a cow and he killed a woman, Bhishma. How can he deserve
praise?

"'This is the best of the wise. This is the lord of the world.' If Janardana
believes this on your word, and thinks it is all so, then surely it is all
false. A song does not teach the singer, however often he sings it. Creatures
follow their nature, like the bhulinga bird. Surely your nature is base. There
is no doubt of it. And so the nature of these Pandavas is held to be even worse,
for Krishna is the most worthy of honour to them, and you are their guide. You
speak of dharma and do not know dharma. You have fallen from the path of the
good.

"What man who knew himself to be righteous, best of the wise, would do what you
have done, Bhishma, with your eye on dharma? Bless you, you who know dharma and
think yourself wise, how did you come to carry off the girl called Amba, when
she loved another? The girl you carried off, Bhishma, was not wanted by your
brother, King Vichitravirya, who followed the ways of the good. And while you
looked on, you who think yourself wise, another man fathered children on his two
wives, on the path the good follow!

"There is no dharma in you, Bhishma. This celibacy you keep is useless. You keep
it out of folly or out of impotence, no doubt of it. I see no growth in you
anywhere, you who know dharma. You cannot have sat with the elders, to talk of
dharma as you do. Sacrifice, giving and study, and sacrifices with rich fees,
are not worth a sixteenth part of a child. Whatever a man does with many vows
and fasts, Bhishma, is all surely wasted if he has no child. You are childless
and old, and you teach a false dharma. Now you too may meet your death from your
own kin, like the goose.

"For wise men of old used to tell this story, Bhishma. I will tell it to you
properly, and you listen.

"Long ago, they say, there was an old goose who lived by the sea. He spoke of
dharma but lived otherwise, and he taught the birds. 'Practise dharma, not what
is against dharma.' This, they say, is what the birds heard him say, all the
time, Bhishma, as he spoke of dharma. Then other birds, egg-born creatures that
fed in the waters of the sea, brought him food, Bhishma, for dharma's sake, so
we have heard. And they left all their eggs near him, Bhishma, and went off
happily to feed in the waters of the sea. The wicked goose ate all their eggs,
wide awake to his own business while they were off their guard.

"Then, as the eggs grew fewer and fewer, one bird, a very wise one, grew
suspicious, and one day he saw him. When he had seen the goose's crime, he told
all the other birds, in the greatest grief. Then the birds gathered, saw it for
themselves, and killed that goose of false conduct, pride of the Kurus. These
lords of the earth are angry, Bhishma, and they may kill you, who are like that
goose, as the birds killed him.

"Those who know the old stories sing a verse about this, Bhishma, and I will
tell it to you properly, Bharata.

"*With your true self hidden away, bird, you cry out words that are false. Your
foul work of eating the eggs speaks louder than your words.*

"I thought highly of mighty King Jarasandha. He would not fight this man. 'He is
a slave,' he said. What Keshava did, with Bhimasena and Arjuna, in killing
Jarasandha, who thinks that was well done? Krishna came in by the wrong door, in
disguise, posing as a brahmin, and saw wise Jarasandha's power. Jarasandha was a
righteous man, who knew himself devoted to brahmins, and he offered this wicked
man water for his feet first, and the wicked man would not take it. 'Eat,'
Jarasandha said to Krishna, Bhima and Dhananjaya, Kauravya, and Krishna twisted
even that. If he is the maker of the world, as you think him, you fool, why does
he not truly count himself a brahmin?

"But this is a wonder to me, that the Pandavas, whom you have led away from the
path of the good, think it well done. Or perhaps it is no wonder, Bharata, when
their guide in everything is you, an old man, and like a woman."

---

When he heard these words of his, so many of them, so harsh in sense and in
sound, the best of the strong, mighty Bhimasena, flew into a rage. His eyes,
like lotuses, long and wide by nature, grew redder still, their corners coppery
with anger. All the kings saw the triple frown on his forehead, like the Ganga,
which runs three ways, on the mountain with three peaks. They saw his face as he
gnashed his teeth in anger, like the face of Time at the end of the ages, ready
to burn up every living thing. As he leapt up, the strong-armed Bhishma himself
caught hold of him, the proud man, as the lord Shiva caught Mahasena. As
Bhishma, his elder, held him back, Bharata, with words of every kind, Bhima's
anger calmed. The tamer of enemies did not go beyond Bhishma's word, as the
great ocean, whipped up when the rains are over, does not go beyond its shore.

But Shishupala, lord of men, did not tremble at Bhimasena's anger. The hero
stood firm in his own manhood. Though the tamer of enemies leapt up at him with
force again and again, he gave him no thought, as a lion gives none to a little
deer. The mighty king of the Chedis laughed when he saw Bhimasena of fearsome
courage so enraged, and said, "Let him go, Bhishma. Let the kings see him burned
up in the fire of my might, like a moth in a flame."

Then, when he heard the Chedi king's words, Bhishma, the best of the Kurus, the
best of the wise, spoke to Bhimasena.

"This Shishupala was born in the family of the king of Chedi with three eyes and
four arms. He brayed like a donkey and roared. His father and mother were
terrified at this, and so were their kin. When they saw that he was deformed,
they made up their minds to abandon him.

"Then, as the king, with his wife, his ministers and his family priest, stood
there with his heart dazed with worry, a voice spoke, from no body. 'This son of
yours, king, is born fortunate and very strong. So you must not be afraid of
him. Guard the child without fear. You are not his death, and his time has not
yet come. His death, the one who will kill him with a weapon, has been born,
lord of men.'

"When she heard what was said, the mother, burning with love for her son, spoke
to the being that could not be seen. 'I bow with joined hands to whoever spoke
these words about my son. Let him speak again. I want to hear who will be my
son's death.'

"Then the being that could not be seen spoke again. 'Someone will take him on to
his lap. When that happens, his two extra arms will fall to the ground like
five-headed snakes, and the third eye on the child's forehead will sink away at
the sight of him. That man will be his death.'

"When they heard of the child with three eyes and four arms, and of what had
been said, all the kings of the earth came to see him. The king honoured them as
they deserved when they came, and set his son on the lap of each king in turn.
So the child sat on the laps of thousands of kings, each in his turn, and the
sign did not come.

"Then the two Yadavas, Samkarshana and Janardana, came to the city of the
Chedis, to see their father's sister, the Yadava woman. They greeted her as was
proper, and the kings in order of age, and asked after their health. Then Rama
and Keshava sat down. When the two heroes had been honoured with more than usual
warmth, the queen herself set her son on Damodara's lap. As soon as he was set
on his lap, the two extra arms fell off, and the eye on his forehead sank away.
When she saw this, she was stricken and afraid, and she asked Krishna for a
boon.

"'Give me a boon, strong-armed Krishna, for I am in the grip of fear. For you
are the comfort of the suffering, and you free the frightened from fear.'

"'Do not be afraid, aunt,' said Janardana. 'What boon shall I give you, aunt?
What shall I do for you? Whether it can be done or not, I will do what you say.'

"When he said this, she said to Krishna, the joy of the Yadus, 'Mighty one, you
must forgive Shishupala his offences.'

"'I will forgive a hundred of your son's offences, aunt,' said Krishna, 'each
one deserving death. Do not set your heart on grief.'

"So this is the wicked King Shishupala, hero, the dull fool, who challenges you,
proud of Govinda's boon."

---

"This is not the Chedi king's own thought, when he challenges Achyuta," said
Bhishma. "Surely it is the resolve of Krishna himself, the lord of the world.
For what king on earth, Bhimasena, would dare to insult me today, as this
disgrace of his family does, unless his soul were in the grip of fate? For this
strong-armed man is surely a portion of Hari's energy, and far-famed Hari wants
to take it back. That is why the Chedi king, tiger of the Kurus, roars like a
tiger, the fool, and thinks nothing of us all."

The Chedi king could not bear Bhishma's words, and he spoke to Bhishma again in
fury.

"May our enemies have the power Keshava has, Bhishma, whose praises you sing,
always standing up like a bard. If your mind always delights in praising others,
Bhishma, if you must praise kings, leave Janardana. Praise this Bahlika, Darada,
the best of kings, who split this earth when he was born. Praise Karna here,
Bhishma, lord of the lands of Vanga and Anga, a match for the thousand-eyed god
in strength, who draws a great bow. Praise Drona and his son, Bhishma, the
father and son, great chariot fighters, the best of the twice-born, who are
always worth praising. Either one of them, Bhishma, if he were angry, could
leave this earth with nothing on it, moving or unmoving. That is what I think. I
see no king equal to Drona in battle, Bhishma, or to Ashvatthama, and yet you do
not want to praise them. Why do you not praise Shalya and the other kings,
Bhishma, if your mind is always set on praising?

"What can I do, king, when you have surely never listened to the old men who
spoke of dharma? 'Blaming oneself and praising oneself, blaming others and
praising others: these four things the noble never do.' You go on praising
Keshava, who should not be praised, in folly, out of devotion, and no one agrees
with you, Bhishma. How can you place the whole world, just because you want to,
in this wicked man, the Bhoja's servant, a herdsman?

"Or perhaps this devotion of yours goes back to your nature, Bharata, as I said
before, like the bhulinga bird. There is a bird called the bhulinga, Bhishma, on
the far side of the Himalaya. Its words are always heard, and they are at odds
with its deeds. 'Do not be rash!' It is always crying this, they say, and it
does not see that it is itself doing a very rash thing. For the foolish bird
picks the shreds of meat stuck between the teeth of the lion, Bhishma, from his
mouth as he eats. It lives only as long as the lion wants, Bhishma, there is no
doubt of it. So you too, who do not know dharma, are always talking. You live
only as long as these kings want, Bhishma, there is no doubt of it. For there is
no one else whose deeds the world hates as much as yours."

When Bhishma heard the Chedi king's bitter words, king, he spoke while the Chedi
king listened. "So I live only as long as these kings want? I do not count these
kings as worth a blade of grass."

When Bhishma said this, the kings were enraged. Some of them bristled, and some
cursed Bhishma. Some of the great archers, when they heard Bhishma's words,
said, "This Bhishma is wicked and proud and old, and he does not deserve
forgiveness. The fool should be slaughtered like an animal by the kings. Or let
us all join together in our anger and burn him in a fire of straw."

When he heard their words, the wise Bhishma, the grandfather of the Kurus, spoke
to those same lords of the earth. "I see no end here to words answering words.
But listen, all of you, lords of the earth, to what I will say. Whether I am
slaughtered like an animal or burned in a fire of straw, I have set my foot on
the heads of you all. Here stands Govinda, Achyuta, whom we have honoured.
Whoever among you is in a hurry to die, let him challenge Madhava, Krishna, who
carries the bow Sharnga and the mace, to battle today. Then let him fall, and
enter this god's body."

---

When he heard Bhishma, the Chedi king of wide-ranging courage wanted to fight
Vasudeva, and he said to Vasudeva, "I challenge you. Come and fight me,
Janardana, so that I may kill you today, with all the Pandavas. For the Pandavas
are all to be killed along with you, Krishna, by every means. They passed over
the kings and honoured you, who are no king. You are a slave, no king, and a
fool, Krishna, and they have honoured you as if you were worthy, when you are
not, out of childishness. I think they should all be killed." With that, the
tiger among kings stood there roaring, unable to bear it.

When he had said this, Krishna spoke gently to all the kings, in front of him
and before the Pandavas.

"Kings, this son of a Satvata woman is our bitter enemy. He is cruel to the
Satvatas and wishes us ill, though we have done him no harm. This man of cruel
deeds knew we had gone to Pragjyotisha, and he burned Dvaraka, kings, though he
is our sister's son. When the princes of the Bhojas were at play on Mount
Raivataka, he fell on them, killed them, bound them all, and went back to his
own city. With wicked purpose, to wreck my father's sacrifice, he stole the
horse let loose for the horse sacrifice, though it was guarded. And he carried
off glorious Babhru's wife, against her will, in his folly, as she was going
from here to the Sauviras. Hidden by magic, the cruel man carried off the
unhappy Bhadra of Vaishali, for Karusha, from his uncle.

"I have borne this very great pain for my father's sister's sake. But by good
fortune, now it happens in the presence of all the kings. For you see today how
far he has wronged me. As for what he did out of my sight, learn it from me. But
this offence of his today I cannot forgive, in his arrogance, before the whole
circle of kings, for he deserves death. The fool wanted Rukmini, when he was
bent on dying. But the fool did not get her, as a shudra does not get to hear
the Veda."

When they heard Vasudeva's words, all the kings together began to condemn the
king of Chedi. But when he heard them, mighty Shishupala laughed out loud.
Laughing, he said, "Krishna, how are you not ashamed to talk of Rukmini, who was
mine first, in assemblies, and above all before kings? What man who thought
himself a man, Madhusudana, would ever talk among good people of a woman who had
been another's first, except you? Forgive me if you have the faith to, Krishna,
or do not forgive me. Angry or pleased, what can you do to me?"

As he was still speaking, the blessed Madhusudana, the scourge of enemies, cut
off his head in anger with his discus. The strong-armed man fell like a mountain
struck by a thunderbolt.

Then the kings saw a great light rise out of the body of the king of Chedi,
great king, like the sun rising from the sky. Then that light bowed to
lotus-eyed Krishna, whom the worlds honour, and entered him, lord of men. All
the lords of the earth thought it a marvel, when they saw that light enter the
strong-armed Krishna, the highest of beings. When Krishna had killed the Chedi
king, rain fell from a cloudless sky, blazing thunderbolts fell, and the earth
shook.

Then some of the kings said nothing at all. That was a time beyond words, and
they only watched Janardana. Others pressed their fingertips into their palms,
unable to bear it. Others, beside themselves with anger, bit their lips with
their teeth. Some of the kings praised the Varshneya in private. Some were
enraged, and others stood aside. The great seers were delighted, and came to
Keshava, singing his praises, and so did the great-souled brahmins and the
mighty kings.

The Pandava said to his brothers, "Perform the last rites for Damaghosha's son,
the hero, the king, with honour, and quickly." And the brothers did as their
brother commanded. Then the son of Pritha, with those lords of the earth,
consecrated Shishupala's son as king and lord of the Chedis.

---

Then the Kuru king's sacrifice, rich in everything, shone out, king, a delight
to the young, full of great energy. Its troubles were over and its course was
happy. It was rich in wealth and grain, with plenty of food and many good things
to eat, and Keshava guarded it. So the great rite of the Rajasuya was brought to
its end. The strong-armed, blessed Janardana, Shauri, carrying the bow Sharnga,
the discus and the mace, guarded the sacrifice until it was over.

Then, when Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, had bathed in the final bath, the
whole company of kshatriya kings came to him and said, "By good fortune you
prosper, you who know dharma. You have won the rule of all kings, lord. You have
made the fame of the Ajamidhas grow, and by this deed, king of kings, you have
done great dharma. We have been honoured with all we could wish, tiger among
men, and we take our leave. We will go to our own kingdoms. Give us your leave."

When he heard the kings' words, Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, honoured the
kings as they deserved, and said to all his brothers, "All these kings came to
us out of friendship. Now, tamers of enemies, they have taken leave of me and
are setting out for their own kingdoms. Bless you, go with these best of kings
to the edge of our land."

At their brother's word, the Pandavas, who followed dharma, went with each of
the chief kings in turn, as was fitting. Mighty Dhrishtadyumna quickly went with
Virata. Dhananjaya, the great chariot fighter, went with the great Yajnasena.
Mighty Bhimasena went with Bhishma and Dhritarashtra, and Sahadeva, the great
chariot fighter, with the hero Drona and his son. Nakula went with Subala and
his son, king. The sons of Draupadi, with the son of Subhadra, went with the
kings of the mountains. So too the bulls of the kshatriyas went with the other
kshatriyas. Honoured in this way, they left, and all the brahmins left too.

When all the kings had gone, bull of the Bharatas, mighty Vasudeva said to
Yudhishthira, "I take my leave of you, joy of the Kurus. I will go to Dvaraka.
By good fortune you have performed the Rajasuya, the best of sacrifices."

When he said this, the Dharma King answered Madhusudana. "It is by your grace,
Govinda, that I have performed the sacrifice. By your grace the whole company of
kshatriya kings has come under my power and waited on me, bringing the finest
tribute. Without you, hero, we can have no joy here at all. But you must go to
the city of Dvaravati."

When he was told this, righteous Hari, of wide fame, went with Yudhishthira to
Pritha and said to her gladly, "Your sons have won the rule of all kings today,
aunt. They have gained what they set out to gain, and they are rich. Be happy.
With your leave, I would go to Dvaraka." Keshava took his leave of Subhadra and
Draupadi too.

He came out of the women's quarters with Yudhishthira, bathed, said his prayers,
and had the brahmins speak blessings. Then Daruka yoked his fine chariot, like
the best of clouds, and brought it round, great king. When the great-minded,
lotus-eyed one saw the chariot standing ready, with its banner of Garuda, he
walked round it, keeping it on his right. He mounted it and set out for the city
of Dvaravati.

Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, went after mighty Vasudeva on foot, with his
brothers. Then Hari, the lotus-eyed one, held the fine chariot still for a
moment and said to Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, "Always be watchful, lord of
the people, and guard your subjects. As all creatures live on the rain, as birds
live on a great tree, as the immortals live on the thousand-eyed god, so let
your kin live on you."

When Krishna and the Pandava had made this pact with each other, they took leave
of each other and went back to their own homes.

When Krishna, the best of the Satvatas, had gone to Dvaravati, king, only King
Duryodhana and Shakuni, the son of Subala, stayed on in that heavenly hall,
those two bulls among men.

**Divergences.**

* **The hundred offences.** In the CE Krishna promises his aunt to forgive a hundred of
  Shishupala's offences (2.40.22), but nobody counts them. No one says the hundredth has been
  reached, and Krishna gives his own reason for killing him: he will not forgive this offence
  "before the whole circle of kings" (2.42.14). The count reaching a hundred is only in
  rejected lines: \*398, and \*401, where Krishna tells the kings the hundred is full. See
  ledger L-113.
* **The birth.** The CE has the three eyes and four arms, the donkey's bray, the voice and the
  sign on Krishna's lap (2.40.1–17). See ledger L-114.
* **The discus.** The CE says only that Krishna cut off his head with the discus (2.42.21).
  Krishna thinking of the discus and it coming to his hand is in \*397 and \*401. The popular
  detail of Krishna's finger bleeding as the discus leaves it, and Draupadi binding it with
  her sari, is not in the CE, in its apparatus for this passage, or in Ganguli. See ledger
  L-115.
* **Krishna's childhood deeds.** Shishupala mocks the bird, the horse and the bull, the cart,
  Govardhana and Kamsa (2.38.7–11). The longer list with the two trees and the serpent is \*367
  and \*369. Bhishma's own account of these deeds, which Shishupala says he has heard "again",
  is not in the CE's 2.33–36.
* The vulgate adds a battle between Shishupala's army and the Yadavas before the killing
  (App. I No. 24, \*396, App. I No. 28), a gift of ten million coins to the brahmins (\*406),
  and Karna and Duhshasana staying in the hall with Duryodhana (\*419). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 2.37.11 *adhokṣajaḥ*: Adhokshaja, a name of Vishnu, kept as a name.
* 2.37.14 *caturvidhānāṃ bhūtānāṃ*: "beings of the four kinds", those born from wombs, eggs,
  sweat and shoots.
* 2.38.2 *tṛtīyāyāṃ prakṛtau vartatā*: "living as one of the third sex". Ganguli reads this as
  celibacy. The insult matches 2.38.24 and 2.39.8.
* 2.38.7 *śakuniḥ*: "a bird". *aśvavṛṣabhau*: "the horse and the bull". The text gives no
  names.
* 2.38.9 *valmīkamātraḥ*: "the size of an anthill".
* 2.38.10: the eating on the mountain top is quoted as Bhishma's words. The CE has no speech
  by Bhishma that says it.
* 2.38.25 *na hi te sevitā vṛddhāḥ*: "You cannot have sat with the elders". The sense is
  uncertain.
* 2.38.29–37 *haṃsa*: "goose". The bird lives by the sea and eats eggs.
* 2.38.40: the verse (*gāthā*) is given in short lines. *antarātmani vinihite*: "with your
  true self hidden away". *patraratha*: "bird". The sense is uncertain.
* 2.39.4: Jarasandha offered the foot water first, and Krishna would not take it. The syntax
  is uncertain, and Ganguli reads it the same way.
* 2.39.5 *vikṛtaṃ kṛtam*: "twisted even that", as Ganguli, "refused".
* 2.39.13 *mahāsenam iveśvaraḥ*: "as the lord Shiva caught Mahasena", Skanda.
* 2.40.14 *yādavīm … svasāraṃ pituḥ*: the queen is the Yadava sister of Krishna's father.
  The CE does not name her here.
* 2.41.8 *daradaṃ … bāhlīkam*: "this Bahlika, Darada", as Ganguli. The sense is uncertain.
* 2.41.17 *bhojasya puruṣe vargapāle*: "the Bhoja's servant, a herdsman". The Bhoja is Kamsa.
* 2.41.31 *mūrdhni vo nyastaṃ mayedaṃ sakalaṃ padam*: "I have set my foot on the heads of you
  all".
* 2.42.6 *sātvatīsutaḥ*: "son of a Satvata woman".
* 2.42.8 *bhojarājanyān*: "the princes of the Bhojas".
* 2.42.10 *sauvīrān pratipattau*: "as she was going … to the Sauviras". Ganguli names Babhru as
  Akrura. The CE here does not.
* 2.42.11 *karūṣārthe … mātulasya*: "for Karusha, from his uncle". Ganguli reads "the intended
  bride of king Karusha". The sense is uncertain. *tapasvinīm*: "unhappy".
* 2.42.22 *tejaḥ*: "light", the same word as the "energy" of 2.37.11 and 2.41.3.
* 2.42.31: the son is not named here.
* 2.42.36 *ājamīḍhājamīḍhānām*: "the Ajamidhas", the line of Ajamidha.
