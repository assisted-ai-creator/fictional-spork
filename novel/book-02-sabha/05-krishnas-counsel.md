# Krishna's Counsel

When he had heard the seer's words, Yudhishthira sighed. He thought about how to
achieve the Rajasuya, and he could find no peace, Bharata. He had heard of the
greatness of the great royal seers, and he saw how those who sacrifice win
heavenly worlds by their holy deeds. Above all he saw the royal seer
Harishchandra shining, a man who had sacrificed. And he wanted to perform the
Rajasuya sacrifice.

Then Yudhishthira honoured all who sat in the hall, and they all honoured him in
return, and he set his mind on the sacrifice. The bull of the Kurus, king of
kings, turned his mind towards performing the Rajasuya, thinking about it again
and again. And again, as he went on guarding dharma, he of wonderful strength
and courage set his mind on what would be good for all the worlds. The best of
all who know dharma was kind to all his people, and he did good to all of them,
without distinction. So the people trusted him as they would a father. No one
hated him, and so he was Ajatashatru, the one who has no enemy.

The best of speakers called his ministers and brothers together and asked them,
again and again, about the Rajasuya. When they were asked, the ministers all
spoke together, and said this to the wise Yudhishthira, who wanted to sacrifice.

"The rite by which a consecrated king reaches the standing of Varuna, that is
the rite by which, though he is already a king, he seeks the whole standing of
an emperor. You are worthy of the standing of an emperor, joy of the Kurus, and
your friends think the time has come for your Rajasuya. The time for that
sacrifice lies in your own hands, through the wealth of your kshatriyas. In it,
priests of strict vows build the six fires with the chants. He who offers the
ladle offerings and so wins all the sacrifices, and is consecrated at the end of
the sacrifice, is called the conqueror of all. You are able, strong-armed one.
We are all at your command. Great king, set your mind on the Rajasuya without
hesitating."

So all his friends spoke, each alone and all together. The Pandava, the slayer
of enemies, heard their words, which were right, bold, welcome and excellent,
and he took them to heart, lord of the people. Hearing his friends' words, and
knowing what he himself was able to do, he set his mind again and again on the
Rajasuya, Bharata.

The wise king took counsel again with his brothers, with the great priests, with
Dhaumya and Dvaipayana and the rest, and with his ministers. "How can this wish
of mine for the Rajasuya, the fine sacrifice fit for an emperor, come true? I
have faith in it, and I say so."

When the king said this to them, lotus-eyed one, they said this to the righteous
Yudhishthira at the proper time: "You are worthy of the Rajasuya, the great
sacrifice, you who know dharma."

When the priests and seers had said this to the king, his ministers and brothers
approved their words. But the wise king Partha, master of himself, thought it
over once more within himself, wanting the good of the worlds. A wise man who
looks at what he is able to do, at place and time, at cost and gain, and weighs
them well with his mind before he acts, does not come to grief. Knowing that the
undertaking of a sacrifice does not concern only oneself, he bore the task with
care.

To decide what to do, he thought of Krishna, Janardana, Hari, as higher than all
the world. The Pandava reasoned from his deeds, which were like a god's, that he
was beyond measure, strong-armed, unborn, and born among men by his own wish.
*There is nothing he does not know, nothing he cannot do, nothing he cannot
bear.* So he thought of Krishna. When Partha Yudhishthira had come to that firm
decision, he sent a messenger at once to the teacher of all beings, as to an
elder. Going quickly in a swift chariot, the messenger reached the Yadavas, and
found Krishna, who lived in Dvaraka, in Dvaravati.

Wanting to see Partha, who wanted to see him, the unfallen one went to
Indraprastha with Indrasena. Janardana passed through many lands, hurrying, with
swift horses, and came to Partha in Indraprastha. In the house the Dharma King
honoured him like a brother, and Bhima too. Then he saw his father's sister,
full of love. He was glad, and he enjoyed himself with his dear friend Arjuna,
and the twins waited on him as on an elder.

When the unfallen one had rested in a fine place, and was at leisure and at
ease, the Dharma King came to him and told him what he wanted.

"I have wished for the Rajasuya," said Yudhishthira. "But it is not won by
wishing alone. You know all about how it is won, Krishna. The king in whom
everything is possible, who is honoured everywhere, who is lord of all, he
achieves the Rajasuya. My friends have met and told me I should perform the
Rajasuya. But the final word on it, Krishna, must be yours. For some, out of
friendship, do not point out the faults. And others say only what is pleasing,
for their own gain. Some want only what is pleasing, and what is good for
themselves. That is how people's advice is usually found to be, where there is
something to gain. But you are beyond these motives, and beyond desire and
anger. You should tell us truly what is best for us in the world."

---

"You are worthy of the Rajasuya, great king, by every quality," said Krishna.
"But though you know it all, I will say something, Bharata.

"The kshatriyas left by Rama Jamadagnya were lesser men, and what is now called
the kshatriya class in the world descends from them. The kshatriyas have made
this resolution about their families, lord of the earth, by words of command.
You know this, bull of the Bharatas. They say that the root stock is the line of
Aila and the line of Ikshvaku, and that the kings bound in their ranks, and the
other kshatriyas on earth, come from them. Know that the kings of the Aila line
and the kings of the Ikshvaku line make up a hundred and one families, bull of
the Bharatas. The line of Yayati's Bhojas is very great and widespread, great
king, and it reaches in all four directions. All the kshatriyas honour their
glory. But he has taken the middle land, and set his mind on dividing us.

"There is another king, lord of a quarter of the earth, in whom those hundred
and one families have met: that is Jarasandha, who has won sovereignty by his
birth. All the kings have taken refuge with him, wise king. The mighty
Shishupala has become his general, king. Vakra, the mighty lord of the Karushas,
who fights with magic, waits on him like a pupil, great king. Two other great
and mighty warriors, Hamsa and Dibhaka, have taken refuge with mighty
Jarasandha. So have Dantavakra, Karusha, Kalabha and Meghavahana. Then there is
the king who wears on his head the heavenly gem they call the Jewel of Beings.
He rules Mura and Naraka, the two lords of the Yavanas, and his strength is
without limit, like Varuna's in the west. He is Bhagadatta, great king, your
father's old friend. He bows to Jarasandha in word and deed, Bharata. But in his
heart he is bound to you by love, like a father, and he is devoted to you.

"The king who guards the western and southern edges of the earth, your mother's
brother, the hero Purujit, who makes the Kuntis great, he alone, the tamer of
enemies, bows to you out of love. And that fool among the Chedis, whom I did not
kill before, who is known as the Best of Men, has gone over to Jarasandha. He
calls himself the Best of Men in this world, and in his folly he always takes my
emblem. He is the mighty king among the Vangas, Pundras and Kiratas who is
famous in the worlds as Paundraka Vasudeva.

"Then there is the mighty Bhoja, lord of a quarter, Indra's friend. By the
strength of his learning he conquered the Pandyas, the Krathas and the
Kaishikas, and his brother is the hero Ahriti, equal to Jamadagnya in battle.
That king, Bhishmaka, the slayer of enemy heroes, is devoted to the Magadhan. We
are his kin, and good ones, always humble, always doing what pleases him. But
though we are loyal to him, he is not loyal to us, and he stands with those who
wish us ill. He does not know his own family or his own strength, king. He has
seen Jarasandha's blazing fame, and gone to him for shelter.

"The eighteen families of the northern Bhojas, lord, have fled to the west for
fear of Jarasandha. The Shurasenas, Bhadrakaras, Bodhas, Shalvas and
Patachcharas have left the north in fear. So have the Sustharas and Sukuttas,
the Kunindas with the Kuntis, and the kings of the Shalveyas with their brothers
and followers. So have the southern Panchalas and the Kosalas east among the
Kuntis. And the Matsyas and the Samnyastapadas have gone to the south. So too
all the Panchalas, stricken with fear of Jarasandha, have left their own kingdom
and fled in every direction.

"Then, after some time, Kamsa, in his folly, crushed his kin and married two
daughters of Brihadratha's son, two queens named Asti and Prapti, the younger
sisters of Sahadeva. With that strength behind him, the fool overpowered his kin
and made himself their chief. His misrule was very great. The elders of the
Bhoja nobles, oppressed by that wicked man, wanted to save their kin, and they
turned to us. So, when Ahuka's daughter Sutanu had been given to Akrura, I did
what was needed for our kin, with Samkarshana at my side. Kamsa and Sunaman were
killed by me and by Rama.

"But when the danger had passed, Jarasandha rose against us. Then the eighteen
younger families took counsel, king. 'Even if we struck at his army without
rest, with great weapons that kill hundreds, we could not destroy it in three
hundred years.' For he had two warriors, godlike and the strongest of the
strong, named Hamsa and Dibhaka, the best of fighters. Those two heroes
together, and mighty Jarasandha, three of them, were enough for the three
worlds. That is what I think. And it was not only we who thought so, wisest one.
All the other kings thought so too.

"Now there was a great king called Hamsa. He was killed in a battle with others,
by the eighteen families, king, and someone announced, 'Hamsa is dead,' Bharata.
When he heard it, Dibhaka, king, drowned himself in the water of the Yamuna. 'I
cannot live in this world without Hamsa.' With that thought Dibhaka went to his
death. And when Hamsa, the conqueror of enemy cities, heard what Dibhaka had
done, he went to the Yamuna too, and drowned himself in it. When King Jarasandha
heard that the two had died in the water, he went back from the Shurasenas to
his own city, bull of the Bharatas. When the king had gone back, slayer of
enemies, we all lived happily again in Mathura.

"But then Kamsa's wife, his lotus-eyed daughter, went to her father, King
Jarasandha of Magadha. Grieving for her husband's death, king of kings, she
urged him again and again, 'Kill my husband's killer!' Then, great king,
remembering the counsel we had taken before, we left, downcast, lord of men. We
fled in different directions, king, gathering up our great wealth, and fled in
fear of him, with our wealth, our kin and our relations. With this in mind we
all went to the west, to the lovely city of Kushasthali, graced by Mount
Raivata. There we settled again, king, and we fortified it so that even the gods
could not attack it. Even the women could fight in it, let alone the bulls of
the Vrishnis. We live there without fear, slayer of enemies. When they saw that
finest of mountains, and the bathing place of the Madhavi, the Madhavas were
overjoyed, tiger of the Kurus.

"So we, wronged by Jarasandha from the start, have come to you for shelter,
because of our kinship, and we are able to help. Our dwelling is three leagues
long, with three lines of defence beyond each league. At the end of each league
there are a hundred gates, with arches for the brave, and it is packed with the
kshatriyas of the eighteen younger families, mad for battle. There are eighteen
thousand bands of fighting men in our family. Ahuka has a hundred sons, and each
is worth three hundred at least. Charudeshna with his brother, Chakradeva,
Satyaki, I myself, Rohini's son, and Samba, Shauri's equal in battle: these are
seven chariot warriors, king. Hear of the others. Kritavarma, Anadhrishti,
Samika, Samitimjaya, Kahva, Shanku and Nidanta: these are seven great warriors.
The two sons of Andhakabhoja and the old king make ten. They are heroes as firm
as the world, mighty and strong. They remember the middle country, and they live
among the Vrishnis, free of care.

"You, best of the Bharatas, have always had the qualities of an emperor, and you
should make yourself emperor among the kshatriyas, Bharata. But you cannot
achieve the Rajasuya while mighty Jarasandha lives. That is my view, king. For
he has conquered all the kings and shut them up in Girivraja, like great
elephants shut by a lion in a cave of the king of mountains. King Jarasandha
wants to sacrifice with the lords of the earth. He worshipped Mahadeva, and so
he has conquered the kings of the earth. He defeats each king who comes against
him in battle, brings him to his city, binds him, and has made a pen full of
men. And we, great king, for fear of Jarasandha, left Mathura then and went to
the city of Dvaravati. If you want to achieve this sacrifice, great king, strive
to free those kings and to kill Jarasandha. There is no other way, joy of the
Kurus, to carry out the whole undertaking of the Rajasuya, wisest of the wise.
That is my view, king. Or do as you think best, sinless one. Since things are as
they are, decide for yourself, by reason, and tell me."

---

"You have spoken with a wisdom that no one else could," said Yudhishthira.
"There is no one on earth but you who can free us from our doubts. There are
kings in every house, each doing what is good for himself. But they have not won
sovereignty. For the word 'emperor' is hard to earn. How can one who knows the
power of others praise himself? The one who is praised when he meets another is
the one who is honoured. The earth is wide and full of every kind of jewel. One
learns what is best by going far, joy of the Vrishni line. I think peace is
best. But peace would not come from freeing those kings. I do not think the
highest place can be won by undertaking this. That is what men of spirit, born
in good families, think. Someone among them, at some time, may become the
greatest, Janardana."

"A king who undertakes nothing sinks like an anthill," said Bhima, "and so does
a weak one who attacks a strong one without a plan. But a weak man, if he is
tireless, can often defeat a strong enemy by good policy, and win by his policy
what is good for him, king. In Krishna there is policy, in me strength, and in
Partha Dhananjaya victory. Like three fires, we will overcome the Magadhan."

"A fool who is bent on gain takes," said Krishna, "and does not look at what
follows. That is why enemies do not put up with a fool bent on gain. We have
heard that five were emperors: Yauvanashva by giving up taxes, Bhagiratha by
protecting his people, Kartavirya by the power of his austerity, the lord
Bharata by his strength, and Marutta by his wealth. You wish for sovereignty in
every way, Yudhishthira. But Jarasandha, son of Brihadratha, bull of the
Bharatas, has earned by every mark of dharma, wealth and policy the name of one
who must be restrained. Know that. The hundred and one royal families do not
follow him willingly. So he rules as emperor today by force alone. Kings who
hold jewels serve Jarasandha, but even that does not satisfy him, for in his
folly he has taken the path of wrong. He seizes by force a consecrated king, the
leading man of his land, and we have not seen one man spared, anywhere. So
Jarasandha has brought them all under his power, no fewer than a hundred. How
can a weaker king come near him, Partha? What joy can those kings take in life,
sprinkled and cleaned like beasts for sacrifice in the house of the lord of
beasts, bull of the Bharatas? When a kshatriya dies by weapons, he is honoured.
Why should we not all stand against the Magadhan? Jarasandha has gathered
eighty-six kings, king. Fourteen are still to come. Then he will go on to the
cruel deed. Whoever stops him there will win blazing fame. And whoever conquers
Jarasandha will surely be emperor."

"I want the standing of an emperor," said Yudhishthira, "and I am bent on my own
interest. How can I send you, and Bhima, by mere rashness, trusting in force?
Bhima and Arjuna are my two eyes, and I think of Janardana as my mind. What is
life without mind and eyes? Jarasandha's army is terrible in its strength and
hard to get past. Even your efforts would end in defeat. What could you do
there? In this matter, what seems right may turn to harm. Hear what I think, as
I weigh it alone. I think it is better to give up this task, Janardana. The
Rajasuya is hard to achieve, and my mind turns against it today."

Then Partha, who had won the finest bow, the two great quivers that never run
out, the chariot, the banner and the hall, spoke to Yudhishthira.

"I have won the bow, the weapons, the arrows, courage, allies, land, fame and
strength, king, things that are hard to win and that I longed for. The learned
praise birth in a good family, and rightly. But nothing is equal to strength,
and it is courage I like. What will a man without courage do, even if he is born
in a family of courage? A kshatriya's whole way of life, king, lies in defeating
his enemies. A man with courage can overcome his enemies, even if he lacks every
other quality. But what will a man without courage do, even if he has every
quality? All qualities rest in courage as their substance. The causes of victory
are success and effort, which depend on fate. A man with strength may fail to
use it through carelessness, and by that door a strong man is destroyed by his
enemies. Weakness in the strong, and folly in the strong, are both causes of
ruin, and a king who wants victory must give them up. If for the sake of the
sacrifice we destroy Jarasandha and free the kings, what could be greater? If we
do not undertake it, we are sure to be judged without merit. When merit is
beyond doubt, king, how can you think of being without it? The ochre robe is
easy to take up later, for sages who want peace. But we want sovereignty for
you, and we will fight the enemy."

**Divergences.**

* **Krishna and Jarasandha.** In the CE Krishna says the Yadavas fled Mathura for Kushasthali
  (Dvaraka) for fear of Jarasandha, after Kamsa's death (2.13.44–51, 65). The vulgate adds that
  Krishna fought Jarasandha eighteen times without killing him (\*158), and that Jarasandha hurled
  his mace ninety-nine leagues to Mathura (\*197, \*198). Neither is in the CE. See ledger L-109.
* The vulgate names more Yadava warriors, among them Pradyumna and Aniruddha (\*159). The CE's
  lists are given here.
