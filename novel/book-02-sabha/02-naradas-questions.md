# Narada's Questions

Then King Yudhishthira made his entry into the hall. The lord of men fed ten
thousand brahmins with ghee and rice cooked in milk, with honey, with good food,
roots and fruit. He gave them new clothes and garlands of every kind, and he
gave each of them a thousand cows. The cry of "A holy day!" rose there, Bharata,
as if it touched the sky. With music of every kind, with songs and perfumes, the
best of the Kurus worshipped the gods and set up their images. Wrestlers,
actors, cudgel-fighters, bards and heralds attended the great Yudhishthira there
for seven nights. When the Pandava had performed that worship with his brothers,
he enjoyed himself in that lovely hall as Shakra does in heaven.

In that hall the seers sat with the Pandavas, and kings came from many lands and
sat there too. There were Asita Devala, Satya, Sarpamali and Mahashiras,
Arvavasu, Sumitra, Maitreya, Shunaka and Bali. There were Baka Dalbhya,
Sthulashiras, Krishna Dvaipayana and Shuka, and Vyasa's pupils Sumantu, Jaimini
and Paila, and I myself. There were Tittiri and Yajnavalkya, Lomaharshana with
his son, Apsuhomya and Dhaumya, Animandavya and Kaushika, Damoshnisha and
Traivani. There were Parnada, Ghatajanuka, Maunjayana, Vayubhaksha, Parasharya
and the two Sarikas. There were Balavaka, Shinivaka, Sutyapala and Kritashrama,
Jatukarna and Shikhavan, Subala and Parijataka. There was the fortunate Parvata,
and the sage Markandeya, Pavitrapani, Savarni, Bhaluki and Galava. There were
Janghabandhu, Raibhya, Kopavegashravas and Bhrigu, Haribabhru, Kaundinya,
Babhrumali and Sanatana. There were Kakshivat and Aushija, Nachiketa and
Gautama, Painga, Varaha and Shunaka, Shandilya of great austerity, Karkara,
Venujangha, Kalapa and Katha. These sages were devoted to dharma, steady and
masters of their senses. They and many others who had mastered the Veda and its
limbs, the best of seers, sat with the great king in the hall. They told holy
stories. They knew dharma, and they were pure and spotless.

So too the best of the kshatriyas sat with the Dharma King. There was glorious
Munjaketu, great and righteous, and Vivardhana. There were Samgramajit and
Durmukha, and mighty Ugrasena, King Kakshasena, and Kshemaka the unconquered.
There were Kamala, king of the Kambojas, and mighty Kampana, who alone kept the
Yavanas trembling, as the god with the thunderbolt kept the Kalakeya asuras.
There were Jatasura and the king of the Madrakas, Kunti, and Kuninda king of the
Kiratas. There were the kings of Anga and Vanga with Pundraka, and the kings of
the Pandyas and Odras with the king of the Andhras. There were Sumanas king of
the Kiratas, and the lord of the Yavanas, Chanura and Devarata, Bhoja and
Bhimaratha. There were Shrutayudha of Kalinga and Jayatsena of Magadha, Susharma
and Chekitana, and Suratha, the crusher of enemies. There were Ketuman and
Vasudana, the king of Videha, and Kritakshana, Sudharma, Aniruddha and mighty
Shrutayu. There were the unassailable king of Anupa, Kshemajit and Sudakshina,
Shishupala with his son, and the lord of the Karushas. There were the young
princes of the Vrishnis, godlike and unassailable: Ahuka, Viprithu, Gada and
Sarana, Akrura, Kritavarma and Satyaki, Shini's son. There were Bhishmaka and
Ahriti, mighty Dyumatsena, the Kekayas, great archers, and Yajnasena of the
Somakas.

Mighty princes, wearing the skins of the ruru deer, came to Arjuna to learn the
science of the bow. There the princes of the Vrishnis were trained, king:
Rukmini's son, Samba, and Yuyudhana Satyaki. These and many other kings were
there, lord of the earth.

Dhananjaya's friend Tumburu was always there too, and Chitrasena with his
ministers, and gandharvas and apsarases skilled in song and music, masters of
rhythm and beat. The kinnaras, trained in measure and in tempo, and the
gandharvas, urged on by Tumburu, sang together. Those gifted ones sang heavenly
melodies as was proper, and attended the sons of Pandu and the seers, and
delighted them. Seated in that hall, those men of good vows and true promises
attended Yudhishthira as the gods in heaven attend Brahma.

---

As the great Pandavas sat there, Bharata, with great gandharvas seated among
them, the seer Narada came to that hall, wandering through all the worlds. The
divine seer, of boundless splendour and great power, was as swift as thought. He
came with other seers, with Parijata and wise Raivata and gentle Sumukha, glad
to see the Pandavas in their hall.

When he saw the seer Narada come, the eldest Pandava, who knew all dharma, rose
at once with his younger brothers and greeted him with love, bowing humbly. He
gave him a fitting seat, as the rules require, and he honoured him with jewels
and every good thing. Honoured by all the Pandavas, the great seer, who had
mastered the Veda, questioned Yudhishthira about dharma, wealth and pleasure.

"I hope your wealth is well used," said Narada, "and that your mind delights in
dharma. I hope you enjoy your pleasures, and that your mind is not troubled. I
hope, god among men, that you keep to the unfailing way of conduct among men,
joined to dharma and wealth, that your fathers and grandfathers followed. I hope
you do not injure dharma for the sake of wealth, or wealth for the sake of
dharma. I hope you do not injure both for the sake of pleasure, which is only
sweet. I hope, best of victors, that you divide your time. You know times, giver
of boons: I hope you give to wealth, to dharma and to pleasure each its proper
time.

"I hope, sinless one, that with the six qualities of a king you weigh the seven
means, and your own strength and weakness, and the fourteen, as you should. I
hope, best of victors, that you study yourself and your enemies, and then,
making alliances, attend to the eight tasks, Bharata. I hope your six elements
of state are not weakened, bull of the Bharatas, and that they are rich, free of
vice, and loyal to you in every way.

"I hope your counsel is never let out, by your own doing or your ministers',
through guesswork, or through messengers, or through those you do not suspect. I
hope you make peace and war at the right times, and that you behave rightly to
kings who are neutral and kings who stand between. I hope, hero, that you have
made men your counsellors who are like yourself in understanding, pure, able to
give their lives, well born and loyal. For a king's victory has its root in
counsel, Bharata, well guarded by ministers who keep counsel as their wealth and
know the treatises.

"I hope you do not give way to sleep, and that you wake at the right time. I
hope that in the last part of the night you think about what is useful, you who
know what is useful. I hope you do not take counsel alone, and not with too
many. I hope the counsel you have taken does not run through the kingdom. I hope
that when you have settled on undertakings that cost little and yield much, you
begin them quickly, and do not put obstacles in their way. I hope that none of
your works are done out of your sight, where they may be doubted. Nor should all
of them be left to others, for the right way lies in between. I hope, king, that
people know of your works only when they are done, or nearly done, hero, and not
before they are achieved.

"I hope all your teachers, skilled in all the treatises, train your princes and
your chief warriors in every way. I hope you would buy one wise man for a
thousand fools. For in hard times a wise man can bring the greatest good. I hope
all your strongholds are full of wealth, grain, weapons and water, and machines,
and craftsmen and archers. Even one minister who is wise, brave, self-controlled
and shrewd can bring a king or a prince to great fortune.

"I hope, with three spies each, unknown to one another, you watch the eighteen
officers of the enemy and fifteen of your own. I hope that, unknown to your
enemies, always ready and always on guard, you watch all your enemies, slayer of
enemies.

"I hope your family priest is honoured by you, well trained, well born and
learned, free of envy, one who asks the right questions. I hope your priest for
the fires is devoted, knows the rules, and is intelligent and straightforward. I
hope he always tells you in time what has been offered and what is to be
offered. I hope your astrologer is skilled in the limbs of the Veda, expounds
the heavenly bodies, and knows the meaning of every portent.

"I hope your chief servants are given great tasks, your middling ones middling
tasks, and your lowest ones the lowest. I hope you appoint to the best tasks the
best ministers, who have passed every test, men whose fathers and grandfathers
served before them, and who are pure. I hope, bull of the Bharatas, your
ministers do not govern your kingdom with harsh punishments that terrify your
people. I hope the priests at your sacrifices do not despise you as they would
despise a fallen man. I hope they do not despise you, as women despise a man who
presses his desire on them, because you take too harshly.

"I hope your commander is bold and brave, intelligent, steady and pure, well
born, loyal and skilful. I hope all the chief men of your army are skilled in
war, proven in deeds, courageous, and honoured and respected by you. I hope you
give your army its food and pay, as is fitting, when it falls due, and do not
hold it back. For when food and pay are late, the soldiers grow angry with their
master in their hardship, and that is said to be a great disaster. I hope all
the men of good family, above all, are loyal to you. I hope they always lay down
their lives for your sake in battle. I hope no one man, ruled by his own desires
and heedless of your orders, directs as he likes all the business of war. I hope
that a man who does his work well by his own effort wins more honour, or more
food and pay. I hope you honour with gifts, as they deserve, men who are trained
in learning and skilled in knowledge, according to their merit. I hope, bull of
the Bharatas, you support the wives of men who have died for you, or who have
come to ruin in your service.

"I hope, Partha, that you protect like a son an enemy who comes to you in fear,
or who comes in weakness, or who has been beaten in battle. I hope, lord of the
earth, that to the whole earth you are even-handed and above suspicion, like a
mother, like a father.

"I hope, bull of the Bharatas, that when you hear an enemy is in trouble, you
march against him at once. But first weigh the three kinds of strength. Consider
the danger in the rear and at home, your resolve and the chance of defeat. And
pay your army in advance, great king. I hope, tamer of enemies, that you give
hidden jewels to the enemy's chief soldiers in his own kingdom, as they deserve.
I hope, Partha, that you first conquer yourself and master your senses, and then
seek to conquer enemies who are careless and have not mastered theirs. I hope
that before you march against your enemies, conciliation, gifts, division and
force, the four means, go ahead of you, well used. I hope, lord of the people,
that you make your base firm before you march. I hope you strive to conquer your
enemies, and that when you have conquered them, you protect them. I hope your
army of four branches, with its eight parts, well led by its commanders, can
hold back your enemies. I hope, great king, tamer of enemies, that you do not
neglect the enemy's harvest and his famine, but strike your enemies in battle.

"I hope, great king, that many officers of yours in your own kingdom and in the
enemy's look after your affairs and protect one another. I hope trusted men,
with your approval, guard your food, the things that touch your body, and your
perfumes. I hope your treasury, your granary, your mounts, your gates, your
weapons and your revenue are looked after by men who are loyal to you and wish
you well. I hope, lord of the people, that you protect yourself first from those
within and from those without, and them from your own people, and from one
another.

"I hope your servants do not tell you, in the morning, of money spent on your
vices, on drink, dice, sport or women. I hope your spending is covered by half
your income, or by a quarter, or by three quarters. I hope you help your
kinsmen, your elders and the old with wealth and grain. I hope you help the
merchants and craftsmen who depend on you, and those who have fallen on hard
times. I hope all your accountants and clerks, busy with your income and your
spending, report your income and spending to you every morning. I hope you do
not dismiss from their work, without first finding some fault, men who are
experienced in affairs, who wish you well, and who are loyal. I hope, Bharata,
that you know men for the best, the worst and the middling, and appoint them to
work that suits them. I hope no greedy men, thieves, enemies or men without
experience are employed in your work. I hope your kingdom is not oppressed by
greedy men or thieves, by princes or by the women's quarters, or by you
yourself. I hope your farmers are thriving.

"I hope there are great tanks in your kingdom, full of water, spaced about it,
so that farming does not depend on the rain alone. I hope that, as a kindness,
you lend the farmer seed and food when he is in want, at one in a hundred. I
hope, my son, that the business of the country is well run by good men. For this
world prospers happily when it rests on business. I hope, king, that five by
five, men who are brave, wise and able work together for the good of your
country. I hope that for the city's safety the villages are made like towns, and
the guard posts like villages, and all of them answer to it. I hope that former
thieves, with armed forces behind them and officers over them, patrol your land,
the level places and the rough.

"I hope you comfort the women, and that they are well protected. I hope you do
not put your trust in them, and that you do not tell them secrets. I hope that
when you have heard your spies at night, and thought about what must be done,
you go to bed pleasantly, knowing who your people within are. I hope, lord of
the people, that you sleep for the first two watches of the night, and rise in
the last watch to think about dharma and wealth. I hope that you rise at the
proper time, as one who knows times, and always show yourself to your people,
well dressed, with your counsellors, Pandava. I hope men in red, well adorned,
with swords in their hands, stand round you to guard you, tamer of enemies. I
hope, lord of the people, that you deal like Yama with those who deserve
punishment, and rightly with those who deserve honour. Examine them first,
whether they are dear to you or not.

"I hope, Partha, that you drive away bodily illness with medicines and a strict
life, and grief of the mind by serving the old. I hope your physicians, skilled
in the eight branches of medicine, are friendly and loyal, and always look after
your body. I hope, lord of the people, that you never turn away, out of pride or
folly or desire, those who come to you as plaintiffs and defendants. I hope you
never cut off the living of those who depend on you, out of greed or folly, or
out of trust or fondness. I hope the townspeople and the people of your country
never join together against you, bought by your enemies. I hope, Yudhishthira,
that you have crushed a weak enemy by force, and a strong one by counsel, or by
both.

"I hope all the kings of the earth, above all, are loyal to you. I hope they lay
down their lives for your sake, won over by you. I hope your honour for every
kind of learning is given by merit, to brahmins and good men, for your own
highest good. I hope that, standing in the dharma rooted in the three Vedas that
men followed before you, you strive to act as they did. I hope that in your
house brahmins of good qualities eat tasty and good food in your presence, and
receive their gifts. I hope that, with your mind on it and self-controlled, you
strive to perform all the sacrifices in full, the Vajapeya and the Pundarika. I
hope you bow to your kinsmen, your elders, the old, the gods, the ascetics, the
shrines, the trees of good omen and the brahmins.

"I hope your mind is set like this, and your way of life is like this, sinless
one. It gives long life and fame, and looks to dharma, pleasure and wealth. For
when a king lives with his mind set like this, his kingdom does not suffer. When
he has conquered the earth, that king grows in happiness without end.

"I hope that no noble man of pure heart, accused of theft, is put to death out
of greed by men not trained in the treatises. Suppose a thief has been
questioned and caught in the act, seen by those who know the matter, and found
with the proof. I hope, bull among men, that he is not set free for the sake of
money. I hope, Bharata, that when a dispute arises between a rich man and a poor
one, your ministers are not bought with money, and do not see the matter
falsely. I hope you avoid the fourteen faults of a king. They are unbelief,
lying, anger, carelessness, delay, not seeing the wise, laziness and
distraction. They are thinking about affairs alone, and taking counsel with
those who do not understand affairs. They are not starting what has been
decided, letting counsel out, not performing the rites of good omen, and giving
yourself up to the pleasures of the senses. I hope your Vedas bear fruit. I hope
your wealth bears fruit. I hope your wives bear fruit. I hope your learning
bears fruit."

"How do the Vedas bear fruit?" said Yudhishthira. "How does wealth bear fruit?
How do wives bear fruit? How does learning bear fruit?"

"The Vedas bear fruit in the fire offering," said Narada. "Wealth bears fruit in
what is given and what is enjoyed. Wives bear fruit in love and sons. Learning
bears fruit in character and conduct."

When he had said this, the sage Narada, of great austerity, went on to question
the righteous Yudhishthira.

"I hope the merchants who come from far away for profit have the duty taken from
them only as the law lays down, by those who live by duties. I hope, king, that
the men who bring goods to your city and kingdom are respected, and not cheated
by tricks. I hope, my son, that you always listen to the old, whose words join
dharma and wealth. Listen to men who know what is useful and men who look to
dharma. I hope that honey and ghee from your farms, your cattle, your flowers
and fruit are given to brahmins for the sake of dharma. I hope you always give
all your craftsmen their materials and tools, properly, for four months at
least. I hope, great king, that you recognise what has been done, and praise the
man who did it, and honour him with respect in the presence of good men.

"I hope, bull of the Bharatas, that you master all the manuals, lord, the
manuals on elephants, on horses and on chariots. I hope, bull of the Bharatas,
that the manual of the science of the bow, and the manual of machines for
cities, are always practised in your house. I hope, sinless one, that you know
all the weapons, and the brahmin's curse, and all the ways of using poisons that
destroy enemies. I hope you protect your kingdom from the fear of fire, from the
fear of snakes and beasts of prey, and from the fear of disease and rakshasas. I
hope, you who know dharma, that you protect like a father the blind, the dumb,
the lame and the deformed. Protect those who have no kin, and the wandering
ascetics too."

The great bull of the Kurus heard these words of the best of brahmins. He bowed
at his feet and greeted him, and the king said joyfully to godlike Narada, "I
will do as you have said. My understanding has grown still more."

When he had said this, the king did just that, and he won the earth with its
girdle of seas.

"The king who lives like this," said Narada, "protecting the four classes, lives
happily here, and goes to the world of Shakra."
