# Duryodhana in the Hall

While he stayed in that hall, bull of the Bharatas, Duryodhana slowly looked
over the whole of it with Shakuni. In it the joy of the Kurus saw heavenly
designs he had never seen before in the city named after the elephant.

One day King Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, came in the middle of the
hall to a floor of crystal. He took it for water, and in his confusion the king
lifted up his clothes. Then he wandered round the hall, unhappy and turning
away. Then he came to a pool with crystal water, bright with crystal lotuses. He
took it for dry ground, and fell into the water with his clothes on. When they
saw him fall into the water, the servants laughed loudly, and at the king's
command they gave him fine clothes. When they saw him in that state, mighty
Bhimasena, and Arjuna, and both the twins, all of them laughed.

He could not bear their laughter, for he could never bear anything. But he kept
his face still, and did not look at them. Again he lifted his clothes, as if to
wade across, and stepped up on to dry ground, and then all the people laughed
again. He struck his forehead on a door that looked open. At another doorway he
stopped short, thinking the door was shut.

So, lord of the people, he met with tricks of many kinds there. Then King
Duryodhana took his leave of the Pandava. He had seen the wonderful wealth at
the great sacrifice of the Rajasuya, and his heart was not glad as he went to
the city named after the elephant.

As he went, scorched by the Pandavas' glory and sick with brooding, an evil
thought was born in King Duryodhana. He saw the Parthas in good spirits, and the
kings obedient to them, and the whole world, down to the children, well disposed
to them, pride of the Kurus. He saw the great glory of the great-souled
Pandavas, and Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, went pale.

As he went, his mind was on the hall and nothing else, and on the matchless
fortune of the wise Dharma King. Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, was so
lost in it that he did not answer the son of Subala, though Shakuni spoke to him
again and again. When Shakuni saw him so distracted, he said, "Duryodhana, why
do you walk along sighing like this?"

"I have seen this whole earth obedient to Yudhishthira," said Duryodhana,
"conquered by the blazing weapons of the great man with the white horses. I have
seen that sacrifice of Partha's, uncle, as splendid as Shakra's among the gods,
splendid one. I am full of resentment, and it burns me day and night. I am
drying up like a little water when the hot months come.

"Look how Shishupala was struck down by the chief of the Satvatas, and not a man
there went after him. The kings were burning in the fire the Pandavas had lit,
and they let that offence pass. Who could let such a thing pass? That great deed
was done by Vasudeva, wrong as it was, and it succeeded through the might of the
great-souled Pandavas. And the kings bring jewels of every kind and wait on the
king, the son of Kunti, like vaishyas paying tax.

"I have seen such fortune, blazing as it were, in the Pandava's hands, and I am
in the grip of resentment. I burn, and I am not used to it. I will go into the
fire, or eat poison, or go into the water. I cannot live. For what man in the
world, with any spirit, will bear to see his rivals prosper and himself decline?
If I bear it today, when such fortune has come to them, I am neither a woman nor
not a woman, neither a man nor not a man. Who like me would not burn, when he
had seen such lordship over the earth, such wealth, and such a sacrifice?

"I am not able, alone, to take that royal fortune for myself, and I see no
allies. That is why I think of death. When I see that bright fortune come to
Kunti's son like this, I think fate is everything, and a man's own effort is
useless. I tried before to destroy him, son of Subala. He got past all of it,
and grew like a lotus in the water. So I think fate is everything, and a man's
own effort is useless. For the sons of Dhritarashtra decline, and the Parthas
always grow.

"I have seen that fortune, and that hall, and the servants laughing at me, and I
am burning as if in a fire. Give me leave, uncle, for I am in great pain. Tell
Dhritarashtra of the resentment that has taken hold of me."

---

"Duryodhana, you must not resent Yudhishthira," said Shakuni. "The Pandavas
always enjoy their own good fortune. You have set about them before, many times,
by many means, and the tigers among men got free, with their good fortune going
before them. They have won Draupadi for a wife, and Drupada with his sons, and
mighty Vasudeva, as allies in winning the earth. They have gained their father's
share, lord of the earth, and it was not taken from them, and it has grown by
their energy. Why grieve over that?

"Dhananjaya satisfied the fire, and won Gandiva, the two great quivers that
never run out, and heavenly weapons. With that finest of bows, and the strength
of his own arms, he brought the kings under his power. Why grieve over that?
Savyasachin, the scourge of enemies, saved the danava Maya from the burning of
the fire, and had him build that hall. And at Maya's word the terrible rakshasas
called the Kimkaras carry that hall. Why grieve over that?

"As for what you said about having no allies, king, Bharata, that is not true.
These brothers of yours are your allies, great chariot fighters. You have the
great archer Drona, with his wise son, and Radheya, the son of the suta, and the
great chariot fighter Gautama. You have me with my brothers, and mighty
Saumadatti. With all these beside you, conquer the whole earth."

"With you beside me, king, and these and other great chariot fighters," said
Duryodhana, "I will conquer these very Pandavas, if you agree. When they are
conquered, the earth will be mine today, and all the kings, and that hall so
rich in wealth."

"Dhananjaya, Vasudeva, Bhimasena, Yudhishthira, Nakula and Sahadeva, and Drupada
with his sons," said Shakuni, "cannot be conquered by force in battle, even by
the hosts of the gods. They are great chariot fighters and great archers,
masters of weapons, wild in battle. But I know how Yudhishthira himself can be
conquered, king. Listen, and take it up."

"If they can be conquered without risk to our friends and to other great men,"
said Duryodhana, "tell me how, uncle."

"Kunti's son loves dice, and does not know how to play," said Shakuni. "And if
the king of kings is challenged, he will not be able to refuse. I am skilled at
play, and there is no one like me on earth, or in the three worlds. Challenge
Kunti's son to a game of dice. I am skilled with the dice, king, and I will
surely take his kingdom and that blazing fortune from him for you, bull among
men. But tell all this to the king, Duryodhana. If your father gives me leave, I
will conquer him, no doubt of it."

"You tell it to Dhritarashtra, the chief of the Kurus, son of Subala," said
Duryodhana, "as it should be told. I will not be able to say it."

**Divergences.**

* **Who laughed.** In the CE the servants laugh, then Bhima, Arjuna and the twins (2.43.6–7), and
  then "all the people" (2.43.9). Draupadi is not there, and nobody says "a blind man's son is
  blind". See ledger L-17. Duryodhana's later claim that Draupadi laughed is at 2.46.30.
* **The doors.** The CE gives the doors in one verse (2.43.10). The vulgate's fuller scene, with
  Duryodhana reeling from the blow and falling at a second door, is \*424.
* **Shakuni's dice.** In the CE Shakuni says only that he is the best player on earth (2.44.19).
  He says nothing about loaded dice or dice made from bones. See ledger L-18. The vulgate adds a
  line that victory by cheating is possible (\*434). Not in the CE here, though in the game the
  CE says at each throw that Shakuni "resorted to deceit" (2.54.7 and after). See ledger L-116.

**Choices.**

* 2.43.8 *amarṣaṇaḥ*: "for he could never bear anything". The word is his standing epithet
  here.
* 2.43.10: "He struck his forehead on a door that looked open. At another doorway he stopped
  short, thinking the door was shut." The verse does not say "another". This follows Ganguli.
* 2.43.21 *śuciśukrāgame kāle*: "when the hot months come", Shuchi and Shukra.
* 2.43.24 *tathāyuktam*: "wrong as it was". The sense is uncertain.
* 2.43.26 *atathocitaḥ*: "and I am not used to it".
* 2.43.29 *na strī na cāpy astrī na pumān nāpumān api*: kept word for word.
* 2.43.35 *rakṣibhiḥ*: "the servants", literally "the guards".
* 2.44.8 *kiṃkarā nāma rākṣasāḥ*: the rakshasas called the Kimkaras, who carry the hall (as at
  2.3.25).
* 2.44.10 *gautamaḥ*: Kripa. 2.44.11 *saumadattiḥ*: Somadatta's son, kept as the text names
  him.
