summary: Yudhishthira stakes his gold, his chariot, his elephants, his slaves, his armies and his treasure, and Shakuni, resorting to deceit, wins each throw; Vidura begs Dhritarashtra to stop the game and cast off Duryodhana, and Duryodhana abuses Vidura as a traitor; Yudhishthira stakes and loses his wealth beyond counting, his herds, his kingdom, the princes' ornaments, then Nakula, Sahadeva, Arjuna, Bhima and himself; at Shakuni's prompting he stakes Draupadi, the elders cry shame, Dhritarashtra asks again and again what has been won, and Shakuni throws and cries "Won!"

# The Game

"You have beaten me in this throw by trickery alone," said Yudhishthira. "Come,
Shakuni, let us play, staking thousands. Here are a hundred jars, each full of a
thousand gold coins, a treasury that cannot be emptied, and wrought gold beyond
counting. This is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he said this, Shakuni said to the king, "Won!"

"This royal chariot," said Yudhishthira, "is worth a thousand. It is covered
with tiger skin and runs smoothly, with fine wheels and fittings, splendid and
hung with nets of little bells. It is the thundering chariot that brought us
here, the victorious, holy chariot, whose sound is like a cloud or the sea.
Eight fine horses, the colour of ospreys, the pride of the kingdom, draw it, and
no creature that walks the ground can escape them. This is my wealth, king. With
this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have a thousand elephants, son of Subala," said Yudhishthira, "in rut, with
girths of gold, with fine headdresses, with lotus markings and chains of gold.
They are well trained, fit to carry kings, and bear every noise of battle. They
have tusks like chariot poles, and huge bodies, and each has eight
she-elephants. All of them can break down city walls, and they are like
mountains and clouds. This is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

Shakuni, the son of Subala, all but laughing at Partha as he said this, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have a hundred thousand slave girls," said Yudhishthira, "young and
fortunate, with bracelets of shell and armlets, with gold coins at their
throats. They are beautifully adorned, with costly garlands and ornaments and
fine clothes. They are anointed with sandal paste, they wear jewels and gold,
and all of them are dressed in fine cloth. They are skilled at dance and song,
and at my command they wait on the snatakas, the ministers and the kings. This
is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have just as many thousands of slave men," said Yudhishthira, "respectful and
obliging, always dressed in cloaks. They are wise and clever and skilful, young,
with polished earrings. Dishes in hand, they feed guests day and night. This is
my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have just as many chariots," said Yudhishthira, "with golden fittings and
banners, with trained horses and fighters of wonderful skill. Every one of them
draws up to a thousand in pay each month, whether he fights or not. This is my
wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When Partha said this, the wicked man, who had made himself an enemy, Shakuni,
said to Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"There are the horses speckled like partridges," said Yudhishthira, "the
gandharva horses with garlands of gold, that Chitraratha gave, well pleased, to
the bearer of Gandiva. This is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have chariots, carts and horses by the tens of thousands," said Yudhishthira,
"yoked and ready, with draught beasts of every kind. And I have men, chosen by
the thousand from every class, who drink milk and eat fine rice, sixty thousand
of them, all broad in the chest. This is my wealth, king. With this I play
against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have four hundred treasure chests bound with copper and iron," said
Yudhishthira, "each holding five dronas of beaten gold. This is my wealth, king.
With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

---

"Great king, understand what I say to you, and listen," said Vidura, "though
what you hear may not please you. Medicine does not please a man who is dying.
Long ago, as soon as he was born, this evil-minded Duryodhana howled harshly
like a jackal. He is the destroyer of the Bharata line, and he will surely be
the cause of your death. You do not see that a jackal lives in your house in the
shape of Duryodhana. Listen to the words of the poet Kavya, as I tell them.

"The man who gathers honey finds the honey and does not see the drop. He climbs
up, and then he either drowns or falls. This man is drunk on the game of dice,
as on honey, and he does not look. He has made enemies of great warriors, and he
does not see the drop.

"You know, great king, what wrong can be done even among kings. The Andhakas,
the Yadavas and the Bhojas joined together and cast off Kamsa. When, at their
bidding, Krishna, the killer of enemies, had killed him, all his kin rejoiced
for a hundred years. So let Savyasachin, at your command, restrain Suyodhana.
When this wicked man is restrained, let the Kurus live in happiness. Buy these
Pandavas, these peacocks and tigers, with a crow and a jackal, king. Do not
drown in a sea of grief.

"'For the family, give up one man. For the village, give up a family. For the
country, give up a village. For yourself, give up the earth.' So said Kavya, who
knows all, who knows every creature's heart, who is the terror of every enemy,
when the great asuras were giving up Jambha.

"There were birds, they say, that spat out gold, that lived in the forest and
nested in a man's house. Out of greed, king, the man harmed them. Blinded by
greed for gold, scourge of enemies, he killed the birds that could always have
served him, and at once he ruined both his present and his future. Do not betray
the Pandavas, bull of the Bharatas, out of greed for the present. In your folly
you will be sorry later, like the man who killed the birds.

"Take flower after flower from the Pandavas as each one blooms, Bharata, like a
gardener in his garden, caring for the plants again and again. Do not burn them
root and all, like a charcoal burner with the trees. Do not go down to ruin,
with your sons, your ministers and your army. For who could fight the Parthas
when they are together, Bharata? Not even the lord of the Maruts himself, king,
with the Maruts.

"Dice are the root of strife. They lead to division, or to a great war.
Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, has taken them up, and he is making a
fierce enmity. Through Duryodhana's offence all the descendants of Pratipa and
Shantanu, of Bhimasena and of Bahlika will come to grief. In his madness
Duryodhana drives welfare out of the kingdom, as a bull in its madness breaks
off its own horn by force.

"A man may be brave and wise, king. But if he follows another man's mind and
gives up his own sight, he sinks in terrible misfortune, like a man who goes to
sea in a boat steered by a child. Duryodhana plays against the Pandava, and you
are delighted that he is winning. Too much play turns into a fight, and from a
fight comes men's ruin. This attraction of yours will bear fruit below. It is
badly planned, though the plan has grown strong in your heart. Let your
friendship with Yudhishthira bear fruit. Peace is a well-strung bow against the
enemy's plans.

"Descendants of Pratipa and Shantanu, king, listen to the poet's words. Do not
let them pass you by. Put out this terrible blazing fire as it leaps up, without
a war. When the Pandava Ajatashatru, beaten at dice, can no longer hold back his
anger, nor Vrikodara, nor Savyasachin, nor the twins, who will be your island
then, in the tumult?

"Great king, before the game you were the source of wealth, as much as you could
wish for. If you win much wealth from the Pandavas, what good will it do? Win
the Parthas, and they will be your wealth. We know how the son of Subala plays.
The mountain man knows the deceit of dice. Let Shakuni go back where he came
from, Bharata. The mountain man fights with tricks."

"You always boast of other men's fame," said Duryodhana, "and in secret you
despise the sons of Dhritarashtra. We know whom you love, Vidura. You scorn us
as if we were children. A man who wants others is easily known. He mixes blame
and praise like this. Your tongue shows your mind and your heart, and your
hostility speaks louder still. We have taken you on our lap like a snake. Like a
cat, you hurt the one who feeds you. They say nothing is worse than killing
one's master. So, steward, why are you not afraid of evil?

"We have beaten our enemies and won a great prize. Do not speak harshly to us
here, steward. You delight in making common cause with our enemies, and again
and again, in your confusion, you come to hate us. A man becomes an enemy when
he says what cannot be borne. He hides his secret when he praises the enemy. Why
does shame not stop you, living on us as you do? You say what you like here
today.

"Do not despise us. We know this mind of yours. Learn wisdom from the elders.
Guard the fame you have won, Vidura. Do not busy yourself in other men's
affairs. Do not despise us, Vidura, thinking, 'I am the one who acts.' Do not
always speak harshly to us here. I do not ask you what is good for me, Vidura.
Farewell, steward. Do not wound those who are patient with you.

"There is one ruler. There is no second ruler. The ruler rules a man even as he
lies in the womb. I am taught by him, and as I am directed, so I flow, like
water running downhill. The man who breaks a rock with his head, or feeds a
snake, directs his own acts himself. He who tries to teach by force makes an
enemy by it. But a wise man bears with advice given in friendship. He who sets a
fire blazing, and does not run at once to put it out, will not find even the
ashes left anywhere, Bharata. No one should give house room to a partisan of the
other side, steward, one who hates him, least of all one who wishes him harm. Go
wherever you like, Vidura. An unfaithful wife leaves, however kindly she is
treated."

"Tell me, king, what end friendship comes to with those who abandon a man for so
little," said Vidura. "For the minds of kings are fickle. They give comfort, and
then they have a man beaten to death with clubs. You think you are no child,
prince, and that I am the child, you dull fool. But the man who takes someone
into his friendship and afterwards reviles him is the child.

"A fool cannot be led to his own good, any more than a wicked wife in the house
of a learned brahmin. Surely good advice will not please this bull of the
Bharatas, any more than a husband of sixty pleases a young girl. If you want to
hear only what pleases you, prince, in everything you do, good or bad, ask
women, idiots and cripples, and fools like them. It is easy enough, descendant
of Pratipa, to find a man here who says pleasant things. But the man who speaks
words that are unpleasant but good for you is hard to find, and so is the man
who will listen to them. A man may trust in dharma, and say unpleasant things
that are good for his master, without regard for what pleases or displeases him.
Through such a man a king has a true ally.

"Anger does not come from illness, but it is bitter, sharp and burning. It
steals fame, it is harsh and it stinks. The good swallow it, and the wicked do
not. Swallow your anger, great king, and be calm. I always wish fame and wealth
for Vichitravirya's son and his sons. Let it be with you as it may. I bow to
you. And let the brahmins wish me well too. A wise man should not anger snakes
whose eyes are poison. I say this to you with care, joy of the Kurus."

---

"You have lost much of the Pandavas' wealth, Yudhishthira," said Shakuni. "Tell
us, son of Kunti, if you have any wealth that you have not yet lost."

"I have wealth beyond counting, son of Subala," said Yudhishthira, "that I know
of. Why do you ask me about my wealth, Shakuni? Ayuta, prayuta, kharva, padma
and arbuda, shankha, nikharva and samudra: let that be staked here. This is my
wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"Cattle and horses, many milch cows, and goats and sheep beyond counting," said
Yudhishthira, "whatever there is of every kind east of the Sindhu, son of
Subala. This is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"My city, my country, my land, with all the wealth of those who are not
brahmins, and the men who are not brahmins, king, are the wealth I have left.
This is my wealth, king. With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"The ornaments that make these princes shine, king," said Yudhishthira, "the
earrings and gold coins and every ornament they wear: this is my wealth, king.
With this I play against you."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"Dark, young, red-eyed, with the shoulders of a lion and mighty arms, Nakula is
my one stake," said Yudhishthira, "and all that he has."

"Prince Nakula is dear to you, King Yudhishthira," said Shakuni. "Now he is our
wealth. What will you play with next?"

When he had said this, Shakuni took up the dice, and said to Yudhishthira,
"Won!"

"This Sahadeva teaches the laws of dharma," said Yudhishthira, "and he is known
in the world as a learned man. With this prince, who does not deserve it, I play
against you, as though he were not dear to me, though he is dear."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have won these two sons of Madri, king, who are dear to you," said Shakuni.
"But I think Bhimasena and Dhananjaya are dearer to you still."

"You are surely doing wrong," said Yudhishthira, "and you have no regard for
what is right, when you try to divide us, fool, who are all of one heart."

"A drunk man falls into a pit," said Shakuni, "and a careless man walks into a
post. You are the eldest, king, and the best. I bow to you, bull of the
Bharatas. Gamblers, Yudhishthira, when they play, rave like madmen about things
they never see, asleep or awake."

"He takes us across in battle, like a boat," said Yudhishthira. "He is the
conqueror of enemies, the swift prince, the hero of the world. With Phalguna,
who does not deserve it, I play against you, Shakuni."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"I have won this archer of the Pandavas," said Shakuni, "Savyasachin, the
Pandava. Now play with your dear Bhima, king, Pandava, whatever you have left to
stake."

"He leads us," said Yudhishthira. "He leads us in battle, alone, like the
thunderbolt-bearer, the enemy of the danavas. He looks askance, with knitted
brows, great-souled, with the shoulders of a lion, always quick to anger. No man
is his equal in strength. He is the foremost of those who carry the mace, the
crusher of enemies. With that prince, who does not deserve it, I play against
you, king, with Bhimasena."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"You have lost much wealth," said Shakuni, "and your brothers, with the horses
and elephants. Tell us, son of Kunti, if you have any wealth that you have not
yet lost."

"I am left," said Yudhishthira, "the eldest of all my brothers, and dear to
them. If I am won, we will do your work ourselves, now that this disaster has
come on us."

When he heard this, Shakuni, set on his course and resorting to deceit, said to
Yudhishthira, "Won!"

"You have done the most wicked thing," said Shakuni, "in losing yourself. When
there is wealth left, king, to lose oneself is wicked."

When he had said this, the master of dice taunted each of those heroes of the
world in turn, all of them standing there as the stakes he had won.

"There is still your dear queen," said Shakuni, "one stake not yet lost. Stake
Krishna, the princess of Panchala, and win yourself back with her."

"She is neither short nor tall," said Yudhishthira, "neither too dark nor too
fair. Her eyes are red with love. With her I play against you. Her eyes are like
the petals of the autumn lotus, and her scent is the scent of the autumn lotus.
She loves the autumn lotus, and in beauty she is the equal of Shri. She is all
that a man could want in a woman, in kindness, in beauty and in goodness. She is
the last to go to bed and the first to wake. She knows all that has been done
and not done, down to the cowherds and the shepherds. Her face shines like a
lotus, and when she sweats it is like jasmine. Her waist is like the waist of an
altar, her hair is long, her eyes are coppery, and she has little hair on her
body. With such a woman, king, with the slender-waisted, lovely-limbed Draupadi
of Panchala, I play the stake against you, son of Subala."

When the Dharma King said this, Bharata, voices broke from the elders in the
hall. "Shame! Shame!" The hall was in uproar, king, and the kings began to talk
among themselves. Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the others broke out in sweat.
Vidura held his head in his hands and sat as if the life had gone out of him. He
sat with his face down, brooding, hissing like a snake. But Dhritarashtra was
delighted, and asked again and again, "What has been won? What has been won?" He
did not hide his feelings. Karna rejoiced greatly, with Duhshasana and the
others. But tears fell from the eyes of the others in the hall.

The son of Subala did not stop to think. Flushed with victory, wild with pride,
he took up the dice again. "Won!"

**Divergences.**

* **Shakuni's deceit.** At almost every throw the CE says Shakuni, "set on his course and resorting
  to deceit", said "Won!" (2.54.7 and after). It never says how he cheated. See ledger L-116.
* **The order of the brothers.** In the CE Yudhishthira stakes Nakula, then Sahadeva, then Arjuna,
  then Bhima, then himself (2.58.11–28). Shakuni's taunt that the sons of Madri are less dear to
  him than Bhima and Arjuna is in the CE (2.58.16).
* **Draupadi's stake.** It is Shakuni who suggests staking Draupadi, to win Yudhishthira back
  (2.58.31). Yudhishthira's praise of her beauty and goodness as he stakes her is in the CE
  (2.58.32–37).
* **Kamsa.** The CE says only that Vidura's hearers know "what wrong can be done even among
  kings" (2.55.6). The vulgate adds a line about a king who gave up his son for the citizens'
  sake (\*516), and Ganguli translates it.
* The vulgate adds more great numbers to Yudhishthira's stake (\*523, \*524), and names more of
  the elders who wept or wrung their hands (\*527). Not in the CE.

* 2.54.6 *kuraracchāyāḥ*: "the colour of ospreys". Ganguli reads "white as the moon-beam".
  *naiṣām ucyeta padā bhūmim upaspṛśan*: "no creature that walks the ground can escape them",
  as Ganguli.
* 2.54.7 and after *vyavasito nikṛtiṃ samupāśritaḥ*: "set on his course and resorting to

* 2.54.20 *sahasraparamāṃ bhṛtim*: "up to a thousand in pay". The coin is not named.
* 2.54.21 *kṛtavairaḥ*: "who had made himself an enemy".
* 2.54.25: the sixty thousand men are chosen "from every class".
* 2.54.28 *pañcadrauṇikaḥ*: "holding five dronas".
* 2.55.3 *kāvyāṃ giram*: "the words of the poet Kavya", Ushanas, who is quoted at 2.55.11.
* 2.55.4 *prapātam*: "the drop", the cliff below the honey.
* 2.55.6 *asamañjasam*: "what wrong can be done". It could also be the name Asamanjas, a king who
  was cast out, which is how the vulgate takes it. The sense is uncertain.
* 2.55.9 *kākena … kroṣṭukena*: "with a crow and a jackal", that is, with Duryodhana.
* 2.55.11 *jambhatyāge*: "when the great asuras were giving up Jambha".
* 2.56.2 *prātipīyāḥ śāṃtanavā bhaimasenāḥ sabāhlikāḥ*: the families descended from Pratipa,
  Shantanu, Bhimasena (the old king) and Bahlika.
* 2.56.6: the verse is obscure. The rendering follows the words closely. The sense is uncertain.
* 2.56.10 *pārvatīyaḥ*: "the mountain man", Shakuni, from Gandhara.
* 2.57.9 *sa eva tasya kurute kāryāṇām anuśāsanam*: "directs his own acts himself", as Ganguli.

* 2.58.3: the stake is a list of number words, each far larger than the last (ten thousand, a
  hundred thousand or a million, and on). They are kept as words.
* 2.58.5 *anuvarṇānām*: "of every kind". The sense is uncertain. Ganguli reads a vulgate text
  ("from the Parnasa").
* 2.58.7 *abrāhmaṇadhanaiḥ*: brahmins' wealth and brahmins themselves are not staked.
* 2.58.30 *ākṣepeṇa*: "taunted". Ganguli: "spoke … of his having won, one after another, all the
  Pandavas". The sense is uncertain.

* 2.58.33 *śāradotpalasevinyā*: "she loves the autumn lotus". The sense is uncertain.
