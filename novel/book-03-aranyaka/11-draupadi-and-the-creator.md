# Draupadi and the Creator

"I bow to the Placer and the Ordainer," said Draupadi, "who have made you so
deluded. Your mind is set otherwise than on bearing the ways of your fathers and
forefathers. In this world a man does not win fortune by dharma or kindness, by
patience or straightness, nor ever by compassion.

"This unbearable disaster has come upon you, Bharata, though you do not deserve
it, nor do these mighty brothers of yours. For you never, then or now, Bharata,
found anything dearer than dharma, even your life. Your kingdom was for the sake
of dharma, and your life too is for the sake of dharma. The brahmins know it,
and your elders, and the gods too. I believe you would give up Bhimasena and
Arjuna, the sons of Madri, and me, but you would not give up dharma. 'Dharma
protects the king who guards dharma, when it is guarded.' So I have heard from
the noble. But I do not think it protects you. Your mind, tiger among men,
always follows dharma alone and nothing else, as a man's own shadow follows him.

"You never looked down on your equals, let alone on those below you, still less
on those above. When you had won the whole earth, your horn did not grow. You
always serve the brahmins, the gods and the fathers, Partha, with the cries of
svaha and svadha and with worship. The brahmins have always been satisfied by
you with all they wished, Partha, and the ascetics who seek release, and
householders, Bharata. You give vessels of metal to those who live in the
forest. There is nothing in your house that you would not give to brahmins. What
is offered morning and evening at the end of the offering to all the gods, you
give to guests and dependants, and you live on what is left, king. The
sacrifices, the animal offerings, those made for a wish and those made on
occasions, the cooked offerings and the rites of sacrifice, always go on. Even
in this great forest, lonely and haunted by robbers, living away from the
kingdom, your dharma does not flag. You sacrificed with the great sacrifices,
with rich fees: the horse sacrifice, the Rajasuya, the Pundarika and the Gosava.

"Yet, king, with your mind turned round in that crooked defeat at dice, you lost
your kingdom, your wealth, your weapons, your brothers and me. You are straight,
gentle, generous, modest and truthful. How did the mind that comes from the vice
of dice fall on you? My mind is utterly bewildered and my heart is tormented
when I see your sorrow and this misfortune of yours.

"On this they tell an old story, that the world stands in the power of the Lord,
not in its own. The Placer, the Lord, gives all creatures their happiness and
sorrow, their pleasant and unpleasant things, and sets their seed before them.
As a wooden doll, hero among men, moves its limbs when it is worked, so, king,
do these creatures. Pervading all beings like space, Bharata, the Lord ordains
here both good and evil. Like a bird tied by a string, man is bound and
powerless. He stands in the Lord's power, and is master neither of others nor of
himself. Like a jewel threaded on a string, like a bull held by the nose rope,
he follows the Placer's command, made of him and given over to him. Man is never
his own master for a moment. He is like a tree fallen from the bank into the
middle of the stream. The creature is ignorant, and not master of his own
happiness or sorrow. Driven by the Lord, he may go to heaven or to hell. As the
tips of grass go where the strong wind drives them, so all creatures go where
the Placer drives them, Bharata. The Lord moves through all beings, yoking them
to noble deeds or to evil, and no one sees that it is he.

"This body, called the field, is only an instrument of the Placer's, by which
the lord makes a man do deeds with good and bad fruits. See how great is the
power of the magic that the Lord has made, who kills creatures by means of
creatures, deluding them with his own magic. Things seen one way by sages who
see the Veda turn out another way, like gusts of the wind. Men think of things
one way, and the lord makes them and unmakes them another way. As one might
split wood with wood, stone with stone and iron with iron, lifeless and without
will, so the blessed god, the self-born great grandfather, kills creatures by
creatures, Yudhishthira, working under cover. The lord joins them and parts them
as he likes. The blessed one plays with creatures like a child with toys.

"The Placer does not act towards creatures like a father or a mother, king. He
acts as if in anger, like any other person. When I see noble men of good
character and modesty worn down in their living, and ignoble men happy, I am, as
it were, beside myself with thought. When I see this misfortune of yours, and
the prosperity of Suyodhana, I blame the Placer, Partha, who looks on so
unequally. What fruit does the Placer reap from giving fortune to the son of
Dhritarashtra, who breaks the noble teachings, cruel, greedy, and a despiser of
dharma? If a deed follows its doer and reaches no one else, then surely the Lord
is stained by that evil deed. And if the evil deed that is done does not reach
its doer, then power alone is the cause here, and I grieve for the weak."

---

"We have heard what you have said, Yajnaseni," said Yudhishthira, "lovely,
finely phrased and smooth. But what you say is unbelief. I do not act, princess,
in search of the fruit of dharma. I give because it should be given. I sacrifice
because it should be sacrificed. Whether there is a fruit here or not, Krishna,
I do as far as I can what a man who lives in a house must do. I practise dharma,
lovely-hipped one, not for the sake of its fruit, but not going beyond the
traditions, and looking to the conduct of the good. My mind, Krishna, is set on
dharma by its own nature.

"The man who wants to milk dharma does not win its fruit, nor does the
evil-minded man who, having done it, doubts it out of unbelief. Do not doubt
dharma out of excess of talk and pride. The man who doubts dharma is bound for
birth as a beast. For the weak man to whom dharma, or the word of the seers, is
doubtful, the undying, ageless world is barred, as the Veda is barred to the
shudra. The righteous king should set among the elders a man who studies the
Veda, devoted to dharma, born of a good family, glorious lady. But the fool who
goes against the teachings and doubts dharma is worse than shudras, worse above
all than thieves.

"You have seen with your own eyes the great ascetic, the seer Markandeya,
boundless of soul, going by, who has won long life by dharma. Vyasa, Vasishtha,
Maitreya, Narada, Lomasha and Shuka, and the other seers and siddhas are pure in
heart through dharma alone. You see them with your own eyes, endowed with divine
yoga, able to curse and to bless, weightier even than the gods. These men, like
the immortals, who see the traditions directly, always speak of dharma first to
me as the thing to be done. So, good lady, you must not revile the Placer and
dharma, or doubt them, with a mind bewildered by passion.

"The man who doubts dharma finds no authority in anything else. He takes himself
for his authority, and in his conceit he despises the good. The fool thinks that
only what is bound up with the pleasure of the senses, what the world sees, is
real, and in anything else he is lost. There is no atonement for the man who
doubts dharma. The wretched sinner broods, and does not reach the worlds. The
fool who goes beyond the authorities, who reviles the meaning of the Vedas and
the teachings, a slave of desire and greed, goes to hell. But the man of settled
mind, who always follows dharma without doubting, good lady, wins the endless in
the next world. The fool who oversteps the authority of the seers and does not
guard the dharmas, going beyond all the teachings, finds no peace in all his
births.

"Krishna, do not doubt the dharma that the cultured have practised, the ancient
dharma taught by seers who know all and see all. Dharma alone, Draupadi, and
nothing else, is the raft for those who go to heaven, as the ship is the only
raft for the merchant who would cross the ocean. If the dharma practised by the
righteous had no fruit, blameless one, this world would sink into bottomless
darkness. Men would never reach release. They would live the life of beasts.
They would meet only with ruin and gain nothing at all. If austerity and
celibacy, sacrifice and study, giving and straightness had no fruit, those of
old and those before them would not have practised dharma. If rites had no
fruit, it would be the greatest fraud. Why would the seers and the gods, the
gandharvas, asuras and rakshasas, lords as they are, practise dharma with care?
It is because they knew the Placer to be the giver of fruit, sure in what is
good, that they practised dharma, Krishna. That is the eternal dharma.

"This dharma bears fruit. Dharma is never said to be fruitless. The fruits of
learning and of austerity can be seen. Consider your own birth, Krishna, as you
have heard it, and you know how mighty Dhrishtadyumna was born. That example
alone is enough, bright-smiling one. The steadfast man is content with little,
knowing that deeds bear fruit. The ignorant are never content, even with much,
the foolish. For them there is no happiness or deed after death that comes from
dharma.

"The fruiting of good and evil deeds, their rise and their passing, spirited
lady, are secrets of the gods. No one knows them. These creatures are bewildered
by them. They are to be guarded by the gods, for the gods keep their magic
hidden. The twice-born whose bodies are lean, who keep good vows, whose sins are
burned away by austerity, and whose minds are clear, see these things. Dharma
must not be doubted, nor the gods, because one does not see the fruit. One
should sacrifice without neglect, and give without envy. 'Deeds have fruit.'
This, Krishna, is eternal dharma, as Brahma told his sons, and as the seer
Kashyapa knows.

"So let your doubt vanish like mist, Krishna. Settle it that all this is so, and
give up the frame of mind of unbelief. Do not revile the Placer, the Lord of
creatures. Learn from him, bow to him, and do not let your mind be like this.
Krishna, do not speak ill in any way of the highest god. By his grace the mortal
devoted to him goes to immortality."

---

"I do not despise dharma, Partha, and I do not blame it in any way," said
Draupadi. "How could I despise the Lord, the lord of creatures? Know, Bharata,
that I say this in my pain, as I lament. I will lament more. Listen to me with a
kind mind.

"Surely whoever is born here must act, scourge of enemies. Only things that do
not move live without action, not other creatures. From drinking at the mother's
breast to going to their last bed, moving creatures win their living by action,
Yudhishthira. Among moving creatures, men above all want to win their living by
action, here and after death, bull of the Bharatas. All creatures know about
effort, Bharata. They eat the fruit of their deeds before their eyes, and the
world is witness to it. I see that creatures live by their own effort. Even the
Placer and the Ordainer do, like the crane here in the water.

"Do your own work. Do not lose heart. Arm yourself with action. The man who
knows what must be done may be one in a thousand, or there may be none. He must
work to make it grow and to keep it. Even the Himalaya wastes away if it is
eaten without being added to. All creatures would perish if they did not work.
And we see men work even when the work bears no fruit. For they know no other
way of living in the world.

"Both the man who relies on fate and the man who talks of chance are the lowest.
The man whose mind is on action is praised. The man who sits and relies on fate,
and sleeps at ease without stirring, sinks, the fool, like an unbaked pot in
water. So too the man who believes in chance, and sits idle when he is able to
act, does not live long. He lives weak, like a man who has no protector. If a
man gains something unexpectedly, people think it came by chance, for it came by
nobody's effort. Whatever a man gains by what is called fate, by divine
ordinance, Partha, that is settled to be fate. The fruit a man wins himself by
his own deed, which is seen with the eye, is known as human effort. Another man,
acting by his nature, wins things without a cause. Know that as the fruit of
nature, best of men. So what a man wins by chance, by fate, by nature and by
action is the fruit of his former deeds.

"For the Placer too, the Lord, divides and ordains for men the fruit of what
they did before, by these causes, and that is his own work. Whatever good or
evil a man does, know it is ordained by the Placer, the rising of the fruit of
former deeds. This body is the Placer's instrument in every deed. As he drives
it, so it acts, helplessly. The great lord is the one who sets it to its tasks,
son of Kunti, and he makes all creatures act, helpless though they are. A man
settles his aims in his mind, then wins them by action. So the steadfast man
himself is the cause there, acting with forethought.

"Deeds cannot be counted, bull among men. The making of houses and cities comes
from men. The steadfast man knows by his wits that there is oil in sesame, milk
in the cow and fire in wood, and the means to bring it out. Then he sets to work
with the means to bring it out, and creatures live on the success of those deeds
here. We can tell which work was well done by a skilled maker, and which by an
unskilled one. If a man were not the cause of what is achieved by action, the
fruits of sacrifices and good works would not be his. There would be no pupil
and no teacher. A man is praised when his work succeeds because he is the doer,
and blamed when it fails. How then can action be worthless here?

"Some say everything comes by chance, some say by fate, some by a man's own
effort. So it is said to be threefold. Others think that this is not enough.
They say all of it is unseen, fate and chance alike. For a train of gain is seen
to come both from chance and from fate. A man wins his fruit partly from fate,
partly from chance, and partly from his own deeds. There is no fourth cause
here. So say the skilled, who know the truth. In the same way, if the Placer did
not give creatures their wanted and unwanted fruits, no creature would be
wretched. Whatever aim a man worked for would bear fruit, if there were no deeds
done before. Men who do not see that success and failure come by three doors are
no better than the common run.

"'Action must be done.' That is the conclusion of Manu. For the man who does
nothing at all comes to ruin. For the man who acts, success mostly comes,
Yudhishthira. The lazy man never finds success of any kind. If success does not
come, the cause must be looked for, and a remedy is found. When the deed is
done, king of kings, a man is free of his debt. Misfortune comes to the lazy man
who lies asleep. The able man, who wins his fruit without doubt, enjoys
prosperity. Steadfast men who love action, with doubt cast aside, choose to face
loss that lies in the balance. Nothing is ever certain. Loss is certain for us
now. But it will not be certain, if you stand firm to act. Or success may come,
and your greatness, and that of Vrikodara, of Bibhatsu and of the twins.

"Whether others' deeds bear fruit, or ours, is known in the long run, as the
doer knows the fruit. The farmer breaks the earth with the plough and sows the
seed, and then sits quiet. The rain is the cause there. If the rain does not
favour him, the farmer is not to blame. He thinks, 'I have done all that another
man could do. If it bears us no fruit, the fault is not ours.' A steadfast man
thinks it over like this, and does not blame himself. 'I act, and no success
comes to me.' A man must not lose heart at this, Bharata. There are two outcomes
of action, success or failure. Not acting is something else.

"A deed succeeds when many things come together. When some quality is lacking,
the fruit is less, or there is none. But when nothing is begun, no fruit is seen
and no quality, unfallen one. The steadfast man, as far as he can and as his
strength allows, joins with his wits the right place and time, the right means,
and good omens, for well-being and growth. It must be done without neglect.
Courage is the guide. In all the business of action, courage is the greatest
thing, the whole of it.

"If a steadfast man sees another who is greater than himself in many qualities,
he should seek his aim from him by conciliation, and set him to work for it. Or
he should wish for his misfortune, or his ruin, Yudhishthira, even if he were an
ocean or a mountain, let alone a mortal. A man who is always ready to rise, and
looks for other men's weak points, frees himself of his debt to others and to
himself. A man should never despise himself. There is no prosperity for the man
who despises himself, Bharata. So success is established in this world, Bharata.
The way to success is said to be many-sided, according to the divisions of time
and state.

"My father once kept a learned brahmin in his house. He told my father all this,
bull of the Bharatas. He taught my brothers the policy that Brihaspati taught,
and I heard their talk then, at home. He spoke kindly to me, king, when I came
on errands and sat listening on my father's lap, Yudhishthira."
