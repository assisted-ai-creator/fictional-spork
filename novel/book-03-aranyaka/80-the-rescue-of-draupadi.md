# The Rescue of Draupadi

When they had roamed in every direction and killed deer, boars and buffaloes,
the Parthas, the finest archers on earth, who had gone their separate ways, came
together again. That great forest was full of deer and beasts of prey and loud
with birds. Yudhishthira heard the cries of the beasts and birds and said to his
brothers, "These beasts and birds are facing the quarter lit by the sun and
crying cruelly. They tell of fierce trouble, or of an insult from enemies. Turn
back quickly. Enough of our hunting. My mind aches and burns. The lord of life
in my body is stirred up, angry, and it clouds my understanding. The Kamyaka
seems to me like a lake whose snake has been carried off by Suparna, like a
kingdom without a king, its fortune taken, like a pot whose liquor has been
drunk by drunkards."

The heroes among men were carried towards the hermitage by great Saindhava
horses, faster than the rush of the wind, yoked to fine big chariots. As they
turned back, a jackal came up on their left and howled loudly. The king thought
about it and said to Bhima and Dhananjaya, "This low-born jackal has come up on
our left and howls. By that it is very clear that the wicked Kurus have scorned
us and made a violent attack."

As they were entering that forest, after hunting in the great wood, they saw a
girl weeping. She was their beloved's maid, the daughter of her nurse. Indrasena
ran to her at once, jumped down from his chariot and rushed up. In his distress,
king, he said to the nurse's daughter, "Why are you weeping, fallen on the
ground? Why is your face dry and pale? I hope the king's daughter Draupadi has
not been carried off by cruel, wicked men, she of faultless beauty and wide
eyes, who is equal to the very bodies of the bulls of the Kurus. If the queen
had gone into the earth, or up to heaven, or into the sea, the Parthas would
follow her track, so much is the Dharma King in pain. What fool would want to
carry off the dearest one of such men, crushers of enemies, able to bear
hardship, never beaten, as dear to them as life, like a peerless jewel? He does
not know that she has protectors here today, that she is the Pandavas' heart
walking outside them. Whose body will the terrible sharp arrows pierce today and
go into the earth? Do not grieve for her, timid one. Know that Krishna will come
back today. The Parthas will kill all their enemies together, and then be with
Yajnaseni again."

Then the nurse's daughter wiped her lovely face and said to Indrasena the
charioteer, "Jayadratha has carried off Krishna by force, scorning the five who
are like Indra. There are his fresh tracks, and the broken branches have not yet
wilted. Turn and follow quickly, for the king's daughter has not gone far. Put
on your great, fine armour, all of you who are like Indra. Take your costly bows
and arrows and follow her track quickly. Before she is bewildered by threats and
blows, and in her confusion, with her face drained, gives her body to someone
unworthy, like a ladle full of the best butter poured on ashes. Go before the
offering is poured on a fire of chaff, before the garland is thrown away in a
cremation ground. Go before the soma at the sacrifice is licked by a dog when
the brahmins are careless, before the jackal wades into the lotus pool after
hunting in the great forest. Let no one who does evil touch your beloved's
lovely face, with its fine nose and lovely eyes, clear and bright as the moon's
light, as a dog would take the sacrificial cake. Follow these tracks quickly. Do
not let the time slip past you here."

"Good woman, be quiet," said Yudhishthira. "Hold your tongue. Do not say harsh
things in front of us. Kings or kings' sons who are drunk with strength come to
be deceived."

---

When he had said this much, they set off quickly, following those same tracks.
Again and again they hissed like snakes and twanged the strings of their great
bows. Then they saw the dust of that army, raised by the horses' hooves, and
Dhaumya among the foot soldiers, shouting to Bhima, "Run at him!" The princes
comforted Dhaumya, their spirits sunk low, saying, "Go easy." Then they rushed
at that army at speed, like hawks at meat. Their courage was like great Indra's,
and they were enraged at the insult to Yajnaseni. When they saw Jayadratha, and
their beloved standing in his chariot, their anger blazed up. Vrikodara and
Dhananjaya, the twins and the king, great archers, shouted at the king of the
Sindhus, and the enemy lost all sense of direction.

Then there was a most terrible noise in the forest among the unforgiving
kshatriyas as they saw Bhimasena and Arjuna. When he saw the tops of the banners
of the bulls of the Kurus, the wicked King Jayadratha lost his nerve, and said
to Yajnaseni, who shone in his chariot, "Here come five great chariots. I think
these are your husbands, Krishna. You know them, lovely-haired one. Tell us
which Pandava stands in each chariot."

"What use is it to you to know the great archers, fool," said Draupadi, "when
you have done this most terrible deed, which will cost you your life? These
heroes, my husbands, have come together, and not one of you will be left alive
in the battle. But since you ask, I must tell you all of it, for you are about
to die. That is dharma. I feel no pain and no fear of you, now that I see the
Dharma King with his younger brothers.

"At the tip of one banner two sweet, well-made drums are sounding, Nanda and
Upananda. That one knows the settled truth of his own dharma and of wealth, and
people with business always follow him. He is pure gold in colour, with a
prominent nose, slender and long-eyed. They call him the best of the Kurus,
Yudhishthira son of Dharma, my husband. That hero among men who walks in dharma
would give his life even to an enemy who came to him for shelter. Run to him
quickly, fool, for your own good, with joined palms and your weapons laid down.

"And the one you see standing in the chariot, strong-armed, like a tall sal
tree, biting his lip, his brows knit in a frown, is my husband named Vrikodara.
Fine, strong, well-trained thoroughbreds carry the hero. His deeds are beyond
men, and the name Bhima, 'the terrible', has gone out for him through the earth.
Those who wrong him are never left alive, and he never forgets an enmity. He
goes on until he has made an end of it, and even then he does not grow very
calm.

"Gentle, generous, steadfast, glorious, the master of his senses, who serves the
old, a hero among men, Yudhishthira's brother and his pupil: this is my husband
named Dhananjaya. He would never give up dharma for desire, or fear, or greed,
nor do a cruel thing. This is Kunti's son, with the splendour of fire, who bears
down enemies and crushes them.

"Here is one who knows the settled truth of all dharma and wealth, who takes
away the fear of the frightened. They call his beauty the finest on earth, and
all the Pandavas protect him. He is dearer to them than life, and devoted to
them. This hero is Nakula, my husband. The one who fights with the sword, whose
hand is quick and skilled, great and wise, without equal, is Sahadeva. You will
see his deeds today, fool, like Shatakratu's in battle among the armies of the
daityas. Brave, trained in weapons, intelligent and wise, he does what pleases
the king, the son of Dharma. He shines like the moon and the sun, the youngest
of the Pandavas and dear to them. No man is his equal in understanding, and he
speaks well among the good and knows what is settled. This is Sahadeva my
husband, brave and always unforgiving, wise and learned. He would give up his
life or walk into fire, but he would never say anything against dharma. He is
always high-minded, devoted to the dharma of the kshatriya, and dearer than life
to Kunti, a hero among men.

"You will see this army of yours, all its warriors killed, broken up by the sons
of Pandu, like a ship full of jewels breaking up on a sea monster's back at the
edge of the ocean. These are the sons of Pandu I have told you of, whom you
scorned in your folly and set out against. If you get away from them with your
body unharmed, you will have been born again while still alive."

Then the five Parthas, like five Indras, angry, left the frightened foot
soldiers, who stood with joined palms, and closing in on the chariot force from
every side, they made it dark with a rain of arrows.

---

"Stand! Strike! Quickly, surround them!" So the king of Sindhu urged on his
kings. Then there was a most terrible noise in the battle among the troops as
they saw Bhima, Arjuna, the twins and Yudhishthira. Despair came on the Shibis,
the Sindhus and the Trigartas when they saw those tigers among men, fierce in
their strength like tigers.

Bhima took up his mace, made all of iron, lofty and chased with gold, and rushed
at the Saindhava, who was driven on by Time. But Kotikashya turned in between
and struck at him, surrounding Vrikodara with a great troop of chariots. Though
he was showered by many with spears, lances and iron arrows thrown by the arms
of heroes, Bhima did not tremble. With his mace he killed an elephant with its
rider, and fourteen foot soldiers, at the head of the Saindhava's army. Partha,
trying to reach the Sauvira, killed five hundred brave mountain chariot fighters
at the head of the army. The king himself killed a hundred of the best Suvira
fighters in the blink of an eye in that battle. Nakula was seen there, jumping
down from his chariot, sword in hand, sowing the heads of the rearguard again
and again like seed. Sahadeva drove his chariot against the elephant fighters
and brought them down with iron arrows, like peacocks from trees.

Then the Trigarta got down from his great chariot, bow in hand, and killed the
king's four horses with his mace. As he came near on foot, King Yudhishthira,
the joy of Kunti, the Dharma King, pierced him in the chest with a
crescent-headed arrow. His heart split, the hero vomited blood from his mouth
and fell facing Partha, like a tree cut at the root. The Dharma King, his horses
killed, jumped down from his chariot with Indrasena and got onto Sahadeva's
great chariot.

Kshemankara and Mahamukha took aim at Nakula and rained showers of sharp arrows
on him from both sides. As they poured arrows on him like clouds in the rains,
the son of Madri killed each of them with a single long arrow. Then Suratha,
king of the Trigartas, who knew how to ride elephants, came up to the front of
his chariot and had an elephant hurl it over. But Nakula, fearless, sword and
shield in hand, leapt from the chariot, took a safe place, and stood firm like a
mountain. Suratha, in anger, sent that great elephant at Nakula to kill him,
with its trunk raised high. As the elephant came near, Nakula cut off its trunk
at the root, with the tusk, with his sword. Trumpeting loudly, the elephant with
its bracelets fell head down on the ground and crushed its riders. When the
brave son of Madri had done that great deed, the great chariot fighter reached
Bhimasena's chariot and found shelter.

As King Kotikashya came on in the battle, Bhima cut off the head of his driver,
who was urging on the horses, with a razor-headed arrow. The king did not
realise that the strong-armed one had killed his driver, and his horses, without
a driver, ran here and there over the field. As he turned away with his driver
dead, Bhima, the Pandava, best of strikers, came up and killed him with a spear.

Dhananjaya cut off with sharp broad-headed arrows the bows and the heads of all
twelve Sauviras. The great chariot fighter killed the Shibis, the chiefs of the
Ikshvakus, the Trigartas and the Saindhavas who came within the range of his
arrows in that battle. Many were seen struck down by Savyasachin, elephants with
their flags and great chariot fighters with their banners. Headless bodies and
bodiless heads lay covering the ground all over the battlefield. Dogs, vultures,
herons, ravens, kites, jackals and crows had their fill of the flesh and blood
of the heroes killed there.

When those heroes had been killed, Jayadratha, king of Sindhu, let Krishna go in
terror and set his mind on flight. In the confusion of the army he set Draupadi
down, and the lowest of men ran off into the forest the way he had come, wanting
to save his life. When the Dharma King saw Draupadi, with Dhaumya before her, he
had the heroic son of Madri take her up into the chariot.

Then, when Jayadratha had gone, Vrikodara struck down that fleeing army with
iron arrows, picking out man after man. But when Savyasachin saw Jayadratha
running away, he held Bhima back from killing the Saindhava's soldiers. "I do
not see Jayadratha on this field," said Arjuna, "though it is through his wrong
that this bitter trouble has come on us. Look for him, bless you. What use to
you are these warriors you have struck down? This is a deed with no gain in it.
What do you think?"

When wise Gudakesha had said this, Bhimasena, who spoke well, looked at
Yudhishthira and said, "Their heroes are killed, king, and most of the enemy
have fled in every direction. Take Draupadi, king, and go back from here. With
the twins and the great Dhaumya, king of kings, reach the hermitage and comfort
Draupadi. That fool the king of Sindhu will not escape me alive, even if he is
in the depths of Patala and Shakra is his charioteer."

"Strong-armed one," said Yudhishthira, "you must not kill the Saindhava, wicked
as he is. Remember Duhshala, and glorious Gandhari."

Draupadi heard this with her senses in turmoil. Angry, modest and wise, she said
to her two husbands, Bhima and Arjuna, "If you would do what pleases me, that
lowest of men must be killed, the vile, wicked, evil-minded Saindhava, the shame
of his family. An enemy who carries off another's wife, and one who seizes a
kingdom, does not deserve to live, even if he begs for it in battle."

At this the two tigers among men went off to where the Saindhava was. The king
turned back, taking Krishna with him, and the household priest. He entered the
hermitage and saw it with its seat-mats and water-pots thrown about, full of
Markandeya and the other brahmins. The very wise one met the brahmins, gathered
there grieving for Draupadi, together with his wife, in the midst of his
brothers. They were delighted to see the king come back, having beaten the
Sindhus and Sauviras and brought Draupadi back again. Surrounded by them, the
king sat down right there, and lovely Krishna went into the hermitage with the
twins.

Bhima and Arjuna heard that the enemy was only a krosha ahead, and they drove
their horses on themselves and raced after him. Here the man Arjuna did a most
wonderful thing: he killed the Saindhava's horses when they were a krosha away.
Armed with divine weapons, not flustered even in a hard moment, he did that hard
deed with arrows charged with the spells of weapons. Then the two heroes, Bhima
and Dhananjaya, ran at the Saindhava, frightened and alone, his horses killed,
his mind in turmoil. When the Saindhava saw his horses killed, and saw
Dhananjaya doing his deeds of valour, he was in misery. Set on flight, he ran
off towards the forest.

Seeing the Saindhava striving to escape, strong-armed Phalguna followed him and
said, "With this courage, how can you want to carry off a woman by force? Turn
back, prince. Running away does not become you. How can you leave your followers
and run off in the midst of your enemies?"

Though Partha said this, the Saindhava did not turn back. Mighty Bhima suddenly
rushed at him, shouting, "Stand! Stand!" But Partha, who was merciful, said to
him, "Do not kill him."

---

When Jayadratha saw the two brothers with weapons raised, he ran off quickly and
steadily, in misery, wanting to live. Mighty Bhimasena, unforgiving, jumped down
from his chariot, ran after him as he ran and seized him by the hair. He lifted
him up in his rage and ground him into the ground. He took the king by the
throat and beat him. When he came to life again and tried to get up, crying out,
the strong-armed one kicked him on the head. Bhima dropped his knee on him and
struck him with his elbow, and the king fainted, crushed by those great blows.

Phalguna held back Bhimasena, whose anger was not yet spent, saying, "Think of
what the king said about Duhshala, Kaurava."

"This low man," said Bhimasena, "whose conduct is wicked, does not deserve to
live at my hands. He has tormented Draupadi, who did not deserve it. But what
can I do, when the king is always merciful, and you always hinder us with your
childish thinking?"

When he had said this, Vrikodara shaved his head into five tufts with a
crescent-headed arrow, while he said nothing. Having shamed the king, Vrikodara
said to him, "If you want to live, fool, hear the terms I lay down. You must say
in assemblies and in halls, 'I am a slave.' On these terms I will give you your
life. This is the law for one beaten in battle."

"So be it," Jayadratha said to that tiger among men, Bhima, who shone in battle,
his life hanging by a thread. Then Partha Vrikodara bound him as he writhed,
unconscious and covered in dust, and put him in the chariot. Bhima, with Partha
behind him, mounted the chariot, came to the hermitage and went up to
Yudhishthira, who was sitting in the middle of it.

Bhima showed Jayadratha to him in that state, and the king laughed when he saw
him and said, "Let him go." Bhima said to the king, "Tell Draupadi. This
wicked-minded man has become the Pandavas' slave." His elder brother said to him
with affection, "Let this man of low conduct go, if we mean anything to you."
Draupadi looked at Yudhishthira and said to Bhima, "He is the king's slave. Let
him go. You have given him five tufts."

When he was freed, the king went to King Yudhishthira and greeted him. Shaken,
he bowed to him and to all the sages there. Then the merciful King Yudhishthira,
son of Dharma, seeing Jayadratha seized like this by Savyasachin, said to him,
"Go free. You are no slave. Never do such a thing again. Shame on you, lusting
after women, mean man with mean companions. What other lowest of men would do
such a thing?"

The best of the Bharatas looked at the doer of evil, who seemed to have lost all
spirit, and the king took pity on him. "May your mind grow in dharma. Do not set
your heart on what is against dharma. Go in peace, Jayadratha, with your horses,
your chariots and your foot soldiers."

At these words the king went off, ashamed, silent, his face turned a little
down, deep in misery, to Gangadvara, Bharata. There he went for shelter to the
god with the strange eyes, Uma's husband, and performed great austerities. The
god with the bull on his banner was pleased with him. The three-eyed one, well
pleased, took his offering himself and gave him a boon. Hear what he chose.

"May I beat all five Pandavas with their chariots in battle," said the king to
the god. "No," said the god. "You will hold them back in battle, though they
cannot be beaten or killed, except strong-armed Arjuna, whom even the gods
cannot face. He is the god they call the unconquered, who holds the conch, the
discus and the mace. He is the foremost of those who know weapons, and he is
protected by Krishna."

When this had been said to him, the king went back to his own home. And the
Pandavas lived on in that Kamyaka forest.

**Divergences.**

* **"For one day."** The popular story is that Shiva let Jayadratha hold back all the Pandavas except Arjuna for a single day. In the CE the boon has no such limit, here (3.256.27–29) or where it is told again before Abhimanyu's death (7.41.15–17). The limit is in rejected passages at both places (\*1228; 7.\*303). See L-153.
* **Shiva's Pashupata and Vishnu's glory.** Rejected passages here (\*1224–\*1227, @27) have Shiva say he gave Arjuna the Pashupata and praise Krishna at length as Vishnu, Nara and Narayana. Not narrated.
* **The battle.** A rejected appendix passage (@26) describes Arjuna's arrows burning the army like a sacrifice, and \*1214 names the twelve Sauviras again. Not narrated.
* **Jayadratha hiding.** A rejected passage (\*1218) has Jayadratha throw down his crown and hide in a thicket. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1204–\*1213, \*1215–\*1217, \*1219–\*1223) add small details. Not narrated.
