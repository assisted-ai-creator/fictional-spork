# The Serpent

"When that best of chariot fighters had won the weapons and come back from the
house of the slayer of Vritra," said Janamejaya, "what did the Parthas do next,
together with the hero Dhananjaya?"

Those lords of men, the heroes, took their pleasure in those same forests with
Arjuna, who was like Indra. They took it on that lovely finest of mountains, in
the pleasure grounds of the Lord of Wealth. Looking at those matchless dwellings
and pleasure grounds among trees of many kinds, the archer Kiritin, a lord of
men, roamed about a great deal, always intent on his weapons. Having found a
dwelling there by the grace of King Vaishravana, the sons of the king did not
long for other creatures, king, and that was a happy time for them. When they
had met the Partha, four years passed there like a single night. With the six
before, those were ten happy years for the Pandavas living in the forests.

Then the swift son of the Wind, and Jishnu, and the heroic twins, like the king
of the gods, sat down by the king in a lonely place. They spoke to him for his
good and his pleasure. "King of the Kurus, wanting to make your promise come
true and to do what pleases you, we will follow you when we leave the forests to
kill Suyodhana and his followers. This is the eleventh year we are living here,
deserving happiness, our happiness taken by Suyodhana. Let us outwit that man of
low mind and conduct and spend our time unknown in comfort. At your command,
king, we will roam the forests free of care and put aside pride. We will be in
places so far away that they will not know us, though they are tempted to think
us living near. When we have spent that year in hiding, we can root out that
lowest of men with ease. We will pay back our enmity, in fruit and flower, to
Suyodhana, the lowest of kings, surrounded by his followers. Then take back the
earth, Dharma King.

"Roaming this mountain, which is like heaven, you can drive away your grief, god
among men. But your fame, fragrant with merit, would be lost in the worlds of
moving and unmoving things, Bharata. When you have won the kingdom of the bulls
of the Kurus, you can win great things and do the rites. What you have from
Kubera you can always have, lord of men. Set your mind on killing your enemies,
Bharata, and on restraining those who have done wrong. Not even the wielder of
the thunderbolt himself could bear your fierce splendour if he met it, king.
Those two will never waver, Dharma King, not even meeting the gods: he whose
banner is Suparna, and the grandson of Shini, who have set out to win your
cause. As Krishna has no equal in strength, king, neither has that hero of the
Shinis. As Krishna has set out with the Yadavas to win your cause, so have we
two, best of kings, and the heroic twins, skilled in the use of weapons. We have
our strength from our bond with your cause, and when we meet the enemy we will
make an end of them."

When the great son of Dharma, best of all, who knew dharma and wealth, of the
highest power, understood their thought, he walked round Vaishravana's dwelling,
keeping it on his right. The Dharma King took leave of the dwellings, the
rivers, the lakes and all the rakshasas, looking at the road by which he had
come and looking back again at the mountain. Steady of mind, he made up his
mind. "When my work is done, and I have conquered my rivals with my friends and
won back the kingdom, I will see you again, king of mountains, for austerity."

Surrounded by all his younger brothers and the brahmins, the lord of the Kurus
went back by the same road. And Ghatotkacha carried them again with their
followers over the mountain streams. As they set out, the great seer Lomasha
counselled them all gladly, like a father his sons, and went gladly to the
holiest dwelling of the dwellers in heaven. Counselled by him and by
Arshtishena, the Parthas, the best of men, went on, looking at lovely fords and
hermitages of austerity and other great lakes.

---

The bulls of the Bharatas took no joy in leaving their happy dwelling. It was
the best of mountains, full of springs, of the elephants of the quarters, of
kinnaras and birds. Then they were full of joy again when they saw Kailasa, the
mountain loved by Kubera, bright as a cloud. The heroes looked at the heights
and the passes between the mountains, the mountain meadows and the chains of
mountain ridges. They looked at the many waterfalls and the high places and the
low ones here and there. They looked too at other great forests, full of beasts,
birds and elephants, and went on in good heart, the best of men, archers bearing
swords. The lovely forests, lakes and rivers, the caves of the mountains and the
mountain hollows were always their dwelling, night after night.

After living many days in hard places, they passed Kailasa, of form beyond
thought, and came to the hermitage of Vrishaparvan, most lovely, finest of
hermitages. They met King Vrishaparvan and he honoured them, and, free of care,
they told Vrishaparvan all about their happy travels at length, just as it was.
They stayed there happily for one night in that holy hermitage, where gods and
great seers go, and then the heroes came easily back to wide Badari to live. The
great-spirited best of men lived there at Narayana's place, free of grief,
looking at the lotus pool loved by Kubera, where gods and siddhas go. The sons
of Pandu, bravest of all men, saw that lotus pool, free of grief. They took
their pleasure, as brahmin seers free of fear do when they come to live in
Nandana.

Then the heroes, all together, went on step by step by the road they had come.
When they had spent a happy month at Badari, they came to the country of Subahu,
king of the Kiratas. They passed the land of the Chinas, the Tukharas, the
Daradas and the Darvas, and the countries of the Kuninda, rich in jewels. They
crossed the hard region of the Himalaya, and the heroes saw Subahu's city. When
King Subahu heard that the sons and grandsons of kings had all come to his
country, he came out gladly to meet them, and the bulls of the Kurus greeted
him. They met King Subahu, and all the charioteers led by Vishoka, with
Indrasena and the servants, the stewards and those of the kitchens. They stayed
there happily for one night. Then they took the charioteers and all the
chariots, sent Ghatotkacha away with his followers, and went on to the king of
mountains from which the Yamuna comes.

On that mountain, full of springs, whose slopes were red and white, with snow
for an upper garment, the heroes came to Vishakhayupa and made their dwelling
there. It was a great forest like Chaitraratha, full of boars and beasts and
birds of many kinds. They travelled happily and, given above all to hunting,
they took their pleasure a year in that forest.

There, in a mountain cave, Vrikodara came upon a mighty serpent, tormented by
hunger, fierce in form like Death, and his heart was shaken with despair and
bewilderment. There Yudhishthira, best of those who uphold dharma, became an
island for Vrikodara. He of boundless splendour freed him when his whole body
was wound in the serpent's grip.

As the twelfth year came on, the Kurus left that forest like Chaitraratha in
good heart to take their pleasure in the forest, blazing with glory and joined
to austerity. Then they went by the edge of the desert, always delighting above
all in the science of the bow. They came to the Sarasvati, wanting to live
there, and from that lake they went to Dvaitavana. When the people who lived in
Dvaitavana saw them settled there, they came to them, joined to austerity,
self-control, good conduct and meditation, carrying grass, water pots and
grinding stones. Plaksha, aksha, rauhitaka and cane, snuhi, jujube, khadira and
shirisha, bilva, ingudi, pilu, shami and karira grew on the banks of the
Sarasvati. The sons of the king roamed gladly and took their pleasure happily by
the Sarasvati, loved by yakshas, gandharvas and great seers, like a place of
sacrifice for the gods.

---

"Bhimasena was mighty, with the breath of ten thousand elephants," said
Janamejaya. "How did he come to feel such sharp fear of that serpent, sage? He
challenged Paulastya, the Lord of Wealth, to battle, proud of his strength, and
made a slaughter of the finest yakshas and rakshasas at the lotus pool. You say
that the harrier of enemies was overcome by fear and brought low. I want to hear
this, for my curiosity is very great."

Those fierce archers were living in that forest full of wonders, having come
from the hermitage of the royal seer Vrishaparvan, king. Then Vrikodara, as it
happened, bow in hand and sword bound on, saw that lovely forest where gods and
gandharvas go. Then he saw the lovely places of the Himalaya mountain, where
divine seers and siddhas go and hosts of apsarases wander. Here and there they
rang with chakoras and chakravakas, jivajivaka birds, cuckoos and bhringarajas.
They had trees always in flower and fruit, soft with the touch of snow, with
thick shade, delighting mind and eye. He looked at the mountain rivers, with
water like beryl, cold to the touch, full of swans and ducks. He looked at the
deodar forests, like nets for the clouds, mixed with yellow sandalwood and tall
kaliyaka. The mighty one roamed about hunting over the level desert places,
piercing deer with clean arrows.

He saw a huge serpent that made the hair stand on end. It lay in a mountain
stronghold, its body filling a cave. Its body was as big as a mountain's flank,
with coils marked like the discs of the moon and sun, its bright body spotted
like a deerskin, its colour like turmeric. Its mouth was like a cave, shining,
with four fangs. Its eyes blazed, deep copper-red, and it licked the corners of
its mouth again and again. It terrified all creatures, like Yama, Time and the
Ender, and it lay as if threatening with the hissing of its breath.

That snake, the python, the seizer, very hungry, came suddenly at Bhima and
seized him by force by both arms. As soon as it touched him, Bhimasena's senses
were suddenly bewildered by the boon it had been given. The strength that ten
thousand elephants have was in Bhimasena's arms, unmatched by others. But the
splendid Bhima, overpowered by that serpent, twitching slowly, could not move.
Mighty-armed, with the shoulders of a lion and the breath of ten thousand
elephants, he was seized and lost his spirit, bewildered by the boon. He made a
fierce effort to free himself, but the hero could not resist it at all.

---

Bhimasena, full of splendour, in the power of the serpent, thought about the
serpent's most wonderful strength. He said to the great serpent, "Tell me, if
you will, snake, who are you, best of serpents, and what will you do with me? I
am Bhimasena the Pandava, next after the Dharma King. How have you brought me
into your power, when I have the breath of ten thousand elephants? Lions,
tigers, buffalo and elephants have met me often in battle, and I have killed
them. Danavas, pishachas and mighty rakshasas cannot bear the force of my arms,
best of serpents. Is this the strength of some knowledge, or a boon given you,
that you have brought me into your power though I struggle? My mind is made up
that men's courage is false, since you have broken my great strength like this,
snake."

As the hero Bhima, tireless in his deeds, said this, the serpent wound its great
coil all round him. Holding the strong-armed one, the serpent then let go of his
thick arms and said, "How fortunate that the gods have given you to me today as
food in my hunger, strong-armed one. How fortunate, over a long time. For to
living beings life is dear. I must tell you today how I came to be a serpent,
tamer of enemies. Listen, good man. I have come to this state through the anger
of the wise. Longing for the end of the curse, I will tell you of the serpent.

"You have surely heard of the royal seer named Nahusha. He was a forefather of
your own forefathers, the son of Ayus, the maker of the line. I am he. Through
Agastya's curse, and because I scorned the brahmins, I have come to this state.
See this fate of mine. You have come to me, and you should not be killed, and
you are very fair to see, but today I will eat you. See how things are ordained.
Nothing that comes into my grip, elephant or buffalo, is ever set free at the
sixth watch of the day, best of men. You have not been seized by a mere serpent
living as an animal, best of the Kauravas. This is the boon given me.

"As I was falling quickly from the top of the aerial car, from Shakra's throne,
I asked the blessed best of sages, 'Make an end of the curse.' Full of pity, the
splendid one said to me, 'You will be freed, king, in the course of time.' Then
I fell to earth, and my memory did not leave me. The ancient lore I learned
stays with me just as I learned it. The seer said to me, 'Whoever can answer the
questions you put to him, knowing the fine points, will free you from the curse.
And any creature you seize, king, even one stronger, even one greater, will
quickly lose his strength.' I heard these words of those merciful ones, who had
come to feel kindly towards me, and then the brahmins vanished. So I, whose
deeds were most evil, live in this unclean hell, having come to the womb of a
serpent, waiting for the time, you of great splendour."

Strong-armed Bhimasena said to the serpent, "I am not angry with you, great
serpent, and I do not blame myself. When a man is powerless over his joy and
sorrow, whether they come or go, he should not let his heart fail in them. Who
can turn back fate by human effort? I think fate is supreme, and human striving
is useless. Look at me: I relied on the strength of my arms, and by a blow of
fate I have come to this state here today for no reason.

"But today I do not grieve so much for myself, destroyed like this, as for my
brothers, left in the forest, fallen from the kingdom. And this Himalaya is very
hard to cross, full of yakshas and rakshasas. Looking for me, they will stumble
in their distress. Or, hearing I am destroyed, they will lose all heart. For
they are righteous, and it is I, greedy for the kingdom, who have held them to
it. Or else wise Arjuna will not despair. He knows every weapon, and gods,
gandharvas and rakshasas cannot withstand him. That strong-armed one, so mighty,
could throw even the king of the gods from his place in a single day by his
power. How much more the son of Dhritarashtra, the player of crooked dice, hated
by all the world, given to deceit and greed.

"And I grieve for my poor mother, greedy for her sons, who always wishes us
greatness beyond others. Now that I am destroyed, snake, how will all her hopes
in me come to nothing, helpless as she is? Nakula and Sahadeva, the twins,
obedient to their elders, always kept steady by the strength of my arms and
proud of their manhood. They will lose heart, their courage and strength gone,
wretched at my death. That is what I think."

So Vrikodara lamented much at that time, bound by the serpent's coils, and he
could not move.

But Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, grew troubled, thinking of the terrible omens
that foretold harm. A female jackal, standing to the south, in the blazing
quarter, frightened, howled a harsh, ill-omened howl at his hermitage. A
terrible quail, with one wing, one eye and one foot, was seen facing the sun,
vomiting blood and crying harshly. A rough, fierce wind blew, dragging gravel,
and all the cries of beasts and birds were on the left. A black crow behind him
cawed, "Go! Go!" His right arm twitched again and again, and his heart and his
left foot throbbed, and his left eye changed in an ill-omened way.

The wise Dharma King, fearing a great danger, asked Draupadi, "Where is Bhima,
Bharata?" The Panchali told him that Vrikodara had been gone a long time. The
strong-armed king set out with Dhaumya. He told Dhananjaya, "You must guard
Draupadi," and charged Nakula and Sahadeva with the brahmins. From that same
hermitage the lord followed his footprints, and saw the earth marked with
Bhima's signs. He saw trees broken by the wind of the hero's thighs as he ran
after deer as fast as the wind, trees bent over on the road. Following those
signs, he saw in a mountain cave his younger brother, seized by the lord of
serpents, unable to move.

**Divergences.**

* **Nahusha's pride.** A rejected passage (\*875) has Nahusha tell how he won the lordship of the three worlds, was carried in a litter by a thousand sages and touched
  Agastya with his foot. In the CE he says only that he fell through Agastya's curse and because he scorned the brahmins (3.176.14). The story is told in Book 5.
* **Bhima's hunting.** An appendix passage (@18, after 3.175.11) describes Bhima killing great beasts in the forest. Not narrated.
* **Yudhishthira's search.** Rejected passages (\*879–\*881) add the herds of dead beasts on Bhima's track and the barren ground of the cave. Not narrated.
