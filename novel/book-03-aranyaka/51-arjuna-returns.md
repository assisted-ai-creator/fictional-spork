# Arjuna Returns

As they lived on that king of mountains, the great ones, keeping good vows, took
delight and joy in it, longing to see Arjuna. Many hosts of gandharvas and great
seers came to them gladly, for they were full of courage, pure in spirit and
full of splendour, and above all true and steady. When they reached that best of
mountains, covered with trees in flower, the great chariot fighters felt the
highest peace of mind, as the hosts of the Maruts do when they reach heaven.
Looking at the peaks and slopes of the great mountain, strewn with flowers and
ringing with the cries of peacocks and swans, they stood there in the greatest
joy. On that best of mountains they saw lotus pools, made by Kubera himself,
their banks walled in, crowded with geese, ducks and swans, and full of lotuses.
They saw pleasure grounds rich in look, bright with lovely garlands, set with
the finest jewels, very beautiful, as those of Kubera the king would be.

Though they were given to austerity and roamed it always, they could not grasp
in thought that mountain peak, covered all over with great fragrant trees of
many colours and wreathed in clouds. Through the mountain's own splendour, and
the power of its great herbs, there was no dividing day from night, bravest of
men. From there the heroes, the lions among men, saw the rising and setting of
the sun, of boundless power, standing on which he gives life to things moving
and unmoving. They saw the sun, the dispeller of darkness, come and go, rise and
set, and the quarters and the space between them covered with the nets of his
rays.

They recited the Veda, performed their rites always, put dharma first and kept
pure vows. They stood in truth, waiting for the coming of that great chariot
fighter, true to his vows. "Let us have joy here, soon, meeting Dhananjaya with
his weapons won." Saying this, and wishing the highest blessings, the Parthas
gave themselves to austerity and yoga. Seeing the bright forests on the mountain
and thinking of Kiritin without rest, each night and each day seemed to them
like a year. Ever since great Jishnu had knotted his hair, with Dhaumya's
consent, and gone away, they had had no joy. How could they have pleasure, when
their minds had gone with him? From the day Jishnu, who walks like a rutting
elephant, left the forest of Kamyaka at his brother Yudhishthira's command, they
had been struck down by grief. So, as they thought of the rider of white horses,
who had gone to Vasava for weapons, a month passed slowly on that mountain for
the Bharatas, Bharata.

---

Then one day they suddenly saw Mahendra's chariot come, yoked with bay horses,
bright as lightning, and the great chariot fighters, who were thinking of
Arjuna, were filled with joy. Blazing, suddenly lighting up the sky, driven by
Matali, it shone like a great meteor among the clouds, like a flame of fire
burning without smoke. Kiritin could be seen standing in it, garlanded, wearing
fine ornaments. Dhananjaya, with the power of the wielder of the thunderbolt,
came to the mountain blazing with glory. Reaching the mountain, the garlanded
wearer of the diadem got down from Mahendra's chariot. First he saluted
Dhaumya's feet, then Ajatashatru's, and he bowed at Vrikodara's feet too, and
the sons of Madri saluted him. Then he went to Krishna and comforted her, and
stood humbly beside his brother.

Meeting him who was beyond measure gave them the greatest joy. And the garlanded
wearer of the diadem, seeing them, was glad and praised the king. The Parthas,
undaunted in spirit, went up to Indra's chariot, in which the slayer of Namuchi
had killed seven troops of Diti's sons, and walked round it, keeping it on their
right. They were overjoyed, and they paid Matali the highest honour, as if to
the king of the gods himself. And the princes of the Kuru king asked him about
all the dwellers in heaven, as was proper. Matali too greeted them, and
counselled them as a father counsels his sons. Then he went back in the chariot
of matchless splendour to the lord of the thirty gods.

When the chariot of the best of the gods had gone, the great son of Shakra, the
crusher of all enemies, gladly gave his beloved, Sutasoma's mother, the
ornaments Shakra had given him. They were very costly, of the finest form,
bright as the sun. Then he sat down among those bulls of the Kurus and those
bulls among brahmins, bright as the sun and fire, and told them everything as it
had happened. "This is how I learned the weapons, from Shakra, from the Wind and
from Shiva himself. And the gods with Indra were all pleased with my conduct and
my concentration." When the one of pure deeds had told them briefly of his going
to heaven, Kiritin lay down to sleep, content, in that dwelling with the sons of
Madri.

---

At that very time a tumultuous sound of all kinds of instruments arose in the
sky from the dwellers in heaven. There was the sound of chariot wheels and of
bells, Bharata, and the separate cries of beasts of prey and of birds on every
side. Gandharvas and apsarases in aerial chariots bright as the sun followed all
round the king of the gods, the tamer of enemies. Then Purandara, the king of
the gods, mounted his chariot yoked with bay horses and adorned with gold, which
thundered like a cloud. Blazing with the highest glory, he came quickly to the
Parthas. When he arrived, the thousand-eyed one got down from the chariot.

As soon as he saw the great king of the gods, the glorious Dharma King
Yudhishthira went up to him with his brothers. He of rich sacrificial gifts
honoured him who is beyond measure by the rules, as was fitting, with the rite
the rules lay down. And splendid Dhananjaya bowed low to Purandara and stood
near the king of the gods, bowed like a servant. Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, of
great splendour, was refreshed when he saw Dhananjaya standing near the king of
the gods, disciplined, with his matted hair, given to austerity and free of
stain. He was filled with great joy at the sight of Phalguna.

To the king, so undaunted in spirit and so flooded with joy, wise Purandara,
king of the gods, said, "You will rule this earth, king, Pandava. Go well, son
of Kunti, back to the Kamyaka hermitage. The Pandava has won all the weapons
from me with care, king. Dhananjaya has done what pleases me, and the three
worlds cannot conquer him." When he had said this to Yudhishthira, son of Kunti,
the thousand-eyed one went gladly back to heaven, praised by the great seers.

This is the meeting of the Pandavas with Shakra, while they lived at the house
of the Lord of Wealth. A learned man who studies it with a collected mind,
living a year as a celibate, restrained and keeping strict vows, will live a
hundred autumns free of trouble and in great happiness.

---

When Shakra had gone as he came, Bibhatsu, with his brothers and Krishna,
honoured the son of Dharma. As he saluted him, Yudhishthira smelled the head of
the Pandava and, overjoyed, said to Arjuna in a voice thick with joy, "How did
you spend this time in heaven, Arjuna? How did you win the weapons and please
the king of the gods? Have you learned the weapons properly, Bharata? Was the
lord of the gods pleased? Did Rudra give you weapons? How did you see Shakra, or
the blessed bearer of Pinaka? How did you win the weapons, and how did you
worship them? The blessed Shatakratu told you, 'You have done what pleases me.'
What was the thing you did that pleased him, tamer of enemies? I want to hear it
at length, you of great splendour. Tell me all of it, fully, Dhananjaya: how the
great god and the king of the gods were pleased with you, sinless one, and what
you did to please the wielder of the thunderbolt, tamer of enemies."

"Listen, great king," said Arjuna, "to how I came to see Shatakratu the god, and
the blessed Shankara. When I had learned that knowledge you taught me, king,
crusher of enemies, I set out for the forest to practise austerity at your
command. From Kamyaka I went to Bhrigutunga and began my austerity. After one
night I saw a certain brahmin on the road. He asked me, 'Son of Kunti, where are
you going? Tell me.' I told him the whole truth, joy of the Kurus. When he had
heard my true account, best of kings, the brahmin honoured me and was pleased
with me, king. Then, pleased, he said to me, 'Practise austerity, Bharata. If
you practise austerity you will soon see the lord of the gods.'

"So at his word I climbed the snowy mountain and practised austerity for a
month, great king, living on roots and fruit. A second month I passed eating
water, and in the third month, joy of the Pandavas, I ate nothing. For the
fourth month I stood with my arms raised. And my breath did not fail me. That
was a kind of wonder. When the fourth month was over and the first day had gone,
a being in the shape of a boar came near me. It struck the earth with its snout
and scratched it with its feet, swept the ground with its belly and rolled over
and over. After it came another great being in the shape of a Kirata, with bow,
arrows and sword, followed by a troop of women.

"Then I took my bow and my two great inexhaustible quivers and struck that
hair-raising creature with an arrow. At the same moment the Kirata drew his
strong bow and struck it harder still, as if shaking my mind. And he said to me,
king, 'It was mine first. Why did you strike it, casting aside the law of the
hunt? I will break your pride with my sharp arrows. Stand firm.' Then that huge
one, great of body, ran at me. He covered me with great arrows as if I were a
mountain, and I covered him with a great rain of arrows. Then, with feathered
arrows with blazing heads, charged with mantras, I pierced him in return, as
thunderbolts pierce a mountain. His form became a hundredfold and a
thousandfold, and I struck all those bodies with my arrows. Again those bodies
were seen to become one, Bharata, great king, and again I struck at them.
Becoming small with a great head, and again great with a small head, then one
again, he came at me in battle, king.

"When I could not overcome him with arrows in the battle, I took up the weapon
of the Wind, bull of the Bharatas. I could not kill him with it. That was a kind
of wonder. When that weapon was turned back, my amazement was great. Then, great
king, I covered the being in the battle all the more with a great host of
weapons. I went at him with the Sthunakarna, the Net of Iron, the Rain of
Arrows, the Thick-with-Arrows, the Mountain weapon and the Rain of Stones. He
laughed, sinless one, and swallowed all my weapons.

"When they were all quelled, I sent the Brahma weapon. Then he was heaped all
round with blazing arrows, and as the great weapon heaped them on him, he grew.
Then the world was scorched by the splendour I had let loose, and in a moment
the quarters and the sky blazed on every side. But the one of great splendour
broke that weapon too in a moment. When the Brahma weapon was destroyed, king, a
great fear came over me. Then I took up my bow and my two great inexhaustible
quivers and suddenly struck at the being, and he ate up those weapons too.

"When all my weapons were destroyed and my arms eaten up, there was a fight with
arms between me and that being. We fought with fists and struck each other with
our palms, and the being threw me down. I fell to the earth, unable to move.
Then the being laughed and vanished there and then with the women, great king,
before my eyes. It was like a wonder. When he had done this, the blessed one
took another form of his own, divine, great king, wearing wonderful clothes. The
blessed one, lord of the thirty gods, cast off the Kirata form, took on his own
divine form, and stood there, the great lord. Then the blessed one himself was
seen, whose banner is the bull, with Uma beside him, green-eyed, of many forms,
bearer of Pinaka.

"He came up to me in the field, where I stood facing him as before, spear in
hand, and said, 'I am pleased,' scorcher of enemies. Then the blessed one took
up that bow and the two quivers with their inexhaustible arrows, and gave them
back to me, and said, 'Choose a boon. I am pleased with you, son of Kunti. Tell
me what I can do for you. Tell me what is in your mind, hero, and I will grant
it. Except for immortality, tell me what is in your mind.'

"Then, with my hands joined and my mind on weapons, I bowed my head to Sharva
and spoke. 'If the blessed one is gracious to me, this is the boon I want. I
want to know the weapons that are among the gods.' 'I will give them,' said the
blessed Three-eyed One. 'My own weapon, Rudra's, will come to you, Pandava.' And
the lord, pleased, gave me the Pashupata weapon. When he had given me the
eternal weapon, the great god said, 'This must never be used against humans in
any way. If you are hard pressed, Dhananjaya, you may use it. And when your
weapons are turned back, you may use it in every way.' That divine weapon, which
cannot be turned back and wards off every other weapon, stood beside me in
bodily form, by the grace of him whose banner is the bull. It destroys enemies
and cuts down enemy armies. Gods, danavas and rakshasas can hardly approach it
or withstand it. With his leave I sat down there, and the god vanished there as
I watched.

**Divergences.**

* **The clay image.** A rejected passage (\*839, after 3.163.39) has Arjuna make an image of Shankara from sand and worship it with flowers, then
  see the flowers on the hunter's head. It is not in Arjuna's account in the CE, as it is not in the narrator's (3.40). See L-120.
* **Five years and every weapon.** A rejected passage (\*832) has Arjuna stay five years in heaven and lists the weapons he won. Not narrated.
* **The danger of the Pashupata.** A rejected line (\*840) says the weapon would burn up the world if loosed at a weak foe. Not narrated.
