# Manu and the Fish

Then the sons of Pandu said to the great Markandeya, "We want to hear of the
greatness of the best of the twice-born. Tell us."

At their words the blessed Markandeya, of great austerity and great splendour,
learned in every science, spoke.

"A young prince of the Haihayas, a king, the founder of their line, conqueror of
enemy cities, handsome and strong, went out hunting. As he wandered in the
forest, which was thick with grass and creepers, he saw a sage close by, wearing
a black deerskin as his upper garment. Thinking he was a deer, the prince killed
him in the forest. Shaken by what he had done, his mind struck down by grief,
the lotus-eyed prince went to the famous kings of the Haihayas, lord of the
earth, and told them what had happened. When they heard of it, my son, and saw
the sage who had lived on roots and fruit lying killed, they were heavy at
heart.

"They all went searching here and there, asking, 'Whose son is this?', and came
straight to the hermitage of Arishtanemi Tarkshya. They saluted that great sage
of strict vows and stood there, and the sage brought them honour. They said to
the great one, 'We do not deserve your welcome, sage, because of what we have
done. We have killed a brahmin.'

"The brahmin seer said to them, 'How did you kill a brahmin? And where is he?
Tell me, all of you, and see the strength of my austerity.' They told him
everything, just as it was, but when they went back together they could not find
the dead seer there. They searched for him, ashamed, their minds lost as in a
dream.

"Then the sage Tarkshya, conqueror of enemy cities, said to them, 'Might this be
the brahmin you destroyed? This is my son, kings, who has the strength of
austerity.' As soon as they saw the seer they were filled with the greatest
wonder, lord of the earth, and said, 'This is a great marvel. We saw him dead.
How has he come back to life? Is this the power of austerity, by which he lives
again? We want to hear it, brahmin seer, if it is fit to be heard.'

"He said to them, 'Death has no power over us, kings. I will tell you the
reason, briefly, and how it comes about. We know only the truth, and we do not
set our minds on untruth. We follow our own dharma. So we have no fear of death.
We speak of what is good in brahmins and not of their misdeeds. So we have no
fear of death. We satisfy our guests with food and drink and our dependants with
plenty to eat, and we live in a land of splendid men. So we have no fear of
death. I have told you only a little of it, you who are free of envy. Go, all of
you together. You have no fear of sin.'

"'So be it,' they all said, and they honoured the great sage and went gladly
back to their own country, bull of the Bharatas.

---

"Hear from me more of the greatness of brahmins. A royal seer named Vainya was
consecrated here for a horse sacrifice, and Atri, we are told, set out to go to
him to ask for wealth. But then, looking to what dharma plainly showed, he would
not go on with it. The splendid one thought it over and chose the forest
instead. He called his lawful wife and his sons and said, 'We shall win a fruit
that is endless, great and untroubled. Let going to the forest please you soon,
for its merit is greater.'

"His wife, holding to dharma, answered him, 'Go to the great Vainya and ask him
for much wealth. The royal seer is sacrificing, and he will give wealth to one
who asks. Take it, brahmin seer, accept much wealth, share it among your
dependants and your sons, and then go where you wish. That is the highest
dharma, say those who know dharma.'

"'The great Gautama has told me, blessed one,' said Atri, 'that Vainya is joined
to dharma and wealth and true to his vows. But brahmins who hate me live there,
as Gautama told me, so I cannot make up my mind. There they would twist the good
words I speak, full of dharma, pleasure and wealth, into empty ones. But I will
go, wise one. Your words please me. Vainya will give me cows and a great store
of wealth.'

"When he had said this, the great ascetic went quickly to Vainya's sacrifice.
When he reached the place of sacrifice, Atri praised the king. 'King Vainya, you
are the lord. You are the first king on earth. Hosts of sages praise you. No one
but you knows dharma.'

"The seer Gautama of great austerity, angry, said to him there, 'Do not say that
again, Atri. Your mind is not steady. Here our first lord is the great Indra,
Prajapati.'

"Then Atri answered Gautama, king of kings, 'This man is the Ordainer, just as
Indra is Prajapati. It is you who are deluded. You have no wisdom.'

"'I know,' said Gautama, 'and I am not deluded. It is you who are deluded, in
your eagerness to speak. You will praise him hoping for gain, because he has let
you see him. You do not know the highest dharma, and you do not see what it is
for. You are a child and a fool, grown old for some reason or other.'

"As the two of them stood arguing in sight of the sages, those who had gathered
at the sacrifice asked, 'What is this about these two? Who let them into
Vainya's assembly? Why do they stand there shouting at each other?' Then
Kashyapa, most righteous, who knew every dharma, told them about the two who had
come to argue. Then Gautama said to the members of the assembly, the best of
sages, 'Hear the question the two of us have raised, bulls among the twice-born.
Atri says Vainya is the Ordainer. We have great doubt about this.'

"As soon as they heard it, the great sages ran quickly to Sanatkumara, who knew
dharma, to cut the doubt. When he had heard their words, as they truly were, the
great ascetic answered them with words full of dharma and meaning. 'Brahman is
joined with kshatra, and kshatra with brahman. The king is the first dharma, the
lord of creatures. He is Shakra, he is Shukra, he is the Creator, he is
Brihaspati. Prajapati, Viraj, Samraj, Kshatriya, lord of the earth, king: who
would not honour one praised with these names? The king is called First Cause,
Victor in Battle, the Fearless, the Joyful, Being, Leader to Heaven, Victor, and
Babhru. Truly angry, living by battle, setting true dharma going: the seers,
fearing adharma, placed power in the kshatra. As the sun among the gods in
heaven drives away the darkness by its splendour, so the king on earth drives
away adharma. So the king comes first, as the scriptures show, and the side that
said "king" has won.'

"Then the great-minded king was glad that his side had won. Pleased, he said to
Atri, who had praised him first, 'Since you called me greater than all men here,
brahmin seer, equal to all the gods and the best of them, I will give you much
wealth of many kinds. A thousand young slave women, well dressed and adorned,
ten crores of gold and ten loads of gold I give you, brahmin, for I think you
know everything.' The great-minded Atri took it all, as was right, and the
splendid ascetic went home. He gladly gave the wealth to his sons, and with his
self restrained, he set his mind on austerity and went to the forest.

---

"Hear too, conqueror of enemy cities, what Sarasvati sang here when the wise
sage Tarkshya asked her, hero.

"'What is best for a man here, good lady?' asked Tarkshya. 'What must he do so
as not to fall from his own dharma? Tell me all of it, lovely one. Taught by
you, I will not fall from my own dharma. How shall I offer into the fire, or
worship? At what time, and how, will dharma not be lost? Tell me all this,
fortunate one, so that I may go through the worlds free of passion.'

"Asked like this by him, full of love, and seeing that the brahmin Tarkshya was
eager to hear, of the highest understanding, joined to dharma and to what is
good, Sarasvati spoke.

"'The man who knows brahman as it is taught, who always studies the Veda, who is
pure and watchful, will go beyond to the city of the gods and find joy with the
immortals. There are lovely wide lotus pools there, free of grief, full of
flowers, very holy, without mud, full of fish, with fine fords, covered with
golden lotuses. A man of holy deeds sits on their banks, honoured by apsarases
each by herself, fragrant, adorned and golden, and he is very glad.

"'Those who give cows reach the highest world. Those who give an ox go to the
world of the sun. Whoever gives clothes goes to the world of the moon. Whoever
gives gold reaches deathlessness. Whoever gives a milch cow of good habits, easy
to milk, with a fine calf, that does not run away, enjoys the world of heaven
for as many years as she has hairs. Whoever gives an ox of good habits, a puller
of the plough, of endless strength, a strong young bearer of the yoke, wins the
worlds of ten givers of cows. Whoever offers oblations into the fire for seven
years, Tarkshya, with good vows and good conduct, purifies by his own deeds
seven generations of his forefathers after him and seven before him.'

"'Tell me, lovely one,' said Tarkshya, 'the ancient rule of the fire offering. I
ask you. Taught by you today, let me know the ancient rule of the fire
offering.'

"'No one impure, and no one with unwashed hands, should offer,' said Sarasvati,
'nor one who does not know brahman, nor one without learning. For the gods are
hungry and want purity, and they do not accept an offering from one without
faith. Do not appoint one not learned in the Veda to the offering to the gods,
for such a man pours it out in vain. Such a one is called "not full", Tarkshya,
one not learned in the Veda. Such a man should not make the fire offering. Those
who offer into the fire with faith, true to their vows, eating what is left of
the offering, reach the fragrant world of the cows and see the highest god, the
true.'

"'I see that you are the understanding that enters into the knower of the
field,' said Tarkshya, 'into the world beyond and into the rising of deeds, and
that you are divine wisdom, fortunate one. Who are you, lovely one?'

"'I have come from the fire offering to cut the doubts of the bulls among
brahmins,' said Sarasvati. 'Because I am joined with you, I have told you this,
standing in the true state, the true meaning as it is.'

"'There is no one like you,' said Tarkshya. 'You shine beyond measure, like
Shri. Your form is divine and most lovely, and you bear divine wisdom, fortunate
one.'

"'By the finest things, best of the two-footed,' said Sarasvati, 'that the wise
offer in sacrifices, I grow and am nourished and made beautiful, brahmin. And
whatever substance is used, of wood, of iron or of earth, know, wise one, that
success comes of it through divine form and wisdom.'

"'The sages strive for this, holding it the highest good,' said Tarkshya. 'Tell
me of that highest release, free of grief, which the steadfast enter.'

"'Those who know the Veda reach that highest, beyond the highest, famous,
ancient,' said Sarasvati, 'by study, giving, vows and holy yoga. Rich in
austerity, free of grief, they are released. In its midst a fragrant reed
shines, spotless, with a thousand branches. From its root flow lovely rivers,
streaming honey and water. Great rivers run to every branch, with banks of fried
grain and cakes, meat and greens, and always a mud of rice cooked in milk. Where
the gods led by Agni, with Indra and the hosts of the Maruts, sacrificed with
the finest sacrifices: that is the highest place, sage.'"

---

Then the Pandava said again to Markandeya, "Tell me here the story of Manu
Vaivasvata."

"There was a son of Vivasvat, king," said Markandeya, "a great seer, mighty,
tiger among men, as bright as Prajapati. In might and splendour, in fortune and
above all in austerity, Manu surpassed his father and his grandfather. At wide
Badari that lord of men practised great and fierce austerity, standing on one
foot with his arms raised. With his head bowed and his eyes unblinking and
steady, he practised terrible austerity for ten thousand years.

"Once, as he was practising austerity in wet bark clothes and matted hair, a
fish came to the bank of the Virini and spoke to him. 'Blessed one, I am a
little fish. I am afraid of the bigger fish. So you should save me, you of good
vows. For the strong fish eat the weak one, above all, as has always been our
way of life. So you should save me, sinking in this great flood of fear. I will
do you a good turn in return.'

"When he heard the fish's words, Manu Vaivasvata was filled with pity, and he
took the fish in his own hand. He brought it to the water's edge and put the
fish, which shone like moonbeams, into a water jar. There the fish grew, king,
and was very well cared for, and Manu came to feel for it as for a son. Then in
a long time the fish grew very large, and it would no longer fit in the water of
the jar. Then the fish saw Manu and spoke to him again. 'Blessed one, good one,
find me another place now.'

"Then the blessed sage Manu took him out of the jar and brought the fish to a
great pool, and threw him in there, conqueror of enemy cities. Then the fish
grew again for many years. The pool was two leagues long and one league wide,
but the fish would not fit in it, lotus-eyed one, and could not move in the
pool, son of Kunti, lord of the people. Then the fish saw Manu and spoke to him
again. 'Take me, blessed one, good one, lord, to the Ganga, queen of the ocean.
I will live there, or wherever you think best, my father.'

"At these words the blessed Manu, master of himself, unfallen, took the fish and
threw it himself into the river Ganga. There the fish grew for some time, tamer
of enemies. Then the fish saw Manu again and said, 'I cannot move in the Ganga,
lord, because I am so big. Take me quickly to the ocean. Be gracious, blessed
one.' Manu himself took the fish out of the waters of the Ganga and brought him
to the ocean, Partha, and let him go there. Great as the fish was, to Manu's
mind he was easy to carry wherever he wished, pleasant to touch and smell.

"When Manu had thrown him into the ocean, the fish, as if smiling, said to him,
'Blessed one, you have guarded me well in every way. Now hear from me what you
must do when the time comes. Soon, blessed one, all this on earth, moving and
unmoving, will be dissolved, fortunate one. The time for the washing of the
worlds has come. So today I tell you what is best for you. For all that moves
and all that does not, the moving and the still, the most terrible time has
come. You must have a strong ship built, fitted with ropes. You must board it
with the seven seers, great sage. And put into that ship all the seeds, as I
told you long ago, each kind well stored apart. When you are in the ship, wait
for me, dear to the sages. I will come with a horn. By that you will know me,
ascetic. This is what you must do. I take my leave. I am going. And you must not
doubt these words of mine, lord.'

"'I will do it,' he answered the fish, and they took leave of each other and
went as they wished. Then, great king, as the fish had told him, Manu took all
the seeds and set out on the ocean, with its great waves, in a fine ship, hero,
tamer of enemies. And Manu thought of the fish, lord of the earth. Knowing his
thought, conqueror of enemy cities, the horned fish came there quickly, best of
the Bharatas.

"Manu saw the horned fish in the ocean, lord of kings, rising like a mountain,
in the shape he had foretold. He fixed the rope on the fish's head, on that
horn, tiger among men. Tied with that rope, conqueror of enemy cities, the fish
dragged the ship through the salt water with great speed. He crossed the ocean
with that ship, lord of men, the ocean that seemed to dance with its waves and
roar with its waters. Tossed by great winds on that great ocean, the ship reeled
like a fickle, drunken woman, conqueror of enemy cities. Neither the earth nor
the quarters nor the spaces between could be seen. All was water, the air and
the sky, bull among men. When the world was in such turmoil, bull of the
Bharatas, only the seven seers, Manu and the fish could be seen.

"So for many years the fish tirelessly dragged the ship through that mass of
water, king. Then the fish dragged the ship to the highest peak of the Himalaya,
joy of the Kurus. Then the fish, laughing softly, said to those seers, 'Bind the
ship to this peak of the Himalaya at once.' When they heard the fish's words,
the seers quickly bound the ship to the peak of the Himalaya, bull of the
Bharatas. And know, son of Kunti, bull of the Bharatas, that that highest peak
of the Himalaya is still known today as Naubandhana, the Binding of the Ship.

"Then the unblinking one said to the seers together, 'I am Prajapati, Brahma.
Beyond me there is nothing to be reached. In the form of a fish I have saved you
from this danger. Manu must create all creatures, with the gods, asuras and men,
and all the worlds, all that moves and all that does not. By very fierce
austerity it will come clear to him, and by my grace he will not be confused in
creating creatures.' When the fish had said this, he vanished in a moment. And
Manu Vaivasvata himself, wanting to create creatures, was confused in the work
of creation, and he practised great austerity. Joined to great austerity, Manu
then began to create all creatures himself, as it should be, bull of the
Bharatas.

"This is the ancient tale called the Tale of the Fish. I have told this story,
which takes away all sin. The man who always hears this story of Manu from the
beginning will be happy, with all his aims achieved, and will go to the world of
heaven."

**Divergences.**

* **Which god is the fish.** The fish is often told of as an avatar of Vishnu. In the CE he says, "I am Prajapati, Brahma" (3.185.48). See L-139.
* **More reasons.** Rejected passages (\*904, \*906) add further reasons why death has no power over Tarkshya's family: pure conduct, the twilight rites, patience,
  pilgrimage and living in a holy land. Not narrated.
* **The king defined.** A rejected passage (\*911) gives a fuller definition of a king. Not narrated.
* **The tawny cow.** A rejected passage (\*914) praises the gift of a tawny cow with a brass milking pail. Not narrated.
* **The fish's need.** A rejected line (\*917) has the fish say that the great waters cannot be crossed without him. Not narrated.
