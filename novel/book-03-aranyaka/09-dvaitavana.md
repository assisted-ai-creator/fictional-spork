# Dvaitavana

When the lord of the Dasharhas had gone, Yudhishthira, Bhimasena and Arjuna, the
twins, Krishna and the family priest mounted their costly chariots, yoked to the
finest horses. The heroes, shining like the lord of beings, set out together for
the forest. They gave gold coins, clothes and cows to those who knew the
teachings, the syllables and the sacred words. Twenty armed servants went ahead
of them. All of them took bows, armour and bright arrows, bowstrings, engines
and shafts, and went behind. Then Indrasena quickly gathered up the princess's
clothes, her nurses and maids and her ornaments, and followed in a chariot at
the back.

Then the townspeople, their spirits undaunted, came to the best of the Kurus and
walked round him, keeping him on their right. The brahmins greeted him gladly,
and all the chief men of Kurujangala. The Dharma King, with his brothers,
greeted them gladly too. The great lord stood there, looking at the crowds of
the people of Kurujangala. The great bull of the Kurus felt towards them as a
father feels towards his sons, and they felt towards that pride of the Bharatas
as sons feel towards their father.

Then the great crowds came up and stood round the hero of the Kurus. "Alas, our
master! Alas, dharma!" they said, and all their faces were wet with tears, from
shame. "The best of the Kurus, the ruler of his people, has left us, as a father
might leave his sons. Where has the Dharma King gone, leaving these townspeople
and all the country people? Shame on the son of Dhritarashtra, with his cruel
mind, and on the son of Subala, and on evil-minded Karna! Those wicked, fierce
men wish harm, lord of men, to you, who are good and always true to dharma. The
great man, whose deeds are never in vain, founded a matchless great city like
the city of the gods, Shatakratu's own. Where has the Dharma King gone, leaving
it? Maya built him a matchless hall, like the hall of the gods, a divine marvel
as if guarded by the gods. Where has the Dharma King gone, leaving it?"

Bibhatsu, who knew dharma, desire and wealth, a man of the highest vigour, spoke
loudly to them all together. "When he has lived out this exile in the forest,
the king will take the glory of his enemies. You who have come here, together
and each of you, should win over the chief of the twice-born and the ascetics,
and those who know dharma and wealth. Ask them for this, that our purpose may be
fully won."

When Arjuna said this, king, the brahmins and people of every class welcomed his
words gladly. They walked round the best of those who uphold dharma, keeping him
on their right. They took leave of Partha, of Vrikodara, of Dhananjaya,
Yajnaseni and the twins. With Yudhishthira's leave they went back to the
kingdom, each to his own home, their joy gone.

---

When they had gone, Yudhishthira, the righteous son of Kunti, true to his word,
spoke to all his brothers. "We have to live in the lonely forest for these
twelve years. Look in the great forest for a place full of beasts and birds,
full of flowers and fruit, lovely and kind, fit for holy men, where we may live
happily through all these years."

When he said this, Dhananjaya honoured the high-minded teacher of men as his
elder and answered the Dharma King. "You yourself have waited on the great seers
and the old. There is nothing you do not know in the world of men. You have
always waited on brahmins, bull of the Bharatas, on Dvaipayana and the others,
and on Narada of great austerity. Master of himself, he always travels through
the gates of all the worlds, from the world of the gods to the world of Brahma
and the worlds of the gandharvas and apsarases. You know all the ways of the
brahmins, no doubt of it, and you know the powers of them all, king. You
yourself know, king, what leads to good. We will make our home wherever you
wish, great king.

"Here is a lake called Dvaitavana, holy, fit for good men, lovely, full of
flowers and fruit, and many kinds of birds live there. I would like us to spend
these twelve years here, if you agree, king. Or what else do you think?"

"What you have said is my wish too, Partha," said Yudhishthira. "Let us go to
the great lake of Dvaitavana, holy and famous."

So all the Pandavas, who walked in dharma, went with many brahmins to the holy
lake of Dvaitavana. Many brahmins surrounded Yudhishthira there, those who kept
the sacred fire and those who did not. There were reciters of the Veda, beggars,
those who murmured prayers, forest-dwellers, ascetics true in character,
hundreds of them, firm in their vows. The Pandavas, bulls of the Bharatas, went
there with many brahmins and entered lovely, holy Dvaitavana.

The lord of the kingdom saw the great forest, full of shala, palm, mango,
madhuka, nipa, kadamba, sarja, arjuna and karnikara trees, all in flower as the
hot season ended. On the tops of the great trees sat flocks of peacocks,
datyuhas and chakoras, and forest cuckoos, singing their lovely songs. The lord
of the kingdom saw great herds of great elephants in that forest, their leaders
wild with rut, like mountains, with herds of she-elephants. He came to the
lovely Bhogavati, and saw in that forest, the home of those who uphold dharma,
many hosts of perfected seers, firm of soul, wearing bark and matted hair.

Then the king got down from his carriage with his brothers and his people, and
the best of the righteous entered that forest, like Shakra of boundless power
entering heaven. Hosts of charanas and siddhas came together, eager to see the
man who kept his word, and the forest-dwellers too stood round the high-minded
lion among kings. He greeted all the siddhas there, and was honoured in return
like a king and like a god. Then the best of those who uphold dharma went in
with all the chief brahmins, his hands joined. The high-minded king of holy
ways, like his father, met the ascetics devoted to dharma, was honoured by them,
and sat down at the foot of a great tree in flower. Bhima, Krishna and
Dhananjaya, the twins and the followers of the king, the pride of the Bharatas,
all unyoked their horses, got down, and waited on him there. That great tree was
bowed by its canopy of creepers. With the five great Pandavas, fierce archers,
come to live there, it shone like a great mountain with the leaders of herds of
elephants.

---

The princes, used to comfort, came to that forest and settled into a hard life.
Like Indra, they took their pleasure in those kindly shala forests by the
Sarasvati. The king, the bull of the Kurus, of great power, fed all the ascetics
and the sages, and the chief of the twice-born, in that forest with the finest
roots and fruit. The family priest Dhaumya, rich in splendour, was like a father
to the Kurus. He performed the sacrifices, the rites for the fathers, and the
first offerings for the Pandavas as they lived in the great forest.

While they were living away from the kingdom, an ancient seer came as a guest to
the hermitage of the glorious Pandavas, Markandeya, of fierce, rich splendour.
The great one, who knew everything, of boundless power, looked at Krishna
Draupadi, at Yudhishthira, at Bhimasena and Arjuna, thought in his mind of Rama,
and smiled among the ascetics.

The Dharma King said to him, as if troubled, "All these ascetics are abashed.
Why do you smile, as if pleased, when you look at me, while the ascetics look
on?"

"I am not pleased, my son, and I am not smiling," said Markandeya. "No pride
born of joy takes hold of me. But seeing your misfortune today, I remember Rama,
the son of Dasharatha, true to his vows. That king too, with Lakshmana, lived in
the forest at his father's command. I myself saw him long ago, Partha, bow in
hand, wandering on the slope of Mount Rishyamuka. The great man, like the
thousand-eyed god, the conqueror of Maya and the killer of Namuchi, the sinless
son of Dasharatha, lived in the forest at his father's command, as his own
dharma. He was Shakra's equal in power, great, unconquerable in battle, and he
gave up his pleasures and wandered in the forests. A man should not do wrong
because he thinks, 'I have the strength.'

"Kings like Nabhaga and Bhagiratha conquered this earth to the edge of the
ocean, and they too won the worlds by truth, my son. A man should not do wrong
because he thinks, 'I have the strength.' They call Alarka a good man, best of
men, true to his vows, king of Kashi and Karusha, who gave up his kingdoms and
his wealth. A man should not do wrong because he thinks, 'I have the strength.'
The seven seers shine in the sky, Partha, because they honour the ancient law
laid down by the Placer, good men that they are, best of men. A man should not
do wrong because he thinks, 'I have the strength.' Look at the mighty tusked
elephants, lord of men, as big as mountain peaks, standing under the Placer's
command, best of men. A man should not do wrong because he thinks, 'I have the
strength.' Look at all creatures, lord of men, how each shows the power it was
given by the Maker, according to its own birth. A man should not do wrong
because he thinks, 'I have the strength.'

"By truth, by dharma, by fitting conduct and by modesty you surpass all
creatures, Partha, and your fame and splendour blaze like the sun, the giver of
light. When you have lived out this hard exile in the forest, as you promised,
great one, you will take back from the Kauravas, king, the fortune that blazes
with your own splendour."

The great seer said this to him among the ascetics, with his friends around him.
Then he took leave of Dhaumya and the Parthas together, and set out towards the
north.

---

While the great Pandavas lived in Dvaitavana, the great forest filled with
brahmins. The lake of Dvaitavana, with the sound of the Veda rising all the time
on every side, was as holy as the world of Brahma. The sound of the Yajus, the
Rig and the Saman verses, and of prose, as they were recited all round, was
lovely to the heart. The twang of the Pandavas' bowstrings and the chanting of
the wise brahmins together, the kshatriya power joined with the brahmin power,
shone out the more.

Then Baka Dalbhya spoke to Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, as the son of Kunti
sat at dusk, surrounded by seers. "Look, Partha, in Dvaitavana it is the hour of
the offering for the brahmin ascetics, and their fires are blazing. Guarded by
you, men of firm vows practise dharma in this holy place: the Bhrigus, the
Angirases, the Vasishthas with the Kashyapas, the fortunate Agastyas and the
Atreyas, keepers of the highest vows. The best brahmins of the whole world have
gathered round you.

"Listen, Partha, with a single mind, with your brothers, to what I will tell
you, son of Kunti, Kaurava. The brahmin power joined with the kshatriya, and the
kshatriya joined with the brahmin, once roused, burn up enemies as fire and wind
burn up forests. A king who wants to win this world and the next should not try
to live long without a brahmin, my son. With a brahmin who has learned dharma
and wealth and is free from delusion, a king drives off his rivals. Bali, as he
practised the dharma that leads to the highest good and made him protect his
people, found no ford to cross by but a brahmin. The asura son of Virochana
lacked nothing he desired, and his fortune never failed, when he had won the
earth by joining with brahmins. When he did them wrong, he perished.

"This earth, with all its riches, does not stay long with the second class of
men without a brahmin. The earth, with the ocean for its rim, bows to the king
whom a brahmin, trained in policy, guides. As an elephant's strength in battle
fails without the man who holds the goad, so the kshatriya's strength fails when
brahmins are cast off. The brahmin's vision is matchless, and the kshatriya's
strength is peerless. When they go together, the world is at peace. As a great
fire burns up dry grass with the wind, so a king burns up his enemies together
with a brahmin. A wise king should seek counsel from brahmins, to win what he
has not got and to increase what he has. To win what he has not got, and
increase what he has, and give it to the worthy, keep beside you a famous
brahmin who knows the Veda, wise and learned. You have always behaved nobly
towards brahmins, Yudhishthira, and that is why your fame blazes in all the
worlds."

Then all those brahmins honoured Baka Dalbhya, and were all the more glad as
Yudhishthira was praised. Dvaipayana, Narada, Jamadagnya, Prithushravas,
Indradyumna, Bhaluki, Kritachetas, Sahasrapad, Karnashravas, Munja, Lavanashva,
Kashyapa, Harita, Sthunakarna, Agniveshya, Shaunaka, Ritavak, Suvak,
Brihadashva, Ritavasu, Urdhvaretas, Vrishamitra, Suhotra and Hotravahana were
there. These and many other brahmins of firm vows honoured Ajatashatru, as the
seers honour Purandara.

**Divergences.**

* **Markandeya's smile.** In the CE he smiles because he remembers Rama, who also lived in the
  forest at his father's word (3.26.5–10). His longer telling of Rama's story comes later
  (3.257–276).
* **Where they were.** The CE has the Pandavas set out from Kamyaka at 3.24 after the allies
  leave, though at 3.12.68 it had already said they went to Dvaitavana after killing Kirmira.
  Both are kept as the text has them.

* 3.24.2 *śikṣākṣaramantravidbhyaḥ*: "to those who knew the teachings, the syllables and the
  sacred words".
* 3.24.3 *yantrāṇi*: "engines". The sense is uncertain.
* 3.24.8 *hriyā*: "from shame".
* 3.24.11 *śatakratuprastham*: "Shatakratu's own", Indraprastha.
* 3.25.17: the trees are named as the text names them.
