# Savitri's Return

"At that very time, in the great forest, Dyumatsena got back his sight," said
Markandeya, "and, his mind clear, saw everything with his eyes. He went to all
the hermitages with his wife Shaibya, bull among men, in the greatest distress
for his son. The couple went round the hermitages, rivers, forests and lakes,
searching this place and that. Whenever they heard any sound, they looked up,
thinking it was their son, and ran, saying, 'Satyavat is coming with Savitri!'
Their feet were cracked and sore, wounded and wet with blood, their limbs
pierced by kusha grass and thorns, and they ran like mad people.

"Then all the brahmins who lived in the hermitage came up to them, surrounded
them, comforted them and brought them back to their own hermitage. There, with
his wife beside him, surrounded by old hermits rich in austerity, he was
comforted with wonderful stories about the kings of old. Then, comforted again,
the old couple, longing to see their son, remembered what their son had done as
a child and were deeply grieved. Wasted by sorrow, they spoke pitiful words
again and wept, 'Oh, my son! Oh, my good daughter-in-law! Where are you? Where
are you?'

"'Since his wife Savitri has austerity and self-control and good conduct,' said
Suvarchas, 'Satyavat is alive.'

"'I have studied the Vedas with their limbs,' said Gautama. 'I have gathered
great austerity. From boyhood I have kept my chastity, and I have satisfied my
teachers and the fire. I have kept all my vows with a collected mind, fasting
and living on air, and all my good deeds. By this austerity I know all that
others mean to do. Know this is the truth: Satyavat is alive.'

"'Words that have come from my teacher's mouth are never false,' said a pupil.
'So Satyavat is alive.'

"'Since his wife Savitri has all the good marks that keep a woman from
widowhood,' said the seers, 'Satyavat is alive.'

"'Since his wife Savitri has austerity and self-control and good conduct,' said
Bharadvaja, 'Satyavat is alive.'

"'Since your sight has come back,' said Dalbhya, 'and Savitri went out after her
vow without eating, Satyavat is alive.'

"'Since the beasts and birds are crying in a calm quarter,' said Mandavya, 'and
your earthly fortunes are stirring, Satyavat is alive.'

"'Since your son has every virtue, is loved by the people, and has the marks of
long life,' said Dhaumya, 'Satyavat is alive.'

"Comforted like this by those truthful ascetics, he weighed each of these things
and grew as if steady again. Then, after a moment, Savitri came back to the
hermitage in the night with her husband Satyavat, and went in full of joy.

"'Now that we see you united with your son, and with your sight,' said the
brahmins, 'all of us ask after your good fortune, lord of the earth. By good
fortune you prosper three times over: in meeting your son, in seeing Savitri,
and in winning back your own sight. What all of us said has come true, no doubt
of it. You will soon prosper more and more.'

"Then all the brahmins lit a fire there and sat round King Dyumatsena, Partha.
Shaibya, Satyavat and Savitri, who stood to one side, sat down free of grief
with the leave of them all. Then all those forest-dwellers, sitting with the
king, were curious, Partha, and asked the king's son, 'Why did you not come back
earlier with your wife, lord? Why have you come back so late at night? What kept
you? Your father and mother were in torment, and so were we, king's son. We know
it was not for nothing. You must tell us all of it.'

"'With my father's leave I went out with Savitri,' said Satyavat. 'Then, as I
was splitting wood in the forest, my head began to ache. I think I slept a long
time from the pain. I have never slept so long before. That is why I have come
so late, and there is no other reason. Let none of you be troubled.'

"'The sudden return of your father Dyumatsena's sight,' said Gautama, 'you do
not know the reason for. Savitri must tell it. I want to hear it, Savitri, for
you know what is near and what is far. I know you, Savitri, to be like the
goddess Savitri in splendour. You know the reason for this, so tell the truth.
If there is no secret in it, tell us.'

"'It is as you know,' said Savitri. 'Your thought is not mistaken. I have no
secret. Hear the truth of it. The great Narada told me of my husband's death,
and this was the day. So I did not leave him. As he slept, Yama himself came to
him with his servant. He bound him and took him towards the quarter where the
ancestors live. I praised that lord god with true words, and he gave me five
boons. Hear them. Two boons for my father-in-law, his sight and his own kingdom.
A hundred sons for my father, and a hundred sons for myself. And my husband
Satyavat, with a life of four hundred years. It was for my husband's life that I
kept my vow so firmly. I have told you the reason in full and truly: how this
great sorrow of mine turned out to end in happiness.'

"'The king's family was sinking, overwhelmed by misfortunes, in a lake of
darkness,' said the seers. 'You, good woman of good family, of fine conduct,
holding to dharma and merit, have lifted it up again.'

"When they had praised and honoured that finest of women, the seers gathered
there took leave of the king and his son and went home at once, glad and at
peace.

---

"When that night had passed and the orb of the sun had risen, all the hermits
rich in austerity gathered after their morning rites. The great seers could not
stop telling Dyumatsena again and again of Savitri's great fortune. Then all the
people of the kingdom came from the Shalvas, king, and told him that his enemy
the king had been killed by his own counsellor. They told him truly that the
counsellor had killed him with his allies and kinsmen, and that the enemy's army
had fled. 'All the people are of one mind about you, king: "With his sight or
without it, let him be our king." With this resolve we have been sent here,
king. These carriages have come, and your fourfold army. Set out, king, bless
you. Your victory has been proclaimed in the city. Sit for long years on the
seat of your fathers and grandfathers.'

"When they saw the king with his sight and his fine form, they all bowed their
heads to the ground, their eyes wide with wonder. Then he greeted the old
brahmins who lived in the hermitage, was honoured by them all, and set out for
the city. Shaibya, with Savitri, went in a well-spread, shining carriage borne
by men, surrounded by the army. Then the household priests gladly consecrated
Dyumatsena, and consecrated his great-hearted son as crown prince.

"Then, after a long time, a hundred sons were born to Savitri, adding to her
fame, heroes who never turned back. And in the same way a hundred mighty
brothers of her own blood were born to Ashvapati, lord of the Madras, by Malavi.
So Savitri lifted out of hardship herself, her father and mother, her
mother-in-law and father-in-law, and all her husband's family.

"So too this lovely Draupadi, honoured for her conduct, will save you all, like
Savitri, the woman of good family."

So the Pandava was comforted by that great one, and, free of grief and free of
fever, king, he lived in Kamyaka.

**Divergences.**

* **The merit of hearing.** A rejected line (\*1333) adds that whoever hears the story of Savitri with devotion will be happy and free of sorrow. Not narrated.
* **The banyan and the vow.** The popular association of Savitri with a vow kept at a banyan tree is not in the text. She fasts three nights standing (3.280.3), and nothing is said of a tree but the sal tree under which Dyumatsena sits (3.279.4). See L-159.
* Other rejected lines (\*1330–\*1332) add the parents embracing Satyavat and weeping. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.282.2 *śaibyayā*: "his wife Shaibya", a princess of the Shibis.
* 3.282.10–19: the hermits' assurances are given in turn, each by its speaker, as in the text. One is spoken by "a pupil", one by "the seers" together.
* 3.282.17 *gatāhāram akṛtvā*: "went out after her vow without eating".
* 3.282.18 *śāntāyāṃ diśi*: "in a calm quarter", a good omen.
* 3.282.34 *sāvitrīm iva tejasā*: "like the goddess Savitri in splendour".
* 3.282.38 *sakiṃkaraḥ*: "with his servant". In 3.281.15 Yama says he came himself rather than send his men.
* 3.283.3: the usurper "had been killed by his own counsellor".
* 3.283.10 *narayuktena yānena*: "a carriage borne by men", a litter.
* 3.283.15: Markandeya's words to Yudhishthira close the story.
