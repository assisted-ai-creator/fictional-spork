# Gandhamadana

When the rakshasa was killed, the lord, the king, the son of Kunti, came back to
the hermitage of Narayana and lived there. In time he called together all his
brothers, with Draupadi, and remembering his brother Jaya, he said, "Four years
have gone by while we lived happily in the forest. Bibhatsu gave us a time,
about the fifth year. He is to reach the White Mountain, king of mountains, best
of peaks, and there too he gave a time to those who long to meet him. The Partha
of boundless splendour made an agreement with me long ago: 'I shall live five
years, to learn.' There we shall see the scorcher of enemies, the bearer of
Gandiva, back in this world from the world of the gods, with his weapons won."

When the Pandava had said this he took leave of all the brahmins, and told the
ascetics the reason. Those brahmins of fierce austerity were pleased. They
walked round the Partha, keeping him on their right, and gave him their blessing
with wishes for his good fortune and well-being. "You will soon cross this
hardship, bull of the Bharatas, and it will end in happiness. You know dharma,
and by the dharma of a kshatriya you will rule the earth."

The king, scorcher of enemies, took the words of those ascetics and set out with
those brahmins and his brothers. The glorious one went with Draupadi, followed
by the rakshasas led by Hidimba's son, and guarded by Lomasha. In some places he
went on foot, in others he was carried by the rakshasas, the one of great
splendour and good vows, with his brothers. Then King Yudhishthira, thinking
over his many hardships, went north, to a region full of lions, tigers and
elephants. He looked at Kailasa and the mountain Mainaka, at the foothills of
Gandhamadana and at the mountain Meru. Going higher and higher up the mountain,
past many auspicious rivers, on the seventeenth day he came to a holy plateau of
the Himalaya.

There on the holy back of the Himalaya, full of trees and creepers of every
kind, the Pandavas saw Gandhamadana close by, king. They saw the holiest
hermitage of Vrishaparvan, surrounded by flowering trees grown from eddies of
water. The Pandavas, tamers of enemies, their weariness gone, went up to that
royal seer, the righteous Vrishaparvan, and saluted him. The royal seer welcomed
the bulls of the Bharatas like sons, and, honoured, the tamers of enemies stayed
there seven nights.

When the eighth day came they took leave of that world-famous seer Vrishaparvan
and made ready to go. One by one they commended those brahmins to Vrishaparvan,
as a trust, at the proper time, well honoured like kinsmen. Then the Pandavas
left their fine clothes and good ornaments in Vrishaparvan's hermitage. He knew
the past and the future. He was skilled and knew every dharma. The knower of
dharma counselled the bulls of the Bharatas like sons. With his leave the
great-spirited heroes went north with Krishna and the great-spirited brahmins,
and King Vrishaparvan followed them as they went. The one of great splendour
commended the Pandavas to the brahmins, went with the sons of Kunti a little
way, and blessed them. Then Vrishaparvan showed them the road and turned back.

Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, true in courage, set out on foot with his brothers
along a road full of herds of beasts of every kind. Living on mountain slopes
shut in by trees of every kind, on the fourth day the Pandavas entered the White
Mountain. It looked like a mass of great clouds. It was fine and full of water,
lovely with jewels and gold, a mountain of many heights. They took the road
Vrishaparvan had told them and followed it as he had pointed, looking at
mountains of every kind. Higher and higher up the mountain there were caves very
hard to reach, many of them, very hard to pass, and they crossed them with ease.
Dhaumya and Krishna and the Parthas, and the great seer Lomasha, went on
together there, and not one of them fell behind.

The mighty ones came to the great mountain Malyavant. It rang with beasts and
birds, crowded with birds of every kind, visited by troops of monkeys, very
lovely, holy, with lotus lakes, pools and great forests. Then, with their hair
standing on end, they saw the mountain Gandhamadana, home of the kimpurushas,
where siddhas and charanas go. Vidyadharas and kinnara women wandered there. It
was full of elephants and lions and teeming with sharabhas, and other beasts
with gentle cries lived there too. The heroes, the sons of Pandu, gladly entered
the Gandhamadana forest one by one, a forest like Nandana, a joy to mind and
heart, a wood with fine groves.

---

With Draupadi and those great-spirited brahmins, the heroes heard sounds that
gave them joy, sweet sounds from the mouths of birds, low with passion, lovely
to the ear. They saw trees heavy with the fruit of every season, bright with the
flowers of every season, bent low with fruit. There were mangoes and hog plums
in flower, coconuts and tindukas, ajatakas and jiras, pomegranates and citrons.
There were jackfruit, likuchas, plantains, date palms and amravetasas, paravatas
and kshaudras, and lovely nipas. There were bilva and wood apple and rose-apple,
kashmari and jujube, plaksha, cluster figs and banyans, holy figs and other
trees with milky sap. There were marking nut, amalaka, haritaka and bibhitaka,
ingudi and oleander, and tindukas with great fruit. These and many others on the
slopes of Gandhamadana were laden with sweet fruit like the drink of
immortality.

So too there were champaka and ashoka, ketaka and bakula, punnaga and
saptaparna, karnikara and ketaka. There were patala and lovely kutaja, mandara
and indivara, parijata and kovidara and deodar trees. There were shala, palmyra
and tamala, priyala and bakula, silk-cotton, kimshuka and ashoka, shimshapa and
sarala. Birds sat in them singing sweetly, lovely to the ear: chakoras,
woodpeckers, bhringarajas and parrots, cuckoos, sparrows, green pigeons and
jivajivakas, priyavratas and chatakas, and other birds of many kinds.

There were bright lakes with clear water, covered on every side with white water
lilies and white lotuses, with red lotuses and blue lilies, with kahlara and
lotuses. All round they were full of geese and chakravakas, ospreys and water
hens, ducks, plavas, swans, herons and cormorants, and other water birds. On the
slopes of Gandhamadana they saw lovely lotus beds loud with sweet-voiced bees.
The bees were glad and heavy with the drink of lotus nectar, and reddened by the
pollen and filaments fallen from inside the lotuses.

In bowers of creepers adorned on every side with clusters of lotuses they saw
peacocks with their peahens, wild with love at the drumming of the clouds.
Making their sweet cries, like singing, spreading their bright tails, playful
and heavy with passion, the bright peacocks danced, the dancers of the forest.
They saw others taking their pleasure happily with their mates, on ledges
crowded with vines and creepers. They saw some birds perched high on branches,
their tails arrayed like bright crowns, and others happy in the hollows of the
trees.

On the mountain peaks they saw sinduvara trees, unbridled, strewn with golden
flowers, like the lances of the god of love. They saw karnikaras set like the
finest ear ornaments. They saw kurabakas in flower along the rows of the forest,
like a volley of Kama's arrows, stirring those in love's power to longing. They
saw tilaka trees shining like the tilaka marks on the forehead of the forest
rows. They saw lovely mango trees shaped like the arrows of the Bodiless One,
humming with bees, bright with their clusters of blossom. With flowers like
gold, like a forest fire, red, dark as collyrium, the colour of beryl, the
shala, tamala, patali and bakula trees clung like garlands to the mountain
peaks.

So, little by little, the heroes looked all round. The slopes of Gandhamadana
were crowded with herds of elephants and full of lions and tigers. They were
loud with the cries of sharabhas, ringing with sounds of every kind, and rich in
the fruits and flowers of every season. There the rows of forest turned yellow
and shining. None of the trees on the slopes of Gandhamadana had thorns, none
was without flowers, and all had glossy leaves and fruit. The Parthas saw on the
mountain slopes lakes and rivers like clear crystal, with white-winged birds,
with royal swans, loud with cranes. They were bright with lotuses and lilies,
their water pleasant to the touch. There were fragrant garlands and juicy
fruits, and the trees in flower shone greatly on the mountain slopes. These and
many other forest trees were there, and creepers of many shapes, heaped with
leaves, flowers and fruit.

---

Yudhishthira, looking at those trees on that best of mountains, spoke sweetly to
Bhimasena. "Look, Bhima, at these lovely places all round, where the gods take
their pleasure. We have reached a road beyond men, Vrikodara. We have been made
perfect. The finest trees, in flower, twined with many creepers, shine on the
slopes of Gandhamadana, Partha. Listen, Bhima, to the cry of the peacocks
roaming the mountain slopes with their peahens. Chakoras, woodpeckers, cuckoos
wild with love and mynas, these winged ones cling to the great trees in flower.
Look, Partha: many jivajivaka birds, red, yellow and pink, on the tops of the
trees, look at one another.

"All round the green and pink meadows, and by the mountain springs, cranes can
be seen. The bhringarajas, upachakras and lohaprishthas, the winged ones, speak
sweet words that go to the heart of every creature. These elephants with four
tusks, the colour of lotuses, with their cows, stir the great lake the colour of
beryl. Streams of water as tall as many palm trees fall from the mountain peak,
from many springs. Silver ores of many kinds, Bhima, bright as the sun, like
masses of autumn cloud, adorn the great mountain. In some places the ores are
the colour of collyrium, in others like gold. There are ores of orpiment and of
cinnabar in places, and caves of realgar like heaps of evening cloud. In places
there are ores of red chalk, the colour of a hare's blood. Like white and black
clouds, bright as the young sun, these ores of many kinds, very bright, adorn
the mountain.

"Gandharvas with their lovers can be seen on the mountain peaks with the
kimpurushas, Partha, as Vrishaparvan said. The sound of songs and of
hand-clapping and of Saman chants is heard in many ways, Bhima, delighting every
creature. Look at the Great Ganga, the holy, fine river of the gods, visited by
flocks of grey geese, where seers and kinnaras go. Look, son of Kunti, tamer of
enemies, at the king of mountains. It is rich with ores and rivers, with
kinnaras, beasts and birds, with gandharvas and apsarases, with lovely forests,
and all round with serpents of many shapes, with a hundred heads."

The brave ones, scorchers of enemies, were glad at heart. They had reached the
highest road, and they could not have enough of looking at that king of
mountains. Then they saw the hermitage of the royal seer Arshtishena, surrounded
with garlands of flowers and trees in fruit. Then they went up to Arshtishena,
fierce in his austerity, lean, his veins standing out, who had gone to the far
shore of every dharma.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi's speech.** A rejected passage (\*794) has Draupadi point out the trees to Bhima. In the CE the only speaker is Yudhishthira (3.155.69–87).
* **More description.** Rejected passages (\*787, \*789–\*793, \*795–\*797) add further descriptions of the mountain, its animals and its lotus pools. Not
  narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.155.2 *jayam*: Jaya, one of Arjuna's names.
* 3.155.3–5: "about the fifth year". Arjuna's agreement to live five years in heaven to learn is reported here by Yudhishthira.
* 3.155.17 *vṛṣaparvaṇaḥ*: Vrishaparvan, called a royal seer. The text does not say whether he is the asura king of that name in Book 1 (1.71–78); the
  novel does not say either.
* 3.155.21 *nyāsabhūtān*: "as a trust". The brahmins are left in his keeping.
* 3.155.40–46, 57–62: the trees are named as the text names them. English names are used where there is a plain one (mango, coconut, pomegranate,
  citron, jackfruit, plantain, date palm, wood apple, rose-apple, jujube, banyan, holy fig, cluster fig, marking nut, oleander, deodar, palmyra,
  silk-cotton); otherwise the Indian name is kept. *indīvara* is a blue lotus in the list of trees and is kept as the text has it.
* 3.155.47–50, 73–76: the birds are named as the text names them. *śatapatra* is taken as "woodpecker", *kalaviṅka* as "sparrow", *hārīta* as "green
  pigeon", *śārikā* as "myna", *kadamba* (a bird here) as "geese", *kurara* as "osprey", *madgu* as "cormorant"; the others keep their Indian names.
* 3.155.60 *anaṅgaśarākārān*: "shaped like the arrows of the Bodiless One", the god of love.
* 3.155.70 *amānuṣagatiṃ prāptāḥ saṃsiddhāḥ sma*: "We have reached a road beyond men. We have been made perfect."
* 3.155.81 *śaśalohitavarṇābhāḥ*: "the colour of a hare's blood".
* 3.155.87 *śataśīrṣaiḥ*: serpents "with a hundred heads".
* 3.155.90 *dhamanisaṃtatam*: "his veins standing out", the mark of long fasting.
