# The Race to Vidarbha

"When King Rituparna heard Sudeva's words," said Brihadashva, "he spoke gently
to Bahuka. 'I want to go to the Vidarbhas, to Damayanti's bridegroom choice, in
a single day, if you think it can be done, Bahuka, you who know the truth of
horses.'

"When the king said this, son of Kunti, Nala's heart split with sorrow, and the
great man brooded. 'Could Damayanti do this? Has sorrow driven her out of her
mind? Or is this a great plan she has thought of for my sake? Ah, the poor
princess of Vidarbha means to do a cruel thing, wronged by me, low, wicked and
foolish as I am. A woman's nature is fickle in this world, and my fault is
terrible. She might do this, helpless, her love gone, the slender one, shaken by
grief for me, out of despair. No, she would never do such a thing, least of all
with children. I will go and find out for certain what is true and what is good.
I will do what Rituparna wants, and what I want for myself.'

"When he had decided this in his mind, Bahuka, sick at heart, joined his hands
and said to King Rituparna, 'I promise you truly, lord of men, you will reach
the city of Vidarbha in a single day, tiger among men, king.'

"Then at the command of King Bhangasvari, king, Bahuka went to the stables and
tested the horses. Hurried again and again by Rituparna, Bahuka chose thin
horses, able and enduring on the road, full of spirit and strength, well bred
and well trained. They were free of bad marks, with wide nostrils and great
jaws, true in their ten curls of hair, bred in Sindhu, swift as the wind.

"When the king saw them, he said, somewhat angry, 'What are you trying to do?
Are you making fools of us? How can these horses of mine carry us, with so
little strength and wind? How can such horses go so far?'

"'These horses will go to the Vidarbhas, no doubt of it,' said Bahuka. 'But if
you think of others, king, tell me which I should yoke for you.'

"'You know the truth of horses, Bahuka, and you are skilled,' said Rituparna.
'Yoke quickly those you think are able.'

"Then the skilled man yoked four good horses to the chariot, well bred and well
trained and full of speed. The king mounted the yoked chariot in a hurry, and at
once those fine horses fell to the ground on their knees. Then the splendid
Nala, the best of men, lord of the people, soothed the horses, full of spirit
and strength. He gathered them up with the reins and made ready to go, putting
the charioteer Varshneya in the chariot and setting out at top speed. Driven by
Bahuka in the proper way, those fine horses flew up as if into the sky, almost
bewildering the man in the chariot.

"When the wise lord of Ayodhya saw those horses running as swift as the wind, he
was filled with wonder. When Varshneya heard the chariot's roar and saw that
handling of the horses, he thought about Bahuka's knowledge of horses. 'Could he
be Matali, the charioteer of the king of the gods? For the great signs are there
in the hero Bahuka. Or could he be Shalihotra, who knows the nature of the
breeds of horses, come here in a human body of the greatest beauty? Or could he
be King Nala, conqueror of enemy cities? Has the king come?' So he thought. 'Or
perhaps Bahuka knows the same art that Nala knows. For I see that Bahuka's
knowledge is equal to Nala's. And I think his age is the same as Nala's. He is
not Nala, the mighty one, but he must know Nala's art. For great men go about
this earth in disguise, under the rule of fate, with the changes of shape the
texts speak of. I might doubt it because of his misshapen body, for I think he
is less than Nala's size. His age and size are the same, but his looks are the
opposite. Still, in the end, I think Bahuka is Nala, with all his virtues.'

"So Varshneya, the charioteer of the king of good name, thought it over again
and again in his heart, great king. But King Rituparna, king of kings, thinking
of Bahuka's knowledge of horses, rejoiced, with Varshneya his charioteer. Seeing
his strength, his courage, his energy, his handling of the horses and his great
effort, he was filled with joy.

---

"He crossed rivers and mountains, forests and lakes in no time, like a bird
flying in the sky. As the chariot went on like this, King Bhangasvari, conqueror
of enemy cities, saw that his upper cloth had fallen. When the cloth fell, the
great-hearted king said to Nala in a hurry, 'I will get it back. Hold these
swift horses, wise one, while Varshneya brings me my cloth.'

"Nala answered him, 'Your cloth fell far back. We have gone a league past it.
You cannot get it again.'

"When Nala said this, King Bhangasvari, king, came to a vibhitaka tree in the
forest, covered with fruit. When he saw it, the king said quickly to Bahuka,
'See, charioteer, my great power at counting. Not everyone knows everything.
There is no one who knows all. Knowledge never rests whole in any one man. On
this tree, Bahuka, the leaves and fruit that have fallen are a hundred and one
more than those still on it. On these two branches there are fifty million
leaves. Count these two branches, and the twigs that grow from them. On these
two there are two thousand and ninety-five fruits.'

"Then Bahuka jumped down from the chariot and said to the king, 'You boast to
me, king, of something beyond my sight, scourge of enemies. When I have counted,
king, it will not be beyond sight. Before your eyes, great king, I will count
the vibhitaka. For I do not know whether it is so or not. I will count its
fruits in your sight, lord of the people. Let Varshneya hold the horses' reins
for a moment.'

"The king said to the charioteer, 'This is no time to delay.' But Bahuka, set on
his purpose, said, 'Wait a moment. Or, if you are in a hurry, the road ahead is
good. Go on, with Varshneya as your charioteer.'

"Rituparna said to him, soothing him, joy of the Kurus, 'You are the only
driver, Bahuka. There is no other on earth. It is because of you that I hope to
go to the Vidarbhas, master of horses. I come to you for refuge. Do not put
obstacles in my way. I will do whatever you ask of me, Bahuka, if you bring me
to the Vidarbhas today while the sun is still in the sky.'

"Bahuka said to him, 'When I have counted this vibhitaka, I will go to the
Vidarbhas. Do as I say.'

"As if against his will, the king said to him, 'Count.' Bahuka quickly got down
from the chariot and cut down the tree. When he had counted, he was filled with
wonder, and said to the king, 'There are just as many fruits as you said. I have
seen this very wonderful power of yours, king. I want to hear the art by which
this is known, king.'

"The king, in a hurry to go on, said to him, 'Know that I know the secret of the
dice, and am skilled at counting.'

"Bahuka said to him, 'Give me this art. And take from me the secret of horses,
bull among men.'

"Because of the importance of the business, and because he longed for the
knowledge of horses, King Rituparna said to Bahuka, 'So be it. Take the highest
secret of the dice, as you wish. Let the secret of horses stay with you as my
deposit, Bahuka.' With these words Rituparna gave the art to Nala.

"As soon as Nala knew the secret of the dice, Kali came out of his body,
spitting out from his mouth, as he came, the sharp poison of Karkotaka. The fire
of the curse came out of Kali too, in his torment, the fire by which the king
had been worn down for a long time, not himself. Then Kali, free of the poison,
took his own form. Nala, the lord of the Nishadhas, was angry and wanted to
curse him. But Kali, frightened and trembling, joined his hands and said to him,
'Hold back your anger, king. I will give you the highest fame. Indrasena's
mother cursed me long ago in her anger, when you had abandoned her. Since then I
have lived in you, king of kings, unconquered one, in great pain, burning day
and night with the poison of the king of snakes. Men who tell of you tirelessly
in the world will never have any fear that comes from me.'

"When Kali said this, King Nala held back his anger. Then Kali, frightened,
quickly entered the vibhitaka tree. And while Kali spoke with the Naishadha, no
one else could see him.

"Then the king of the Nishadhas, killer of enemy heroes, free from his fever,
with Kali gone, king, counted the fruits. Full of the greatest joy and
splendour, he mounted the chariot and drove on with his swift horses, shining.
And the vibhitaka tree fell into ill repute, because Kali had taken refuge in
it. With a glad heart Nala urged on those fine horses, which flew up again and
again like birds. The great-hearted king drove towards the Vidarbhas. When Nala
had gone, Kali too went home. Then King Nala was free of his fever, lord of the
earth, freed by Kali, king, lacking only his own form.

---

"Then at evening people told King Bhima that Rituparna, true in his courage, had
reached the Vidarbhas. At Bhima's word the king entered the city of Kundina,
filling all ten quarters and the spaces between with the roar of his chariot.
Nala's horses there heard the roar of the chariot, and when they heard it, they
were glad, as they used to be when Nala was near. Damayanti too heard that roar
of Nala's chariot, deep, like a cloud thundering when the rains come. She
thought the sound of the chariot was like the sound of old, when Nala held the
reins of Nala's horses, and so did the horses. The peacocks on the terraces, the
elephants in their stalls and the horses there all heard the roar of the king's
chariot. When the elephants and the peacocks heard the roar of the chariot,
king, they raised their heads and cried out, as if they saw the clouds rising.

"'The sound of that chariot, as if filling the earth, gladdens my heart,' said
Damayanti. 'It is Nala, the lord of the earth. If I do not see Nala today, with
his face like the moon, the hero of countless virtues, I shall die, no doubt of
it. If I do not go today into the space between that hero's arms, so good to
touch, I shall die, no doubt of it. If the Naishadha, with his voice like
thunder, does not come to me today, shining like gold, I shall die, no doubt of
it. If the king of kings, with the courage of a lion, who holds back maddened
elephants, does not come to me, I shall die, no doubt of it.

"'I do not remember a single lie of his, nor a single wrong he did, nor a word
of his left unkept, even in jest, the great man. My Naishadha is a lord,
patient, heroic, gentle, self-controlled, master of his senses, and in private
he keeps to me alone, as if other women did not exist for him. As I remember his
virtues day and night, given up to him, my heart, parted from my love, is
breaking with grief.'

"Lamenting like this, Bharata, as if she had lost her senses, she climbed to the
top of the great palace, longing to see the king of good name. Then in the
middle courtyard she saw King Rituparna, lord of the earth, standing in his
chariot, with Varshneya and Bahuka. Then Varshneya and Bahuka got down from the
fine chariot, unyoked the horses and set the chariot in its place. King
Rituparna stepped down from the chariot and went to Bhima of terrible courage,
great king. Bhima received him with the highest honour. He knew nothing of the
woman's plan behind this sudden, unexpected visit.

"'What is your business? You are welcome,' the king asked him, Bharata. He did
not know that he had come for his daughter. And wise King Rituparna, true in his
courage, saw no king or prince at all, no talk of a bridegroom choice, no
gathering of brahmins. Then the lord of the Kosalas, thinking it over in his
mind, said to him, 'I have come to pay my respects to you.'

"King Bhima smiled, and thought in his mind that the reason for his coming a
hundred leagues and more was something else. 'He has passed many villages, and
not come here for this. The reason he gives for coming is a small one. It is not
so.' The king treated him with honour and let him go, saying again and again,
'Rest. You are tired.' Honoured, pleased and glad, the king went with the king's
servants to the house set aside for him.

"When King Rituparna had gone, with Varshneya, king, Bahuka took the chariot to
the chariot shed. He unyoked the horses and tended them as the texts lay down,
soothed them himself, and sat down in the chariot.

"But Damayanti, in her grief, when she saw King Bhangasvari and the charioteer's
son Varshneya, and Bahuka as he was, wondered, 'Whose is that sound of a
chariot? It was great, like Nala's, and I do not see the Naishadha. Surely
Varshneya must have learned the same art, and so his chariot's roar was great,
like Nala's. Or is King Rituparna the same as Nala, so that the sound of this
chariot seems like the Naishadha's?'

"When she had reasoned like this, lord of the people, Damayanti sent a woman
messenger to look for the Naishadha.

"'Go, Keshini,' said Damayanti, 'and find out who this chariot driver is,
sitting in the chariot, misshapen, with short arms. Go up to him, good woman,
carefully, and ask after his welfare, gently at first, and question the man as
to the truth, blameless one. I have a strong suspicion that this may be King
Nala. It would be my heart's content and my heart's peace. At the end of your
talk, tell him what Parnada said, and mark his answer, lovely one, blameless
one.'

"So the messenger went, carefully, and spoke to Bahuka. And lovely Damayanti
watched from the palace.

"'Welcome, lord of men,' said Keshini. 'I wish you well. Listen to Damayanti's
words, bull among men. When did you set out, and why have you come here? Tell me
properly. The princess of Vidarbha wants to hear it.'

"'The glorious king of the Kosalas heard that Damayanti's second bridegroom
choice would be tomorrow, lovely one,' said Bahuka. 'When he heard it, the king
set out with the finest horses, swift as the wind, that go a hundred leagues,
and I am his charioteer.'

"'And the third of you, where is he from, and whose is he?' said Keshini. 'And
whose are you, and how did this work come to you?'

"'He is the charioteer of the king of good name,' said Bahuka, 'known as
Varshneya. When Nala fled, good lady, he went to Bhangasvari. I too am skilled
with horses, and well practised as a cook. Rituparna himself chose me as his
charioteer and cook.'

"'Does Varshneya know where King Nala has gone?' said Keshini. 'He may have told
you something, Bahuka.'

"'He left the children of Nala, whose deeds were ill-omened, here, and then went
where he pleased,' said Bahuka. 'He does not know the Naishadha. No other man
knows Nala, glorious one. The king goes hidden in this world, his shape lost.
Only Nala himself knows Nala, and she who is closest to him. For his marks never
betray Nala at all.'

"'The brahmin who went to Ayodhya first,' said Keshini, 'kept saying these words
of a woman, again and again: "Where have you gone, gambler, my love, after
cutting half my cloth and leaving me, your loving dear wife, asleep in the
forest? She waits for you as you told her, burning day and night, covered by
half a cloth. She weeps all the time with that grief, king. Be kind, hero, and
give her an answer." Speak those words she loves, wise one. The blameless
princess of Vidarbha wants to hear that same answer. They say you gave an answer
when you heard this. The princess of Vidarbha wants to hear from you again what
you said before.'

"When Keshini said this to Nala, joy of the Kurus, his heart ached and his eyes
filled with tears. Holding back his sorrow, burning, the lord of the earth spoke
again in a voice choked with tears.

"'Women of good family, even when they meet misfortune, guard themselves by
themselves. Beyond doubt, the true ones win heaven. Even when their husbands
leave them, they are never angry. Good women keep their lives with their conduct
for armour. The dark woman should not be angry with him, when he wanted only to
stay alive, when the birds took his clothes and worries burned him. Whether he
treated her well or badly, she should see her husband come to this, fallen from
his kingdom, robbed of fortune, hungry and drowned in misfortune.'

"As he said these words, Nala, in the deepest sorrow, could not hold back his
tears, and he wept, Bharata. Then Keshini went and told Damayanti everything he
had said, and how it had moved him."

**Divergences.**

* **The marks of the horses.** A rejected passage (\*320) lists where the ten curls of hair should be.
  The CE says only "true in their ten curls" (3.69.12).
* **The merit of telling Nala's story.** A rejected passage (\*331) expands Kali's promise. In the CE
  he says only that men who tell of Nala will have no fear that comes from him (3.70.33).
