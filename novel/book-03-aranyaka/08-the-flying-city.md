# The Flying City

"When the Rajasuya, your great sacrifice, was over, king," said Vasudeva, "I
went to the city of the Anartas, which had been set free. I saw Dvaraka, great
king, robbed of its splendour. There was no chanting of the Veda, no sacrificial
cry, and the fine women wore no ornaments. The gardens of Dvaraka could not be
recognised. When I saw this I grew anxious and asked Hridika's son, 'The men and
women of this city of the Vrishnis are sick at heart. What is it, tiger among
men? We want to hear.' When I asked him, Hardikya told me at length of the siege
by Shalva and how it had been lifted, best of kings.

"When I had heard all of it, best of the Kauravas, I made up my mind then and
there to destroy King Shalva. I comforted the people of the city, best of the
Bharatas, and King Ahuka and Anakadundubhi, and cheered all the chief heroes of
the Vrishnis. I said, 'Bulls of the Yadavas, always keep watch in the city. Know
that I have gone to destroy King Shalva. I will not come back to the city of
Dvaravati without killing him. When I have killed Shalva and the city of Saubha
I will see you again. Let the war drum that terrifies enemies be beaten three
times.'

"When I had cheered them as was fitting, bull of the Bharatas, the heroes all
said to me gladly, 'Go, and kill your enemies.' Greeted by those glad heroes
with blessings, I had the best of the brahmins speak blessings, and bowed my
head to Ahuka. Then I set out, king, in my chariot yoked to Sainya and Sugriva,
filling the quarters with the sound of my fine conch Panchajanya. I was
surrounded by a great army, tiger among men, a well-ordered army of four
branches, sure of victory.

"I passed through many lands, over mountains full of trees, and lakes and
rivers, and came to Marttikavata. There I heard, tiger among men, that Shalva
had set out from near the city, riding in Saubha, and I followed him. Then I
came to the ocean, and Shalva, riding in Saubha, was in the belly of the
great-waved sea, at the navel of the ocean, killer of enemies. The wicked one
saw me from far off, Yudhishthira, and as if smiling he challenged me again and
again to fight. My many arrows, loosed from Sharnga, that pierce the vitals,
could not reach the city, and anger took hold of me.

"Then that low daitya, wicked by nature, hard to overcome, king, rained streams
of arrows on me by the thousand. He covered my soldiers, my driver and my
horses. But we fought on, Bharata, paying no heed to the arrows. Then the heroes
who followed Shalva loosed hundreds of thousands of straight arrows at me in
battle. The asuras covered my horses, my chariot and Daruka with arrows that
pierce the vitals. Neither my horses nor my chariot nor my driver Daruka could
be seen, hero, so covered were they with arrows, nor could I or my soldiers.

"Then I too, Kauravya, loosed many tens of thousands of arrows from my divine
bow, charged with spells in the proper way. But my army could not reach it,
Bharata. For Saubha hung in the sky about a krosha up. Then all the onlookers,
standing as if in the enclosure of a theatre, cheered me loudly with lions'
roars and the clapping of hands. The arrows loosed from my bow in the great
battle entered the bodies of the danavas, wet with blood, like moths. Then a cry
of alarm grew in the middle of Saubha, as they were killed by the sharp arrows
and fell into the great ocean. With their arms and shoulders cut off, looking
like headless trunks, the danavas fell, roaring terribly.

"Then I filled with my breath the conch Panchajanya, born of the waters, shining
like cow's milk, jasmine, the moon, lotus stalks and silver. When Shalva, lord
of Saubha, saw them fallen, he fought me in the battle with great magic.
Hudahudas, lances, spears, pikes and axes, pattishas and bhushundis fell on me
without stopping. I caught them with magic and quickly destroyed them. When that
magic was destroyed, he fought with the peaks of mountains. Then there was
darkness, and there was dawn, there was a foul day and a fair one, cold and
heat, Bharata. So my enemy fought me, working his magic. I understood it all,
and destroyed it with magic, and in due course I scattered it everywhere with my
arrows.

"Then the sky seemed to have a hundred suns, great king, and a hundred moons,
son of Kunti, and ten million stars. Then no one could tell day from night, or
the quarters. I was bewildered, and I used the weapon of wisdom. That weapon
blew the illusion away like tufts of cotton. So the battle raged, tumultuous and
hair-raising, and when I could see again, king of kings, I fought the enemy once
more.

---

"So Shalva, tiger among men, the great enemy of kings, as he fought me in
battle, went up into the sky again. Then the fool, in his anger, wanting
victory, great king, hurled at me shataghnis and great maces, blazing spears,
pestles and swords. As they came at me, I quickly stopped them in the sky with
swift arrows flying through the air. I cut them in two and in three with arrows
swiftly loosed, and a great noise rose in the sky. Then he covered Daruka, the
horses and the chariot with hundreds of thousands of straight arrows.

"Then Daruka, hero, said to me, as if faltering, 'I stand, because I must stand.
But I am hurt by Shalva's arrows.' When I heard my driver's pitiful words, I
looked at him and saw him hurt by the arrows. On his chest, his head, his body,
his two arms, best of the Pandavas, I saw no place not struck by arrows. Blood
was streaming from him, from the press of the fine arrows, like a mountain rich
in red chalk in the rain. When I saw my driver, the reins in his hands, sinking
in the battle, hurt by Shalva's arrows, strong-armed one, I steadied him.

"Then a man who lived in Dvaraka came quickly up to my chariot, Bharata, as if
out of friendship, and spoke to me. He was a servant of Ahuka's, with a message
from him, hero. He was downcast and his throat was choked. Hear it,
Yudhishthira. 'Hero, Ahuka, lord of Dvaraka, has this message for you. Keshava,
hear what your father's friend says. Today, while you were caught up here,
unconquerable joy of the Vrishnis, Shalva came to Dvaraka and killed the son of
Shura by force. So enough of fighting. Turn back, Janardana. Guard Dvaraka. That
is your great duty now.'

"When I heard his words I was in deep despair. I could not decide what to do, or
what not to do. When I heard that hateful news, hero, I blamed Satyaki, Baladeva
and the great chariot fighter Pradyumna in my mind. For I had set them to guard
Dvaraka and my father, joy of the Kurus, when I set out to bring down Saubha.
'Is strong-armed Baladeva, the killer of enemies, alive? And Satyaki, the son of
Rukmini, mighty Charudeshna, and Samba and the rest?' So I thought in great
distress. For while they lived, tiger among men, not even the thunderbolt-bearer
himself could kill the son of Shura. 'The son of Shura is plainly dead, and
plainly they are all dead too, Baladeva and the rest.' That was my firm belief.
Thinking again and again of that total ruin, I fought Shalva again, great king,
greatly shaken.

"Then, great king, I saw the son of Shura, hero, falling from Saubha, and I
fainted. My father looked, as he fell, lord of men, like Yayati falling from
heaven to earth when his merit was spent. His turban was torn and falling, his
clothes and hair were scattered, and he was seen falling like a planet whose
merit is spent. Then Sharnga, the best of bows, fell from my hand, and in my
faintness, son of Kunti, I sank down in the seat of the chariot. Then my whole
army cried out in dismay, senseless, seeing me in the body of the chariot as if
I were dead, Bharata. With his arms and legs spread out as he fell, my father
looked like a falling bird. As he fell, strong-armed one, heroes with lances and
pattishas in their hands struck him hard, and my heart shook.

"Then after a moment I came to my senses, hero, in that great battle. I saw no
Saubha there, no enemy, no Shalva, and no old father either. Then I was sure in
my mind that this was magic. I woke up again, and scattered arrows by the
hundred.

---

"Then, best of the Bharatas, I took up my fine bow and with my arrows I brought
down from Saubha the heads of the enemies of the gods. I sent at King Shalva
arrows like poisonous snakes, flying upwards, of fierce power, well feathered,
loosed from Sharnga. Then Saubha could not be seen, pride of the Kuru line. It
had vanished by magic, and I was amazed. Then those hordes of danavas, with
hideous faces and hair, cried out, great king, as I stood there, Bharata. Then
in the great battle I quickly used the weapon that finds its mark by sound, to
kill them, and the noise stopped. All the danavas who had raised that noise were
killed by arrows like the sun, blazing, that strike by sound. When that noise
stopped, another noise rose from elsewhere, great king, and I loosed arrows
there too. So the asuras made all ten quarters ring, across and above, Bharata,
and I killed them too.

"Then Saubha, which goes where it wills, went to Pragjyotisha and appeared
again, hero, dazzling my eyes. Then a danava in the shape of a monkey, a
destroyer of worlds, suddenly covered me with a great rain of rocks. Struck on
every side by the rain of mountains, king of kings, I became like an anthill
piled with mountains. Piled with mountains, king, with my horses, my driver and
my banner, I could not be seen at all. Then the heroes of the Vrishnis who were
my soldiers were terrified, and suddenly fled in every direction. Then, they
say, a cry of dismay went up from everything, lord of the people, from heaven,
earth and sky, when I could not be seen. Then my friends, king, were downcast,
and wept and cried out, full of grief and sorrow. My enemies were delighted, and
even those who were not my enemies were distressed. So I heard afterwards,
unfallen one, when I had won.

"Then I raised my favourite weapon, the thunderbolt, which splits every rock,
and smashed all those mountains. My horses, weighed down by the mountains,
barely breathing and moving, seemed to tremble, great king. When my kinsmen saw
me, like the sun rising after breaking through a net of clouds in the sky, they
were glad again.

"Then my driver, bowing, with joined hands, said to me, king, 'Look well,
Varshneya, at Shalva, lord of Saubha, as he stands there. Enough, Krishna, of
slighting him. Make a real effort. Put away gentleness and friendship towards
Shalva today. Kill Shalva, strong-armed one. Do not let him live, Keshava. An
enemy must be killed with every kind of courage, hero, killer of enemies. The
stronger man must never despise an enemy, even a weak one, even one who stays at
home, let alone one who stands in battle. So, tiger among men, kill him with
every effort, my lord, best of the Vrishni family. Do not let the time pass you
by again. He is not to be won over by gentleness, and he is no friend of yours,
hero, this man who has fought you and wrecked Dvaraka.'

"When I heard these and other words of my driver's, son of Kunti, I knew they
were true, and set my mind on the fight, to kill King Shalva and bring Saubha
down. I said to Daruka, 'Stand still a moment, hero.' Then I took up my
favourite weapon, the weapon of fire, unstoppable, divine, unbreakable, of great
power. It bears all things, it is of great radiance, and it burns to ashes in
battle yakshas, rakshasas and danavas, and hostile kings. I charged with spells
the matchless discus, pure, edged like a razor, like Time, the Ender and Yama,
destroyer of enemies. 'Destroy Saubha by your own power, and all my enemies
here.' With these words, in my anger, I hurled it at him with all the strength
of my arm.

"As Sudarshana fell through the sky it looked like a second sun with a halo at
the end of the age. It reached the city of Saubha, whose splendour had gone, and
cut it through the middle, as a saw cuts a tall log. Cut in two, struck by the
power of Sudarshana, Saubha fell, as the triple city fell when shaken by the
arrow of the great lord. When Saubha had fallen, the discus came back to my
hand. I sent it off again with force, crying, 'At Shalva!' Then, as Shalva
whirled his heavy mace in the great battle, it suddenly cut him in two, and he
blazed up with its fire.

"When that hero had fallen, the danavas, terrified, crying out in dismay, fled
in all directions, hurt by my arrows. Then I drew up my chariot near Saubha,
blew my conch in joy, and cheered my friends. When they saw that city, like the
peak of Meru, its towers and gates shattered, burning, the women there fled. So,
when I had killed Shalva in battle and brought Saubha down, I came back to the
Anartas and gave my friends joy.

"That is why, king, I did not come to the city named after the elephant, killer
of enemy heroes. If I had come, Suyodhana would not be alive."

---

When he had said this to the Kaurava, the strong-armed, wise Madhusudana, the
highest of beings, took leave of the Pandavas to go. The strong-armed one bowed
to Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, and the king and Bhima smelled his head. He
had Subhadra and Abhimanyu mount his golden chariot, and Krishna mounted it,
honoured by the Pandavas. When he had comforted Yudhishthira, Krishna went to
Dvaraka in his chariot, as bright as the sun, yoked to Sainya and Sugriva.

When the Dasharha had gone, Dhrishtadyumna, the son of Prishata, went back to
his own city too, taking the sons of Draupadi. Dhrishtaketu, king of the Chedis,
took his sister and went to the lovely city of Shuktimati, when he had seen the
Pandavas. The Kekayas too, with the leave of the son of Kunti of boundless
power, took leave of all the Pandavas and went, Bharata.

But the brahmins, the vaishyas and the people of the country would not leave the
Pandavas, though they were urged to go. That gathering of those great men in the
Kamyaka forest was a great and wonderful sight, king of kings, bull of the
Bharatas. Then the great-minded Yudhishthira, with the brahmins' leave, told his
men, when the time came, "Yoke the chariots."
