# The Forest

"When Varshneya had gone," said Brihadashva, "Pushkara won from the king of good
name, as he played, his kingdom and every other thing of value he had. Pushkara
laughed and said to Nala, who had lost his kingdom, king, 'Let the game go on.
What stake have you left? Only Damayanti is left to you. Everything else I have
won. Let Damayanti be the stake, if you think fit.'

"When Pushkara said this, the heart of the king of good name seemed to split
with anger, but he said nothing to him. Nala, the glorious, looked at Pushkara,
full of fury, and took off all the ornaments from his body. With one garment on,
his body bare, making his friends' sorrow grow, the king went out, leaving his
great fortune behind. Damayanti, in one garment, followed him as he went. The
Naishadha stayed with her outside the city for three nights.

"But Pushkara, great king, had it proclaimed in the city: 'Whoever treats Nala
well shall be put to death by me.' Because of Pushkara's words and his hatred of
Nala, the people of the city did not treat him with kindness, Yudhishthira. So
the king, who deserved kindness and received none, stayed near the city for
three nights, living on water alone.

"Tormented by hunger, after many days Nala saw some birds with wings like gold.
The mighty lord of the Nishadhas thought, 'This will be food for me today, and
wealth as well.' He threw the cloth he wore over them. The birds all took his
cloth and flew off into the sky. As they flew up, the birds spoke to Nala,
seeing him standing on the ground naked, wretched, his head hung low. 'We are
the dice, you fool. We came to take your clothes. We were not pleased that you
went away with clothes on.'

"When he saw the dice gone and himself without clothes, the king of good name
said to Damayanti, 'Blameless one, look at them. Their anger threw me down from
my lordship, so that I cannot find a living, wretched and tormented by hunger.
For their sake the Nishadhas would not treat me kindly. Now they have become
birds and taken even my clothes from me. I have come to the worst of misfortune,
I am wretched and out of my mind. I am your husband. Listen to these words for
your own good.

"'These many roads go to the southern country, past Avanti and past the mountain
Rikshavat. This is the great mountain Vindhya, and the Payoshni, which flows to
the sea. Those are the hermitages of the great seers, full of flowers and fruit.
This road goes to the Vidarbhas, and that one to the Kosalas. Beyond, to the
south, is the southern country.'

"Then Damayanti, worn with sorrow, said to the Naishadha pitifully, in a voice
thick with tears. 'My heart is trembling, and all my limbs give way, king, when
I think again and again of what you have in mind. You have lost your kingdom and
your wealth, you have no clothes, you are worn with hunger and weariness. How
could I leave you and go, in the empty forest? When you are tired and tormented
by hunger, great king, and thinking of the happiness you had, I will take away
your weariness in the terrible forest. There is no medicine, as the doctors
hold, for every sorrow, like a wife. I tell you the truth.'

"'It is just as you say, Damayanti, slim-waisted one,' said Nala. 'A man in
trouble has no friend and no medicine like a wife. I have no wish to leave you.
Why are you afraid, timid one? I would leave my own life, blameless one, but
never you.'

"'If you do not want to leave me here, great king,' said Damayanti, 'why do you
point out the road to the Vidarbhas? I know, king, that you would not leave me.
But your mind has been dragged away, and you might leave me, great lord. You
keep telling me the road, best of men, and for that reason you make my sorrow
grow, you who shine like an immortal. If this is what you intend, king, that I
should go to my people, then let us go to the Vidarbhas together, if you agree.
The king of Vidarbha will honour you there, giver of honour. Honoured by him,
king, you will live happily in our house.'

---

"'Your father's kingdom is as good as mine, no doubt of it,' said Nala. 'But I
will never go there in this plight. I went there rich, and made your joy grow.
How can I go there ruined, and make your sorrow grow?'

"So King Nala spoke to Damayanti again and again, and comforted the good woman,
who was covered by half his cloth. The two of them, wrapped in one garment,
wandering here and there, worn out with hunger and thirst, came to a rest house.
When the lord of the Nishadhas reached that rest house, the king sat down on the
ground with the princess of Vidarbha. Without clothes, dirty, his hair unkempt,
covered in dust, he lay down on the ground with Damayanti, worn out, and slept.
Damayanti too, the good woman, tender and pitiable, was carried off by sleep, so
suddenly had sorrow come on her.

"While Damayanti slept, King Nala, lord of the people, his mind and heart
churned by grief, did not sleep as he used to. He looked at the loss of his
kingdom, at the desertion of all his friends, and at his ruin in the forest, and
he fell to thinking. 'What will happen to me if I do this? What will happen if I
do not? Is death better for me, or to leave my people? She is devoted to me, and
suffers this sorrow because of me. Without me she might go at some time to her
own people. With me, beyond doubt, this peerless woman will find sorrow. If I
leave her, there is a chance. She might even find happiness somewhere.'

"He decided, turning it over in his mind again and again, and the king thought
it best to leave Damayanti. He thought of his own nakedness and of her single
cloth, and the king made up his mind to cut the cloth in half. 'How can I cut
the cloth without my darling waking?' Thinking this, the Naishadha king walked
about the rest house. As Nala ran here and there, Bharata, he found a fine sword
in one part of the rest house, out of its sheath. With it the scourge of his
enemies cut the cloth in half and put it on, and leaving the princess of
Vidarbha asleep, he ran away, out of his mind.

"Then, his heart bound to her, the lord of the Nishadhas came back to the rest
house, and seeing Damayanti there, he wept. 'My darling, whom neither the wind
nor the sun saw before, sleeps today on the ground in the middle of a rest
house, like a woman with no protector. How will she be, when she wakes, this
lovely one with the sweet smile, wrapped in a cut piece of cloth, like a
madwoman? How will Bhima's good daughter, alone, parted from me, wander in the
terrible forest, full of beasts and snakes?'

"King Nala went, and went, and came back again and again to the rest house. He
was dragged away by Kali and drawn back by love. His heart seemed to split in
two in his misery. Like a swing he came back again and again, and went again and
again from the rest house. At last, dragged away by Kali, Nala ran off,
bewildered, leaving his wife asleep, after many pitiful laments. His self lost,
touched by Kali, thinking of this and that, the king went on into the empty
forest, grieving, and left his wife behind.

---

"When Nala had gone, king, Damayanti, rested now, woke in terror in the empty
forest. Not seeing her husband, full of sorrow and grief, she cried aloud in
terror to the Naishadha, 'Great king!'

"'Ah, my lord! Ah, great king! Ah, my master, why do you leave me? Ah, I am
killed, I am lost, I am afraid in the empty forest. Great king, surely you know
dharma and speak the truth. How could you say what you did, and then leave me
asleep and go, so falsely? How could you leave your wife and go, who is obedient
and devoted to you, and has done you no wrong, when it was another who wronged
you? Will you be able to make those words true, lord of men, that you spoke to
me long ago in the presence of the guardians of the world? This joke has gone
far enough, bull among men. I am afraid, unassailable one. Show yourself, lord.
I see you, I see you, king, there you are, Naishadha! You have hidden yourself
in the bushes. Why do you not answer me? It is cruel, king of kings, that you do
not hold me and comfort me, lord of the earth, when I have come to this and am
weeping here. I do not grieve for myself, nor for anything else. How will you
be, alone? That is my grief for you, king. How will you be at evening, king,
thirsty, hungry, worn with weariness, at the foot of the trees, not seeing me?'

"Then, in bitter grief, as if blazing with anger, she ran here and there,
weeping in her misery. Again and again the girl leapt up, again and again she
fell, distraught. Again and again she hid, frightened, again and again she cried
out and wept. Burning with bitter grief, sighing again and again, distraught,
Bhima's daughter, devoted to her husband, came out and said, weeping, 'There is
a being by whose curse the Naishadha suffers this sorrow, in his misery. May
that being's sorrow be greater than his. May the wicked one who has done this to
Nala, whose mind is without sin, find sorrow worse than this, and live a life
without happiness.'

"So the wife of the great king lamented, and searched for her husband in the
forest full of beasts of prey. Like a madwoman, Bhima's daughter ran here and
there, crying again and again, 'Ah! Ah! King!' She was withering away, crying
like an osprey, grieving and lamenting pitifully, again and again. As she
wandered close by, not seeing, a huge, hungry python seized Bhima's daughter.

"As the python swallowed her, beaten by grief, she did not grieve for herself as
much as she grieved for the Naishadha. 'Ah, my lord, why do you not run to me,
as this python swallows me here in the forest, like a woman with no protector?
How will you be, Naishadha, when you remember me again, when you are freed from
evil and have your mind, your heart and your wealth again? When you are weary,
tormented by hunger, and faint, Naishadha, tiger among kings, giver of honour,
who will take away your weariness?'

"By chance a hunter of deer, wandering in the deep forest, heard her crying and
came quickly. When he saw the long-eyed woman seized by the snake, the hunter
hurried up at speed, and with a sharp blade he split the snake open from the
mouth. When he had killed the snake, and it lay still, the man who lived by
killing deer set her free and washed her with water. He comforted her and gave
her food, and then he asked her, Bharata, 'Whose are you, with the eyes of a
fawn? How did you come to the forest? How did you come into this great trouble,
lovely one?'

"When he asked her this, lord of the people, Damayanti told him everything,
Bharata, just as it had happened. The hunter looked at her, covered by half a
garment, with her full hips and breasts, tender, flawless in every limb. He saw
her face like the full moon, her eyes with their curling lashes, and heard her
sweet speech, and he fell into the power of desire. Tormented by desire, the
hunter began to court her with smooth and gentle words, and the lovely woman
understood.

"When Damayanti, devoted to her husband, saw that he was wicked, she was seized
with fierce fury and seemed to blaze with anger. The low, evil-minded man,
burning to force her, looked at her, unassailable as a blazing tongue of fire.
Then Damayanti, in her misery, robbed of husband and kingdom, when the time for
words had passed, cursed him in her anger, they say. 'As I do not think of any
man but the Naishadha, even in my mind, so may this low man who lives by killing
deer fall dead.' As soon as she had said the words, the hunter fell dead on the
ground, like a tree burnt by fire."

**Divergences.**

* **Nala's last stake.** A rejected passage (\*243) has Nala answer Pushkara and stake his upper
  cloth and her ornaments. In the CE he says nothing and takes his ornaments off himself (3.58.4–5).

  Adityas, Vasus, Rudras and Ashvins before he goes, and another (\*247) says he trusted her virtue
  to protect her. Not in the CE.
