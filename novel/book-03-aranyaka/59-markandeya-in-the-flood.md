# Markandeya in the Flood

Then the Dharma King Yudhishthira humbly questioned the glorious Markandeya
again. "Great sage, you have seen the end of many thousands of ages. No one here
is your equal in length of life, except the great brahmin, the Supreme Lord.
When the world is without sky, without gods and danavas, at the dissolution, you
alone wait on Brahma, brahmin. And when the dissolution is over and the
Grandfather wakes, you alone see the creatures being created here, the four
kinds of them, brahmin seer. You see the Supreme Lord makes the quarters into
wind and scatters the waters here and there. You have worshipped the teacher of
the world, the Grandfather of all the worlds, himself, with meditation fixed on
him, best of the twice-born. So Death, which ends all things, and old age, which
destroys the body, do not enter you, brahmin seer, by the grace of the Supreme
Lord.

"There comes a time when there is no sun, no fire, no wind and no moon, no sky
and no earth, and nothing at all is left. The world is one ocean. All that moves
and does not move is destroyed, the hosts of gods and asuras are destroyed and
the great snakes are gone. Then you alone wait on Brahma, the lord of all
beings, of boundless spirit, lying in the lotus, whose home is the lotus. You
have seen all this that happened before with your own eyes, best of the
twice-born. So we want to hear the story of the cause of everything. You alone
have lived through it many times, best of the twice-born. There is nothing in
all the worlds that you do not know."

"Yes, I will tell you," said Markandeya, "when I have bowed to the Self-born,
the ancient Person, eternal and unchanging. This Janardana here, with his wide,
long eyes, in yellow clothes, is the creator and the destroyer, the maker of
beings who makes all things be. He is beyond thought, a great wonder, pure and
highest, without beginning or end, the living, the all, unfailing and
unchanging. He is the maker, and no one makes him. He is the cause even of human
striving. The man who knows this Person, even the gods do not know him. All
this, best of kings, is a wonder that happened, from the beginning to the
destruction of the whole world, tiger among men.

"They say the Krita age is four thousand years. Its twilight is as many
hundreds, and the twilight's portion after it the same. The Treta age here is
said to be three thousand years, with a twilight of as many hundreds, and the
twilight's portion after it. So too the Dvapara is two thousand years in length,
with a twilight of two hundred, and the twilight's portion after it. Then the
Kali age is held to be a thousand years, with a twilight of a hundred years, and
the twilight's portion after it. Understand that the twilight and its portion
are equal in length. When the Kali age is over, the Krita age begins again. This
is what is called an age, twelve thousand years long. A thousand of these,
ending, is called a day of Brahma. The whole universe turns about completely in
the house of Brahma. The wise know that as the dissolution of the worlds, tiger
among men.

---

"When little is left of the end of the age, bull of the Bharatas, at the end of
the thousand, all men mostly speak untruth. In that time, Partha, there are
substitutes for sacrifice, substitutes for gifts and substitutes for vows. When
the age is gone, brahmins do the work of shudras, and shudras gather wealth or
live by the dharma of kshatriyas. In the Kali age brahmins will give up
sacrifice and study, will give up the offerings of rice balls and water to the
dead, and will eat everything. Brahmins will not recite, my son, and shudras
will be given to recitation. When the world is turned upside down like this, it
is the first sign of the destruction.

"There will be many mleccha kings on the earth, lord of men, evil, ruling
falsely, given to lies. Andhras, Shakas, Pulindas and Yavanas will be kings,
lord of men, and Kambojas, Aurnikas, shudras and Abhiras, best of men. Then no
brahmin will live by his own dharma, and kshatriyas and vaishyas too will do
work that is not theirs, lord of men. They will be short-lived, of little
strength, of little splendour and courage, small in body and in substance, and
they will speak little truth. The countries will be mostly empty, and the
quarters full of beasts and snakes, when the end of the age comes. Those who
keep the vow of celibacy will keep it for nothing. Shudras will say "Ho there!"
and brahmins will say "Sir."

"At the end of the age, tiger among men, there will be many creatures. Smells
will not have the same scent, lord of the people, and tastes will not be so
sweet, tiger among men. At the end of the age women will have many children, and
be short and without good conduct or good ways, and shameless, king. At the end
of the age, king, the countries will sell their food, the crossroads will sell
the Veda, and women will sell their hair.

"The cows will give little milk, lord of the people, and the trees will bear few
flowers and little fruit, and be full of crows. The twice-born will take gifts
from kings stained with the killing of brahmins and from false accusers, lord of
the earth. Overcome by greed and delusion, covered in a false flag of dharma,
the twice-born will roam the quarters begging, lord of the earth. Householders,
afraid of the burden of taxes, will become thieves, and men hidden in the dress
of sages will live by trade. Men will wear their nails and hair for show, tiger
among men, and those who keep the vow of celibacy will keep it for nothing, out
of greed for wealth. In the hermitages men will live idly, drinking, lying with
their teachers' wives, seeking only this world, the growth of flesh and blood.
At the end of the age the hermitages will be crowded with heretics, and men will
praise the virtues of other men's food, and the hermitages will be no more,
tiger among men.

"The blessed chastiser of Paka will not rain in season, and then the seeds will
not grow well, Bharata. The fruit of adharma will be great then, sinless one.
And then, lord of the earth, any man joined to dharma should be thought
short-lived, for there will be no dharma at all. People will mostly sell their
wares with false measures, and the merchants will be full of tricks, tiger among
men. The most righteous will waste away, and the wickeder man will grow. The
strength of dharma will fail, and adharma will be strong. At the end of the age
the most righteous men will be short-lived and poor, and those without dharma
long-lived and rich. The people will deal by the most wicked means, and with a
little wealth they will become rich and proud. Most men, full of tricks, will
set out to steal wealth left with them in trust, king. Man-eating creatures,
birds and beasts will lie in the pleasure grounds of the cities and in the
shrines.

"Girls of seven and eight will be with child, king, and sons will be born to men
of ten and twelve. Men will go grey at sixteen, and the life of men will quickly
run out. When the age is worn out, great king, the young will behave like the
old, and the ways of the young will be found in the old. Women will turn against
their ways, deceive their husbands in secret and go astray, wicked, even with
slaves and with beasts.

"When that end of the thousand ages has come, and life is running out, there is
a drought of many years, great king. Then the creatures, of little strength and
hungry, mostly perish on the earth, lord of the earth. Then seven blazing suns,
lord of men, drink up all the water in the oceans and rivers. Wood and grass,
dry and wet, all are seen turned to ash, bull of the Bharatas. Then the fire
called Samvartaka, with the wind, enters the world already dried up by the suns,
Bharata. Then it breaks through the earth, enters the underworld and brings
great fear to the gods, danavas and yakshas. Burning the world of the snakes and
everything that is on the earth, it destroys all below in a moment, lord of the
earth. Then that ill-omened wind and that fire, the Samvartaka, burn for
thousands and hundreds of stretches of twenty leagues. Then the blazing lord
burns the whole world, with its gods, asuras and gandharvas, its yakshas, snakes
and rakshasas.

"Then great clouds rise in the sky, like herds of elephants, wreathed in
garlands of lightning, wonderful to see. Some are dark as blue lotuses, some
like white lilies, some like lotus filaments, some yellow. Some are like
turmeric, some like crows' eggs, some like lotus petals, some bright as
vermilion. Some are like the finest cities, some like herds of elephants, some
like collyrium, some in the shape of makaras. The clouds rise, their bodies
bound with garlands of lightning. Terrible to see, great king, roaring terribly,
the clouds fill the whole sky. By them this whole earth, with its mountains,
forests and mines, is filled and flooded with streams of water, great king. Then
those terrible roaring clouds, urged on by the Supreme Lord, quickly flood
everything, bull among men. Raining great waters, filling the earth, they put
out the most terrible, ill-omened, fierce fire. Then for twelve years the
clouds, urged on by the Great Self, fill the flood with their streams. Then the
ocean goes over its shore, Bharata, the mountains split, and the earth splits
too. Suddenly, wheeling all round, the clouds wrap the sky and are destroyed,
struck by the force of the wind. Then the Self-born god, whose first home is the
lotus, drinks that terrible wind and sleeps, lord of men, Bharata.

---

"Then there is one terrible ocean. All that moves and does not move is
destroyed, the hosts of gods and asuras are destroyed, and there are no yakshas
or rakshasas, no men, lord of the earth, no beasts of prey and no trees. In that
world without a sky I wander alone, as he wills. Wandering in that one terrible
water, best of kings, and seeing no creatures, I fell into the greatest despair.
I went on a very long way, floating, lord of men, tireless, and weary, and I
found no refuge anywhere.

"Then at some time, in that flood of water, I saw a very great, wide banyan
tree, lord of the earth. On a broad branch of that tree, lord of men, there was
a couch spread with divine coverings, lord of the earth. On it I saw a child
sitting, great king, his face like the full moon, his eyes wide as a lotus in
flower, Bharata. Then a very great wonder came over me, lord of the earth. How
does this child lie here, when the world has been destroyed? Even thinking by my
austerity, I could not make out that child, though I know what was, what is and
what will be, lord of men. He was the colour of flax flowers, and bore the mark
of the Shrivatsa. He seemed to me the very dwelling of Lakshmi.

"Then the lotus-eyed child, splendid, bearing the Shrivatsa, said words that
were pleasant to the ear. 'I know you are worn out, my son, and long for rest.
Stay here, Markandeya, as long as you like, Bhargava. Go into my body, best of
sages, and stay there. A dwelling is made for you. I have granted you my grace.'

"When the child said this to me, Bharata, I grew weary of long life and of being
human. Then the child suddenly opened his mouth, and by the working of fate I
was drawn into his mouth, helpless. When I had entered his belly, I suddenly
saw, lord of men, the whole earth, crowded with kingdoms and cities. I saw the
Ganga, the Shatadru, the Sita, the Yamuna and the Kaushiki, the Charmanvati, the
Vetravati, the Chandrabhaga and the Sarasvati. I saw the Sindhu and the Vipasha,
the river Godavari, the Vasvokasara, the Nalini and the Narmada, Bharata. I saw
the river Tamra and the Venna, with its holy water that brings good fortune, the
Suvena and the Krishnavena. I saw the Irama and the great river, the Shona,
tiger among men, the Vishalya and the Kampuna. I saw these and the other rivers
on earth, best of men, as I went round inside the belly of that great one.

"Then I saw the ocean, where hosts of water creatures live, the mine of jewels,
slayer of enemies, the great treasure house of water. Then I saw the sky, lit by
the moon and the sun, blazing with lights as bright as fire and the sun. And I
saw the earth, king, adorned with forests. At that time, king, brahmins were
sacrificing with many sacrifices, and kshatriyas were busy pleasing all the
classes. Vaishyas were farming as is right, lord of men, and shudras were given
to serving the twice-born.

"Then, as I went on inside the belly of that great one, king, I saw the Himalaya
and the mountain Hemakuta. I saw the Nishadha, and the White Mountain veined
with silver, and I saw the mountain Gandhamadana, lord of the earth. I saw
Mandara, tiger among men, and the great mountain Nila, and I saw Meru, the
golden mountain, great king. I saw Mahendra and the Vindhya, finest of
mountains, and I saw Malaya and the mountain Pariyatra. These and all the many
other mountains there are, I saw in his belly, adorned with every jewel. I saw
lions, tigers and boars, and elephants, lord of men, and all the other creatures
on the earth, lord of the world, and I went about among them all.

"Inside his belly, tiger among men, going about in every direction, I saw Shakra
and the others, all the hosts of the gods. I saw the gandharvas and apsarases,
the yakshas and the seers, lord of the earth. I saw the hosts of daityas and
danavas and the Kaleyas, lord of men, the sons of Simhika and the other enemies
of the gods. Whatever I had seen in the world, moving and unmoving, I saw all of
it in the belly of that great one, as I wandered through this whole world,
living on fruit.

"I was inside his body for more than a hundred years, and nowhere did I see the
end of his body. Running on and on, and thinking, lord of the people, I could
not reach the end of that great one, king. Then I went to him for refuge, as is
proper, to the god, the best, the giver of boons, in mind and in deed. Then,
king, I was suddenly carried out by the force of a wind from that great one's
open mouth, best of men.

"Then I saw him, tiger among men, seated on the branch of that same banyan, lord
of the people, holding the whole world. He was in that same form of a child,
marked with the Shrivatsa, of boundless splendour. Then the child spoke to me,
as if laughing, splendid, bearing the Shrivatsa, in yellow clothes, of great
splendour. 'Well, best of sages, Markandeya, have you lived in this body of mine
and rested well? Tell me.'

"In a moment a new sight came to me again, by which I saw myself set free, my
senses restored. I bowed my head, made pure, and took hold of his two feet, well
set, copper-soled, finely made, adorned with soft red toes. I saluted them, for
I had seen the boundless power of him of boundless might. Humbly, with joined
hands, I went up to him with care and saw the god, the self of beings, the
lotus-eyed one. With joined hands I bowed and said this to him:

"'I want to know you, god, and this highest magic of yours. I entered your body
by your mouth, blessed one, and saw all the worlds together in your belly. In
your body, god, are gods, danavas and rakshasas, yakshas, gandharvas and snakes,
and the world, moving and unmoving. By your grace, god, my memory has not
failed, though I ran about quickly and without rest inside your body. I want to
know you, lotus-eyed one, blameless one. Why do you stay here as a child, having
drunk up this whole universe? Tell me. Why is the whole world in your body,
sinless one? And how long must you stay here, tamer of enemies? I want to hear
this from you, lord of the gods, lotus-eyed one, at length and as it is, for I
am a brahmin and I long to know. For this that I have seen is great and beyond
thought, lord.'

"When I had said this to him, the glorious god of gods, of great splendour, best
of speakers, comforted me and said this.

**Choices.**

* 3.186.14 *ya eṣa … janārdanaḥ*: "This Janardana here". Markandeya points to Krishna, who is present.
* 3.186.18–22: the lengths of the ages are given as the text gives them; "an age" here is the whole cycle of twelve thousand years.

* 3.186.35 *mukhebhagāḥ*: "shameless"; the literal sense is coarse and its meaning disputed.
