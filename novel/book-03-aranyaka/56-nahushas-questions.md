# Nahusha's Questions

Yudhishthira went up to his dear brother, the hero, wound in the serpent's
coils, and said, "Son of Kunti, how have you come to this trouble? And who is
this finest of serpents, as big as a mountain's flank?" The brother told his
elder brother, the Dharma King, all that had happened, from the seizing on.

"Whether you are a god, or a daitya, or a serpent," said Yudhishthira, "speak
the truth, serpent. It is Yudhishthira who asks you. What could I bring you, or
what could you learn, that would please you, snake? What food shall I give you?
How would you let him go?"

"I was King Nahusha, your forefather, sinless one," said the serpent, "famous as
the fifth from Soma, the son of Ayus, lord of men. By sacrifices and austerity,
by study of the Veda and self-control, and by courage, I won the undisputed
lordship of the three worlds. When I had that lordship, pride came over me, and
a thousand of the twice-born carried my litter. Drunk with the pride of my
lordship, I scorned the brahmins, and Agastya brought me to this state, lord of
the earth. But my wisdom has not left me to this day, Pandava, by the grace of
that same great Agastya, king. At the sixth watch of the day your younger
brother has come to me as food. I will not let him go, and I want no other. But
if you answer the questions I put to you, then afterwards I will let your
brother Vrikodara go."

"Ask as you like, serpent," said Yudhishthira, "and I will answer you, if I can,
to please you, snake. You know all that a brahmin should know here. When I have
heard you, king of serpents, I will answer you."

"Who would be a brahmin, king," said the serpent, "and what is to be known,
Yudhishthira? Tell me, for from your words we judge that your understanding is
very great."

"Truth, giving, patience, good conduct, gentleness, self-control and
compassion," said Yudhishthira. "Where these are seen, lord of snakes, that man
is called a brahmin. What is to be known, serpent, is the highest brahman,
beyond sorrow and beyond joy, where those who go grieve no more. What do you
say?"

"The four classes are the standard, Yudhishthira," said the serpent, "and truth
is, and brahman is. But among shudras too there is truth, giving and freedom
from anger, gentleness, non-violence and compassion. And as for what you say is
to be known, beyond sorrow and beyond joy, lord of men, I see no other state
that is free of those two."

"If these marks are in a shudra," said Yudhishthira, "and are not found in one
of the twice-born, then the shudra is not a shudra, and the brahmin is not a
brahmin. Where this conduct is seen, serpent, that man is called a brahmin.
Where it is not, serpent, one should call him a shudra. And as for what you
said, that there is nothing to be known, since no state exists beyond those two:
that is your view, serpent, that nothing exists without those two. But as
between cold and heat there is neither heat nor cold, so there is somewhere a
state without joy and sorrow. That is what I think, serpent, or you may think
otherwise."

"If you judge a brahmin by his conduct, king," said the serpent, "then birth is
useless, long-lived one, until the conduct is seen."

"Here among men, great serpent, wise one," said Yudhishthira, "birth is hard to
be sure of, I think, because all the classes are mixed. Men of all kinds beget
children on women of all kinds. Speech, coupling, birth and death are the same
for all men. And the seers' own authority is the formula, 'We who sacrifice.' So
those who see the truth know that conduct is the chief thing. Before the navel
cord is cut, the birth rite is performed for a male. There the Savitri is his
mother, and the teacher is called his father. Until he is born in the Veda he is
equal to a shudra in his ways. When opinion was divided like this, Manu
Svayambhuva said, 'Even when the classes have done their rites, if good conduct
is not there, the mixing is held to be great, lord of snakes.' Where the conduct
that the rites should bring is now found, great serpent, that man I have already
called a brahmin, best of serpents."

"I have heard your words, Yudhishthira," said the serpent, "and you know what is
to be known. Why should I eat your brother Vrikodara?"

"You are like this in the world," said Yudhishthira, "one who has gone to the
far shore of the Veda and its limbs. Tell me: by doing what deeds does one reach
the highest way?"

"By giving to a worthy recipient, by speaking kindly and speaking the truth,
Bharata," said the serpent, "and by devotion to non-violence, one goes to
heaven. That is what I think."

"Which of these is seen to weigh more, serpent," said Yudhishthira, "giving or
truth? And tell me which weighs more and which less, non-violence or kindness."

"Devotion to giving, truth, non-violence and kindness," said the serpent: "which
weighs more and which less is seen from how much the task in hand demands. For
truth is sometimes worth more than a certain gift, and a certain gift is worth
more than true speech, king of kings. So too, great bowman, lord of the earth,
non-violence is seen to weigh more than kind words, and then again kindness is
wanted. So it is, king, depending on what is to be done in each case. Ask
whatever else you want, and I will tell you."

"How is it, serpent," said Yudhishthira, "that one without a body goes to
heaven, and the fruit of deeds is sure? And tell me about the objects of the
senses."

"Three ways are seen, king, reached by one's own deeds," said the serpent: "the
human state, dwelling in heaven, and birth as an animal. That is the threefold
way. From the world of men, one who is not slothful goes to heaven by giving and
the rest, and by acts joined to non-violence and to the good. By the opposite
acts, king of kings, a man becomes a man again, or an animal, my son, and here
there is a distinction to be told. One full of desire and anger, of violence and
greed, falls from the human state and is born as an animal. From the animal womb
a separate being is born into the human state, and even godhood is seen to come
to cows and horses and the like. So the creature, bound by what it has to do,
moves through all these ways. But the wise one sets the self in the eternal and
the great, king. Born again and again, the self in its body is strong and enjoys
the fruit it seeks, my son. Freed from it, it comes to know the mark of all that
is born."

"Tell me truly, serpent," said Yudhishthira, "the steady ground of sound and
touch, of form, taste and smell. Why do you not grasp all the objects at once,
wise one? Tell me this much, best of serpents, and all will have been told."

"The substance that is the self, long-lived one," said the serpent, "dwelling in
a body and acting through the instruments, enjoys the objects as is ordained.
Knowledge, understanding and mind, bull of the Bharatas: learn from me that
these are its instruments for enjoying the objects. By the mind, my son, the
self of beings goes out from the field and, dwelling in the seats of the
objects, goes round these objects one by one. And here, tiger among men, the
creature's mind is set to one thing, so it cannot grasp them all at once. That
self, tiger among men, dwelling between the brows, sends out the understanding,
high and low, to the many things. After the understanding, the wise see feeling.
This, tiger among kings, is the way the knower of the field is formed."

"Tell me the highest mark of the mind and of the understanding," said
Yudhishthira. "This is held to be the highest task of those who know the self."

"Understanding follows the self, my son, and comes about when something arises,"
said the serpent. "This awareness rests on it, and its work lies in seeking.
Understanding has no qualities, but the mind has qualities. Understanding arises
for a task, but the mind is already there. That is the difference between the
mind and the understanding, my son, as I have told it. You too are awake in
this. What do you think?"

"Ah, best of the wise," said Yudhishthira, "this understanding of yours is fine.
You know what is to be known. Why do you question me? How did delusion come over
you, who know all, who lived in heaven, whose deeds were so wonderful? That is
my great doubt."

"Even if a man is very wise and brave," said the serpent, "wealth bewilders him.
Anyone who lives in happiness loses his sense. That is what I think. So I,
bewildered by lordship and drunk with pride, fell, Yudhishthira, and now I am
awake again, and I wake you. You have done what I needed, great king, scorcher
of enemies. By talking with you, good man, my curse, very hard to bear, has
ended.

"Long ago I went about in heaven in a divine aerial chariot, drunk with pride,
and I thought of no one else. Brahmin seers, gods, gandharvas, yakshas,
rakshasas and kinnaras, all who live in the three worlds, paid me tribute.
Whatever creature I looked at with my eyes, lord of the earth, I took away its
splendour at once. That was the power of my sight. A thousand brahmin seers
carried my litter, and that wrong made me fall from my glory, king. As he was
carrying me, I touched the sage Agastya with my foot. Then, unseen, he said to
me in anger, 'Fall! Be a serpent!' Then I fell from the top of that aerial
chariot, my ornaments falling, and as I fell I knew myself turned into a snake,
head downwards. I begged the brahmin, 'Let there be an end to the curse. Blessed
one, forgive one who acted out of ignorance.' Then, full of pity, he said to me
as I fell, 'Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, will free you from the curse. When
the fruit of your terrible pride and strength has worn away, great king, you
will win the fruit of your merit.'

"Then I was filled with wonder, seeing the strength of his austerity. That is
why I questioned you about brahman and about being a brahmin. Truth,
self-control, austerity, yoga, non-violence and constant giving: these are what
always bring men to their goal, king, not birth and not family. Here is your
brother, strong-armed Bhima, set free and unharmed. May it go well with you,
great king. I will go back to heaven."

When he had said this, King Nahusha left the serpent's body, took on a divine
form and went to heaven. Righteous Yudhishthira, glorious, came back to the
hermitage with his brother Bhima and with Dhaumya. Then the Dharma King
Yudhishthira told all the brahmins who had gathered everything just as it had
happened. When they heard it, all the brahmins and his three brothers were
deeply ashamed, king, and so was glorious Draupadi. All those best of brahmins,
wanting the good of the Pandavas, rebuked Bhima for his rashness and said,
"Never do this again." And the Pandavas, seeing mighty Bhima freed from danger,
were glad, and took their pleasure joyfully.

**Divergences.**

* **The first questions on dharma.** An appendix passage (@19, after 3.177.14) adds an exchange on dharma in general. Not narrated.
* **More marks of a brahmin.** A rejected passage (\*885) adds that a brahmin always seeks others' virtues, overlooks their faults and pities the wretched. Not narrated.
* **The chariot from heaven.** A rejected line (\*890) has an aerial chariot come down to take Nahusha. In the CE he simply leaves the serpent's body and goes to heaven (3.178.45).
