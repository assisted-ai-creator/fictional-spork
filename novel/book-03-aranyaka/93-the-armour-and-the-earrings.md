# The Armour and the Earrings

Then a child came to be in Pritha, lord of the earth, like the lord of the stars
in the sky in the bright half of the month. Afraid of her kin, the girl hid the
child and carried it, and no one knew of it. No other woman knew of her except
her nurse's daughter, who was clever at keeping watch, since she lived in the
maidens' quarters.

In time the lovely woman gave birth to a son, with the splendour of an immortal,
and by the god's grace she stayed a maiden. He was born with his armour on, and
with bright golden earrings, lion-eyed, with shoulders like a bull's, just like
his father. As soon as the child was born, the lovely woman took counsel with
her nurse and laid him in a basket, well lined all round. It was sealed with
wax, comfortable, smooth and with a good lid, and weeping, she set it afloat on
the river Ashva.

She knew that a maiden should not bear a child, and yet out of love for her son,
king of kings, she lamented pitifully. Hear the words that Kunti spoke, weeping,
as she let the basket go into the waters of the Ashva.

"Be safe, my son, from the beings of the air, from those of the earth and from
the divine ones, and from those that move in the water. May your roads be kind,
and may none stand in your way. And may those you meet be without malice, my
son. May King Varuna, lord of the waters, protect you in the water, and the
all-reaching wind in the air. May your father protect you everywhere, the
burning one, best of those that burn, by whom you were given to me, my son, in a
divine way, they say. May the Adityas, the Vasus, the Rudras, the Sadhyas and
the Vishve Devas, the Maruts with Indra, and the directions with the lords of
the directions, may all the gods guard you, on level ground and rough. I shall
know you even in a foreign land by your armour.

"Blessed is your father, my son, the shining god, the Sun, who will see you with
his divine eye as you go down the river. Blessed is the woman who will take you
as her son, whose breast you will drink from when you are thirsty, child of a
god. What dream has she seen, the woman who will take you as her son? You have
the splendour of the sun, your divine armour and your divine earrings, your long
wide lotus eyes, your palms bright and red as lotuses, your fine forehead and
fine hair. Blessed are those who will see you crawling on the ground, my son,
covered in dust, speaking sweet words no one can make out. Blessed are those who
will see you again, my son, with the face of youth, like a maned lion born in
the Himalayan forest."

When Pritha had lamented pitifully in many ways like this, king, she let the
basket go on the waters of the Ashva. Weeping, stricken with grief for her son,
longing to see him, the lotus-eyed Pritha let the basket go at midnight with her
nurse. Afraid that her father would find out, she went back into the royal
palace, sick with grief.

The basket went from the Ashva to the river Charmanvati, from the Charmanvati to
the Yamuna, and from there to the Ganga. From the Ganga it came to the city of
Champa, in the land of the sutas. The child in the basket was carried along by
the waves. That divine armour with the earrings, born of the amrita, kept the
child alive, and so did fate, ordained by the Maker.

---

At that very time a suta named Adhiratha, a friend of Dhritarashtra, went to the
Ganga with his wife. His wife, king, was named Radha, greatly blessed, without
equal on earth in beauty, but she had found no son, and she had made great
efforts to have a child. By chance she saw the basket floating, with protective
amulets bound on it and anointed with sweet ointments, brought close to the bank
by the waves of the Ganga. Curious, the lovely woman had it brought in, and told
Adhiratha the suta.

He lifted the basket out, set it on the ground away from the water, and opened
it with tools. He saw a little boy inside, like the young sun, wearing golden
armour, his shining face adorned with polished earrings. The suta and his wife,
their eyes wide with wonder, took the boy on their laps, and he said to his
wife, "This is a very great wonder, timid one, the greatest I have seen since I
was born. I think this child of a god has come to us. Surely the gods have given
this son to me, who had none." When he had said this, lord of the earth, he gave
the son to Radha.

Radha took him as the rites require, the divine-looking son, like the heart of a
lotus, a child of the gods wrapped in splendour. She nursed him as the rites
require, and the mighty one grew. From then on other sons were born to them of
their own bodies. When they saw the boy wearing armour of wealth and golden
earrings, the twice-born named him Vasushena.

So the one of boundless valour came to be the son of a suta, known as Vasushena
and as Vrisha, the lord. The suta's eldest son grew up mighty among the Angas.
Pritha knew through a spy of the one with the divine armour. When the suta
Adhiratha saw that his son had grown up, he sent him in time to the city named
for the elephant. There he became a pupil of Drona for the art of the bow, and
the mighty one made friends with Duryodhana. From Drona, Kripa and Rama he won
the four kinds of weapons and became famous in the world, counted among the
greatest archers. Allied with the son of Dhritarashtra, set against the Parthas,
he always longed to fight the great Phalguna. For there was always rivalry
between him and Arjuna, lord of the people, and between Arjuna and Karna, from
the moment they saw each other.

When he saw Karna with his earrings and his armour, Yudhishthira thought him
unkillable in battle, and was troubled. At noon, king of kings, when Karna stood
in the water with joined palms praising the shining sun, the maker of day,
brahmins came to him there for wealth. At that time there was nothing he would
not give to the twice-born. Indra, having become a brahmin, came to him and
said, "Give me alms." "Welcome," Radheya answered him.

---

When the bull saw the king of the gods come disguised as a brahmin, he said,
"Welcome," but did not know his mind. "Women with gold at their necks, or
villages with many herds of cattle: what shall I give you?" the son of Adhiratha
said to the brahmin.

"Women with gold at their necks, or anything else that adds to joy, I do not
want given to me," said the brahmin. "Give that to those who want it. This
armour you were born with, sinless one, and your earrings: cut them off and give
them to me, if you are true to your vow. I want this, given by you quickly,
scorcher of enemies. I think this the highest gain of all gains."

"Land, women, cattle, a living for many years," said Karna, "I shall give you,
brahmin, but not my armour and not my earrings."

Though Karna begged him with many words like this, best of the Bharatas, the
brahmin asked for no other boon. Though Karna soothed him as best he could and
honoured him as the rules require, that best of brahmins wanted no other boon.
When the best of brahmins would choose no other boon, Radheya, as if laughing,
said to him again, "My armour was born with me, brahmin, and my earrings came
from the amrita. Because of them I cannot be killed in the worlds. So I do not
give them. Take from me a great kingdom on earth, safe and cleared of thorns,
good bull among brahmins. Stripped of my earrings and of the armour I was born
with, I shall be open to my enemies, best of brahmins."

When the blessed chastiser of Paka would choose no other boon, Karna laughed and
said to him again, "I knew you beforehand, god of gods, lord. But it is not
right for me to give you a boon for nothing, Shakra. For you yourself are the
lord of the gods. You should give me a boon. You are the lord of all other
creatures too, maker of creatures. If I give you my earrings and my armour, god,
I shall come to be killed, and you will be laughed at, Shakra. So make an
exchange, and take my earrings and my fine armour, Shakra, as you wish. I would
not give them otherwise."

"The Sun knew I was coming to you before I came," said Shakra, "and so he told
you all of it. It is just so, no doubt of it. Let it be as you wish, my son
Karna. Choose whatever you want, except my thunderbolt."

Then Karna, delighted, came up to Vasava, and, his heart full, chose the
unfailing spear. "In return for my armour and earrings, give me your spear,
Vasava, the unfailing one that kills hosts of enemies at the head of the army."

Then Vasava thought in his mind for a moment about the spear, lord of the earth,
and said to Karna, "Give me your earrings and the armour born of your body, and
take the spear, Karna, on this condition. Loosed from my hand, the unfailing one
kills enemies in hundreds, and comes back to my hand as I strike down the
daityas. When it reaches your hand, son of the suta, it will kill one powerful
enemy, roaring and blazing, and come back to me."

"I want to kill only one enemy in the great battle," said Karna, "roaring and
blazing, from whom my fear might come."

"You will kill one enemy in battle, roaring and strong," said Indra. "But the
one you seek is protected by a great one: the one the knowers of the Veda call
the Boar, unconquered Hari, Narayana beyond thought. He is protected by that
Krishna."

"Even so, lord," said Karna, "let me have the unfailing, excellent spear for the
killing of one hero, by which I may kill one who blazes. I shall cut off my
earrings and armour and give them to you. But when my limbs are cut, let me not
become loathsome."

"You will not become loathsome in any way, Karna," said Indra, "and there will
be no wound on your limbs, since you do not want to be false. You will be again
just like your father in colour and splendour, Karna, best of speakers. But if,
while other weapons are there and you are in no danger, you loose the unfailing
one carelessly, it will fall on you."

"I shall loose this spear of Vasava's only when I am in the greatest danger,"
said Karna, "as you tell me, Shakra. I tell you this truly."

Then he took the blazing spear, lord of the people, took a sharp blade, and cut
at all his limbs. Gods, men and danavas, and all the hosts of siddhas, cried out
when they saw Karna cutting himself like this, for no change of pain came over
him. Then heavenly drums sounded, and a heavenly shower of flowers fell from
above, when they saw the hero among men, Karna, his limbs cut with the blade,
smiling again and again. Then he cut the divine armour from his body and gave
it, still wet, to Vasava. And he cut off the earrings in the same way and gave
them. By that deed Karna came to be called Vaikartana, "the one who cut".

Then Shakra laughed, having tricked Karna and joined him to glory in the world,
and thought the Pandavas' work was done. After that he flew up to heaven. When
they heard that Karna had been robbed, all the sons of Dhritarashtra were
downcast, as if their pride had been broken. When they heard the suta's son had
been brought to that state, the Parthas in the forest were glad.

"Where were the heroic Pandavas then?" said Janamejaya. "From whom did they hear
this welcome news? And what did they do when the twelfth year was over? Tell me
all of it, lord."

When they had won back Krishna and driven off the Saindhava, and had heard from
Markandeya in full the ancient stories of the divine seers, they left the
Kamyaka hermitage with the brahmins. With their chariots and followers, with all
their cooks and stewards, the heroes among men went back to holy Dvaitavana,
having come through the whole of their hard life in the forest.

**Divergences.**

* **Karna's name.** A rejected passage (\*1350) has the child named Karna, "ear", for his earrings. In the CE the brahmins name him Vasushena, "armour of wealth" (3.293.12), and he is also called Vrisha (3.293.13). The CE explains the name Vaikartana from the cutting of his armour (3.294.38). See L-27.
* **Radha's milk.** A rejected line (\*1349) has milk come to Radha's breasts by fate. Not narrated.
* **Karna's teachers.** In the CE he learns from Drona, Kripa and Rama (3.293.17). His training under Rama Jamadagnya and Rama's curse are told later (12.2–3; L-53).
* **The basket.** A rejected passage (\*1346) describes craftsmen making the basket with floats and skins. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1347–\*1348, \*1351–\*1355) add small details, including a statement that Karna was the Sun's secret. Not narrated.
