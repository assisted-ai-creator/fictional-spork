# Duryodhana's Shame

"Duryodhana was wicked," said Janamejaya. "He was proud, and always boasting,
arrogant and vain, and he always despised the Pandavas for their courage and
generosity. He had been beaten and bound by his enemies, and freed afterwards by
the great Pandavas in battle. It seems to me it must have been hard for him to
enter Hastinapura. Tell me in full, Vaishampayana, how he entered, full of
shame, his mind troubled with sorrow."

Suyodhana, son of Dhritarashtra, sent off by the Dharma King, crept away, his
face turned down in shame, sinking in misery. The king set out for his own city,
followed by his fourfold army, brooding on his defeat with a mind struck down by
sorrow. On the way he unyoked the carriages in a place with good grass and
water, and settled as he wished on a lovely and pleasant piece of ground. He had
the elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers set in their places.

The king was sitting on a couch that shone like fire, like the moon swallowed by
Rahu at the end of night. Then Karna came up to Duryodhana and said, "By good
fortune you are alive, son of Gandhari. By good fortune we have met again. By
good fortune you have beaten the gandharvas, who can take any form they please.
By good fortune I see all your brothers here, joy of the Kurus, great chariot
fighters, eager for victory, free of the battle, their enemies beaten.

"But I was set on by all the gandharvas while you looked on, and I could not
hold my own army together as it broke apart. My body was wounded with arrows, I
was hard pressed, and I ran. But I think it a great wonder, Bharata, that I see
you here, safe and unhurt, freed from that battle that was more than human, with
your wives, your wealth and your mounts. There is no man in this world who could
do what you have done in battle with your brothers, great king."

At Karna's words, King Duryodhana hung his head and spoke in a voice choked with
tears.

---

"You do not know, Radheya," said Duryodhana, "so I do not resent your words. You
think I beat the enemy, the gandharvas, by my own might. My brothers and I
fought the gandharvas a long time, strong-armed one, and there were losses on
both sides. But when the heroes went up into the sky and fought with their
greater magic, the fight with the sky-goers was no longer equal. We were beaten
in the battle and bound, with our servants, counsellors and sons, our wives, our
wealth and our mounts. They carried us off, deep in misery, high along the road
of the sky.

"Then some of our soldiers and counsellors went in distress to the great chariot
fighters, the Pandavas, who give shelter. 'Here is King Duryodhana, son of
Dhritarashtra, with his younger brothers, his counsellors and his wives, being
carried off by the gandharvas who live in the sky. Free the king and his wives,
bless you. Let no hand be laid on the Kuru women.'

"When they said this, the eldest son of Pandu, whose self is dharma, won over
all his brothers and ordered them to free us. Then the Pandavas, bulls among
men, came to that place. Though they had the power, the great chariot fighters
asked first with gentle words. When the gandharvas would not let us go even for
gentle words, Arjuna and Bhima and the twins, fierce in their strength, loosed
rain after rain of arrows at the gandharvas. Then all the sky-goers left the
battle and went off towards heaven, gladly dragging us, wretched, with them.
Then I saw a net of arrows closing in all round, and Dhananjaya using weapons
beyond men. When he saw the directions shut off by the Pandava's sharp arrows,
Dhananjaya's friend showed himself. Chitrasena, the scorcher of enemies,
embraced the Pandava and asked after his welfare, and they asked after his. When
they had met like this and taken off their armour, the heroic gandharvas and the
Pandavas became one, and Chitrasena and Dhananjaya honoured each other.

"Chitrasena met Arjuna, and Arjuna, the slayer of enemy heroes, laughed and said
to him these manly words. 'Hero, best of gandharvas, you must set our brothers
free. They do not deserve to be shamed while the sons of Pandu are alive.' When
the great Pandava said this, Karna, the gandharva told him what we had been
plotting when we set out: that we would see the Pandavas and their wives, fallen
from happiness. While the gandharva was saying these words, I wished for a hole
in the earth to go into, I was so ashamed.

"Then the gandharvas and the Pandavas went to Yudhishthira, and they told him of
our wicked plan, and that we were bound. Wretched, bound, in the power of my
enemies, before the eyes of the women, I was handed over to Yudhishthira. What
sorrow could be greater than this? Those I always spurned, to whom I was always
an enemy, have freed me, fool that I am, and given me my life. If I had met my
death in that great battle, hero, it would have been better for me than a life
like this. I would have had fame on earth for being killed by a gandharva, and I
would have won the holy, unfailing worlds in great Indra's house.

"Hear what I have decided today, bulls among men. I shall sit here and fast to
the death. You go home. Let all my brothers go to the city today. Let Karna and
all my other friends and kin go to the city today, with Duhshasana at their
head. For I will not go back to the city, spurned by my enemies. I used to take
away my enemies' pride and give my friends honour. Now I have become a grief to
my friends and a joy to my enemies. When I reach the city named for the
elephant, what shall I say to the king? Bhishma, Drona, Kripa, Drona's son,
Vidura and Sanjaya, Bahlika and Somadatta, and the others honoured by the old,
the brahmins, the heads of the guilds and those who take no side: what will they
say to me, and what shall I answer them? I stood on my enemies' heads and set my
foot on their chests. Now I have fallen by my own fault. How shall I speak to
them?

"The ill-bred who win fortune, learning and lordship do not keep them long, as I
have not, drunk with pride. Alas, what a painful, wicked thing I have done, fool
that I am, in my folly, by which I have come into this danger! So I shall sit
and fast to the death, for I cannot live. What man with a mind would live,
lifted out of trouble by his enemies, laughed at by his enemies, proud and yet
stripped of manhood, looked on with contempt by the Pandavas, rich in courage?"

---

Lost in these thoughts, he said to Duhshasana, "Duhshasana, hear what I say,
Bharata. Accept the consecration I give you, and be king. Rule the rich earth,
guarded by Karna and the son of Subala. Protect your brothers without fear, as
the slayer of Vritra protects the Maruts. Let your kin live off you, as the gods
live off Shatakratu. Always keep up the brahmins' livelihood, and never be
careless. Always be a refuge for your kin and your friends. Look after your
kinsmen, as Vishnu looks after the hosts of the gods. You must protect your
elders. Go and protect the earth, delighting all your friends and threatening
your enemies." And he embraced him about the neck and said, "Go."

When he heard these words, Duhshasana, wretched, his throat full of tears, deep
in misery, joined his palms and bowed. In a choking voice he said to his elder
brother, "Be gracious," and fell to the ground with a burning heart. In his
misery he shed tears on his brother's feet, and the tiger among men said, "This
will not be. The earth with her mountains would split, and the sky would break
into pieces. The sun would give up its light, and the moon its cool rays. The
wind would give up its speed, the Himalaya would move, the water in the oceans
would dry up, and fire would give up its heat, before I would rule the earth
without you, king." Again and again he said, "Be gracious," and then he said
this: "You alone will be king in our family for a hundred years." When he had
said this, king of kings, he wept aloud, holding the feet of his elder brother,
which deserve honour, Bharata.

When he saw Duhshasana and Suyodhana in such misery, Karna came up to them,
troubled himself, and said, "Why are you two Kauravyas giving way like common
men, out of childishness? No one's sorrow is turned back by his grieving. When
sorrow does not remove the misfortune of the one who grieves, what power do you
two see in grief? Take hold of yourselves. If you grieve, you will only please
your enemies.

"The Pandavas did only their duty in freeing you, king. Those who live in a
king's lands must always do what pleases the king. For it is under your
protection that they live there free of care. You should not take offence like a
common man at what has happened. Your brothers are downcast because you have set
yourself to fast to death. Get up, go, bless you, and comfort your brothers.

"King, today I see how light your spirit is. What is so strange about the
Pandavas freeing you, hero, when you had just fallen into the enemy's power,
crusher of enemies? Those who live by soldiering, Kauravya, and those who live
in the kingdom, known or unknown, must do what pleases the king. It often
happens that leading men stir up an enemy army, are captured in battle, and are
freed by their own soldiers. The men who live by soldiering in a king's lands
must band together and strive as they should for the king's cause. If the
Pandavas, who live in your lands, happened to free you today, king, what is
there to lament in that?

"And it was not good, best of kings, that the Pandavas did not follow behind you
as you set out with your army. They are brave and strong and never run from
battle, and they had already become your servants in the hall. You still enjoy
the Pandavas' treasures. Look at the Pandavas, who kept their courage: they did
not sit down to fast to death. Get up, king, bless you. You should not brood.
The dwellers in a king's lands must do what pleases the king. What is there to
lament in that?

"If you will not do as I say, king of kings, I shall stay here, serving your
feet, crusher of enemies. I cannot live without you, bull among men. But if you
fast to death, king, you will be the laughing-stock of kings."

At Karna's words, King Duryodhana would not bring himself to get up. He had made
up his mind on heaven.
