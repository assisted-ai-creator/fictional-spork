# The Hunter of Mithila

"The brahmin thought over all the wonderful things the woman had said," said
Markandeya, "and he blamed himself and looked like a man who had done wrong.
Thinking of the subtle way of dharma, he said, 'I must have faith. I will go to
Mithila. A hunter lives there, they say, who has made himself perfect and knows
dharma. I will go to him today to ask about dharma, rich in austerity as he is.'

"Having thought this, trusting the woman's words because of the crane and her
fine words full of dharma, he set out for Mithila, full of curiosity. Passing
forests, villages and towns, he came to Mithila, well guarded by Janaka. It was
full of the bridges of dharma, rich in sacrifices and festivals, fine, with gate
towers and turrets, bright with houses and walls. He entered the lovely city,
full of mansions and of many wares, with well laid out highways, full of horses,
chariots, elephants and carriages, crowded with glad and well-fed people, always
busy with festivals. The brahmin, passing through, saw its many doings, and he
asked for the righteous hunter, and the brahmins told him where he was.

"Going there, he saw him standing in the middle of the slaughterhouse, the
ascetic, selling venison and buffalo meat. Because of the crowd of buyers, the
brahmin stood to one side. Knowing that a brahmin had come, the hunter suddenly
got up in a flurry and came to where the brahmin stood apart, on a seat.

"'I salute you, blessed one,' said the hunter. 'Welcome, best of the twice-born.
I am the hunter, bless you. What can I do for you? Command me. The woman
faithful to one husband told you, "Go to Mithila." I know all of it, and why you
have come here.'

"When the brahmin heard his words he was overjoyed, and thought, 'This is a
second wonder.'

"'This is no place for you,' the hunter said to the brahmin. 'Let us go to my
house, blessed one, if it pleases you, sinless one.' The brahmin gladly said,
'Very well.' Putting the brahmin in front of him, he went towards his house. The
best of the twice-born entered the fine house and was honoured with a seat, and
took water for his feet and to sip.

"Then, seated at ease, he said to the hunter, 'This work does not seem fit for
you. I am deeply grieved, my son, at your terrible work.'

"'This work is my family's,' said the hunter. 'It is my father's and
grandfather's. Since I keep to my own dharma, brahmin, do not be distressed. I
keep to my own work, laid down long ago by the Creator, and I serve my two old
elders with care, best of the twice-born. I speak the truth, I do not envy, and
I give as I can. I live on what is left over from the gods, guests and servants.
I despise nothing, and I do not revile the stronger. What was done before
follows the doer, best of the twice-born.

"'Farming, keeping cattle and trade are the livelihood of the world here. The
rule of the rod and the three Vedas: by these the worlds exist. Service is laid
down for the shudra, farming for the vaishya, battle for the kshatriya, and for
the brahmin always celibacy, austerity, mantras and truth. The king rules by
dharma the people given to their own work, and he sets those who do work not
theirs to their own work. Kings must always be feared, for they are the lords of
the people. They kill one who does work not his, as hunters kill a deer with
arrows.

"'Here in Janaka's kingdom, brahmin seer, there is no one who does work not his.
All four classes keep to their own work, best of the twice-born. This King
Janaka puts into punishment even his own son, if he is wicked and deserves
punishment, and he does not harm the righteous. With his spies well placed, the
king sees everything by dharma. Fortune, the kingdom and the rod belong to
kshatriyas, best of the twice-born. For kings seek greater fortune through their
own dharma. The king is the protector of all the classes.

"'I do not kill boars and buffalo myself, brahmin seer. Others kill them, and I
only sell. I do not eat meat. I go to my wife only in her season. I am always
fasting, and I eat only at night, brahmin. Even a man without good conduct
becomes a man of good conduct, and even one given to killing creatures becomes
righteous again. Through the wrongdoing of kings great dharma is thrown into
confusion, adharma grows, and the people are confused too. The misshapen, dwarfs
and hunchbacks, the big-headed, eunuchs, the blind and the deaf, and those with
hanging breasts are born. Through the adharma of kings the people always fare
badly. This King Janaka sees everything by dharma, always favouring all his
people, who keep to their own dharma.

"'Those who praise me and those who blame me, I please them all by work well
done. Those who live by their own dharma, skilled and hard-working, depend on no
one, and kings share their enjoyment with them. Giving food always as one can,
patience, constancy in dharma, honouring others as they deserve and kindness to
all creatures: these virtues stay in a man only through giving things up, and
not otherwise. One should shun lies and do good unasked. One should not give up
dharma out of desire, anger or hatred. One should not rejoice too much at what
is pleasant or burn at what is unpleasant. One should not be bewildered in hard
times for wealth, or give up dharma. If a deed is of one kind, one should not do
another. One should set oneself to whatever good one thinks of. One should not
do evil in return for evil. One should always be good. The evil man who wants to
do evil is struck down by himself.

"'This is the work of the wicked and the crooked, who, thinking there is no
dharma, laugh at the pure. Without faith in dharma, they perish, without doubt.
The evil man is always puffed up like a great waterskin. The talk of proud fools
is empty. The inner self shows itself, as the sun shows forms by day. A fool
does not shine in the world by praising himself alone, but even without polish a
learned man shines here. Yet a man of virtue who speaks no ill of others and
tells no praise of himself is not seen to be famous on earth.

"'One who is pained by a wrong deed is freed from the sin. Saying, "I will not
do this again," he is freed from the second. By whatever deed it may be, he is
freed from sin here, best of the twice-born. This is the teaching seen in the
dharmas, brahmin. The man who was not righteous before, but is righteous later,
destroys the sins he did long ago without knowing. Dharma drives away, brahmin,
the evil men do here through carelessness. When a man has done evil, he should
think, "I am not that man," and wish only to do good, with faith and without
envy. There is the man who lays bare the faults of the good like the holes in a
garment. But if a man who has done evil takes to good, he is freed from all his
sins, as the moon from great clouds. As the rising sun drives away all darkness,
so one who stands in the good is freed from all sins.

"'Know that greed is the seat of sins, best of the twice-born. Greedy men of
little learning resolve on sin. Adharmas wear the form of dharma, like wells
covered with grass. Self-control, purifications, talk that clings to dharma: all
of that is found in them. The conduct of good men is very hard to find.'

"Then the wise brahmin asked the righteous hunter, best of men, 'How may I know
the conduct of good men? Tell me this truly, wise hunter.'

"'Sacrifice, giving, austerity, the Vedas and truth, best of the twice-born,'
said the hunter, 'these five purifications are always in the conduct of good
men. Those who have brought desire and anger under control, and deceit, greed
and crookedness, who are content with the thought, "This is dharma": they are
the good, approved by the good. There is no bad conduct in those given to
sacrifice and study of the Veda, and the keeping of good conduct is the second
mark of the good. Service of one's elders, truth, freedom from anger and giving:
these four, brahmin, are always in the conduct of good men. The contentment a
man wins when he sets his mind on the conduct of good men and makes it firm in
every way cannot be won otherwise.

"'The secret of the Veda is truth. The secret of truth is self-control. The
secret of self-control is giving up. This is always in the conduct of good men.
Those men who, their understanding clouded, find fault with dharma walk a wrong
road, and even those who follow them are hurt. But the good are well restrained,
devoted to scripture and to giving up. They have climbed onto the road of
dharma, devoted to truth and dharma. Joined to the conduct of good men, they
hold to the highest understanding, keeping to their teacher's teaching, seeing
dharma and wealth by their steadiness. Leave those who deny the other world, who
break the bounds, the cruel, who stand in evil thought. Rely on knowledge and
wait on the righteous. Make a boat of steadiness and cross the river whose water
is the five senses, full of the crocodiles of desire and greed, and cross the
hard places of birth. Dharma gathered little by little, great, made of
understanding and yoga, would be fine in the conduct of good men, like dye on a
white cloth.

"'Non-violence and speaking the truth are the highest good for all creatures.
Non-violence is the highest dharma, and it rests on truth. Taking their stand on
truth, all works go forward. Truth, practised in the conduct of good men, weighs
most. Good conduct is the dharma of the good, and the good are marked by their
conduct. Each creature gets its own nature, as its nature is. The evil self
without self-control gets the faults of anger, desire and the rest. An
undertaking joined to what is right is called dharma. Lack of good conduct is
adharma. That is the teaching of the good. Those who do not grow angry, who do
not envy, who are free of egotism and jealousy, straight and calm: they are good
in conduct. Those grown old in the three Vedas, pure, of good conduct and high
mind, serving their elders and self-controlled: they are good in conduct. Their
spirit is never low, their conduct and work are hard, they are honoured by their
own deeds, and all harshness in them is destroyed. Seeing by dharma that dharma,
the wonderful ancient conduct of the good, eternal and sure, the wise go to
heaven. Those who believe in the other world, free of pride, who honour the
twice-born, full of learning and good conduct: those good men go to heaven.

"'The highest dharma is that told in the Veda. Another is in the books of
dharma. And there is what the good have practised. That is the threefold mark of
dharma. Mastery of learning, bathing at holy fords, patience, truth,
straightness and purity show the conduct of good men. Kind to all creatures,
always devoted to non-violence, the good never speak harshly, and they are dear
to the twice-born. Those who know the ripening of the fruit that gathers from
good and bad deeds are the good, approved by the good. Just, full of virtue,
seeking the good of all the world, the good win heaven. They are pure and set on
the good road. Givers, sharers, kind to the wretched, merciful to all creatures:
they are the good, approved by the good. Honoured by all, rich in learning,
ascetics, always giving, they win happy worlds and fortune here. Collected in
mind, when they meet good men they give beyond their means, even if it pinches
their wives and servants. Watching the way of the world, and dharma, and their
own good, the good who live like this thrive for endless years.

"'Non-violence, speaking the truth, gentleness and straightness, bearing no
malice, no pride beyond measure, modesty, patience, self-control and calm: these
are in the good. Honoured by the world, they are wise and steady, kind to
creatures, free of desire and hatred. They say the unsurpassed conduct of the
good has three steps only: do no harm, give, and always speak the truth. The
good, merciful everywhere, feeling compassion, go gladly by the highest road of
dharma here, great-spirited, good in conduct, their dharma well settled. Freedom
from envy, patience, peace, contentment, kind speech, giving up desire and
anger: this is practising the conduct of good men. Those who by their deeds are
full of learning always practise the conduct of good men, the highest road of
the good, tireless in the dharmas. Having climbed the palace of wisdom, best of
the twice-born, they look out on great crowds of bewildered people, on the many
ways of the world, the most holy and the evil, best of the twice-born. I have
told you all this as far as I know it and as I have heard it, putting first the
virtues of the conduct of good men, bull among brahmins.'

---

"Then the righteous hunter said to the brahmin, Yudhishthira, 'This work I do is
terrible, no doubt of it. But fate is strong, brahmin, and what was done before
is hard to escape. This fault of my work comes from an evil done before. I
strive to undo that fault, brahmin. When a thing has been ordained before by
fate, the killer is only the instrument. We are only the instruments of this
work, best of the twice-born. And of those killed whose meat we sell, brahmin,
there would be dharma too, through their being eaten, and through the honour
paid with them to the gods, guests, servants and Fathers. Plants, creepers,
cattle, beasts and birds are the food of the world. So the scripture says too.

"'King Shibi Aushinara, the patient one, won heaven, so hard to win, by giving
his own flesh, best of the twice-born. In King Rantideva's kitchen long ago,
brahmin, two thousand animals were killed every day. Because Rantideva always
gave food with meat, the king won matchless fame, best of the twice-born. At the
four-monthly rites animals are always killed. "The fires love meat": that too
the scripture says. In sacrifices animals are always killed by the twice-born,
brahmin, and, they say, made holy by the mantras, they too have gone to heaven.
If the fires had not loved meat long ago, brahmin, meat would be no one's food,
best of the twice-born. Here too the sages have laid down a rule for eating
meat. Whoever always eats only after giving to the gods and the Fathers, as the
rules say and with faith, is not defiled by eating it. So he becomes one who
does not eat meat, as the scripture says. A brahmin who goes to his wife in her
season is a celibate. Here too the rule is told, after deciding what is true and
what is false. Long ago King Saudasa, overcome by a curse, ate men, brahmin.
What do you think of that?

"'I do not give it up, best of the twice-born, holding it to be my own dharma.
Knowing it was done before, I live by this work. One who gives up his own work
is seen to do adharma here, brahmin, and one devoted to his own work does
dharma: that is settled. The work laid down before does not let go of the
embodied one. The Creator has seen this rule many times in deciding about deeds.
But one who lives by cruel work should consider, wise one: how can I do good
work, how can I be freed from ruin? There can be many ways of settling about
that terrible work. I am always devoted to giving, to speaking the truth, to
serving my elders, to honouring the twice-born, and to dharma. I have turned
away from speaking too much and from too much pride, best of the twice-born.

"'People think farming is good, but there is great violence in it. Men ploughing
with ploughs kill many creatures that live in the ground, and many other living
things. What do you think of that? The seeds of grain they speak of, rice and
the rest, best of the twice-born, are all living. What do you think of that? Men
attack beasts and kill them and eat them, and they cut down trees and plants,
brahmin. For there are many living things in trees and fruits, brahmin, and many
in water too. What do you think of that? This whole world is filled with
creatures that live on creatures, brahmin. Fish swallow fish. What do you think
of that? Creatures live on creatures in many ways, best of the twice-born, and
living things eat one another. What do you think of that? As they walk about,
brahmin, men kill with their feet many living things that dwell in the earth.
What do you think of that? Sitting and lying down, even men of knowledge and
understanding kill living things in many ways. What do you think of that? This
whole sky and earth are full of living things, and men harm them without
knowing. What do you think of that?

"'"Non-violence," men said long ago in their wonder. But who in this world does
not harm living things, best of the twice-born? Thinking it over well, there is
no one here who is non-violent. Even ascetics devoted to non-violence do harm.
By effort it may be less. Men born in good families, of great virtues, noble to
look at, do most terrible deeds and feel no shame. Friends do not see their
friends aright, nor others, nor enemies their enemies, nor those who act
rightly. Kin do not rejoice in the prosperity of their kin. Fools who think
themselves wise revile their elders. Much in the world is seen turned upside
down, best of the twice-born, adharma joined with dharma. What do you think of
that? Much can be said about dharma and adharma in deeds. But the man devoted to
his own work wins great fame.'"
