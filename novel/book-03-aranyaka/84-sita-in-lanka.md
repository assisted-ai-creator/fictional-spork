# Sita in Lanka

"Ravana, struck by the arrows of love, saw her sitting on a stone slab, waited
on by rakshasis," said Markandeya. "She was pained by grief for her husband,
wretched, in dirty clothes, with only her jewels left for ornament, weeping,
faithful to her husband. He went up to her. "He had never been beaten in battle
by gods, danavas, gandharvas, yakshas or kimpurushas, yet bewildered by love he
went to the Ashoka grove. He wore heavenly clothes and polished jewelled
earrings, with a crown and a many-coloured garland, like Spring in bodily form.
He looked like a wishing tree, adorned with care, and yet, though adorned, he
was frightening, like a tree at a shrine in a cremation ground. The night-rover
was seen near the slender-waisted woman like the planet Saturn come near Rohini.

"Struck by the arrows of the god with the flower banner, he spoke to the
lovely-hipped girl, who was frightened like a helpless doe. 'Sita, enough of
this. You have done your husband favour enough. Be gracious, slender one, and
let yourself be adorned. Love me, lovely one, wearing costly ornaments and
clothes. Be the finest of all my women, fair one. I have daughters of the gods,
and wives of royal seers, daughters of danavas and women of the daityas.
Fourteen crores of pishachas stand at my command, and twice as many man-eating
rakshasas of terrible deeds. Three times that many yakshas do my bidding, though
some have gone over to my brother, the lord of wealth. Gandharvas and apsarases
always wait on me when I drink, lovely one, just as they do on my brother. I am
the son of the brahmin seer Vishravas himself, the sage, and my fame has spread
as the fifth guardian of the world. I have heavenly foods and drinks of every
kind, fair lady, just like the lord of the thirty gods. Let the hardship of your
forest life be over. Be my wife, lovely-hipped one, as Mandodari is.'

"When he said this, Vaidehi, with her lovely face, turned away and put a blade
of grass between them before she spoke to the night-rover. With her unlucky
tears she kept wetting her full breasts, and Vaidehi, whose husband was her god,
spoke to that mean creature. 'Again and again you speak such words, lord of the
rakshasas, and I, unlucky, have heard them in misery. Bless you, you whose
happiness is in the good. Turn back your heart. I am another man's wife, not to
be won, and always faithful to my husband. A wretched human woman is no fit wife
for you. What joy will you have in forcing a woman against her will? Your father
is a brahmin equal to Prajapati, born of Brahma. How is it that you, equal to a
guardian of the world, do not keep dharma? How are you not ashamed here, when
you name as your brother the lord of wealth, the king of kings, the friend of
Maheshvara?'

"When she had said this, Sita wept, her breasts and her slender neck trembling,
and covered her face with her garment. As the lovely woman wept, her long,
well-bound braid, dark and glossy, looked like a black snake on her head.

"When Ravana heard these very harsh words from Sita, the fool, though refused,
spoke again. 'Let the god with the sea-monster banner burn my limbs as he will,
Sita. I will not come to you, lovely-hipped one of the lovely smile, while you
do not want me. What can I do, when you still cling to a man, Rama, who is only
food for us?' When he had said this to the woman faultless in every limb, the
lord of the hosts of rakshasas vanished on the spot and went where he pleased.
Vaidehi, surrounded by rakshasis and worn by grief, stayed there, looked after
by Trijata.

---

"Raghava, with Saumitri, protected by Sugriva, was living on the slope of
Malyavat when he saw the clear sky. The slayer of enemies saw the spotless moon
in the clear sky, followed by the planets, the constellations and the stars. On
the mountain, the cool wind carrying the scent of white and blue water lilies
and lotuses suddenly woke him. In the morning, remembering Sita shut up in the
rakshasa's house, Rama, whose self was dharma, said in low spirits to the hero
Lakshmana.

"'Go, Lakshmana, and find out about the lord of the monkeys in Kishkindha. He is
careless in pleasures, ungrateful, clever only at his own ends. I consecrated
that fool, that lowest of his family, as king. All the monkeys, the gopucchas
and the bears serve him. For his sake I killed Valin with you, strong-armed
upholder of the line of Raghu, in the woods of Kishkindha. I think him the
lowest of monkeys on earth, ungrateful, since the fool does not know me today in
the state I am in. I think he does not know how to keep a pact. Surely, with his
small mind, he despises me, now that I have done him a service. If he lies idle,
given up to pleasure, you must send him by Valin's road, the road all creatures
take. But if the bull among monkeys is working for us, bring him with you,
Kakutstha. Hurry. Do not delay.'

"At his brother's words Lakshmana, who delighted in doing his elder's bidding,
took his fine bow with its string and arrows and set out. He reached the gate of
Kishkindha and went in unchallenged. Thinking him angry, the monkey king came
out to meet him. Sugriva, lord of the leaping monkeys, humble, with his wife,
received him gladly with the honour due to him. Saumitri, who feared nothing,
gave him Rama's message. When he had heard all of it, Sugriva, lord of the
monkeys, with his servants and wife, bowing with joined palms, said gladly to
Lakshmana, that elephant among men.

"'I am not stupid, Lakshmana, nor ungrateful, nor pitiless. Hear what effort I
have made in the search for Sita. I have sent out all my trained monkeys in
every direction, and I have set a time for them all to come back within a month.
They are to search this earth, hero, with its forests, mountains and cities,
clothed by the sea, with its villages, towns and mines. That month will be
complete in five nights. Then you and Rama will hear something very welcome.'

"At these words of the wise lord of the monkeys, Lakshmana, high-minded, let go
his anger and honoured Sugriva in return. With Sugriva he went to Rama, who was
on the slope of Malyavat, and told him how the task was going.

"So the lords of the monkeys came in their thousands. They had searched three
directions, all but those who had gone south. They told Rama they had searched
the earth with its girdle of ocean, but had seen neither Vaidehi nor Ravana.
Kakutstha kept himself alive, though in pain, putting his hope in the bulls
among monkeys who had gone to the south.

"When two months had passed, some monkeys came quickly to Sugriva and said, 'The
great, rich Madhuvana that Valin guarded, and you after him, best of monkeys, is
being eaten. The son of the wind is eating it, with Angada, Valin's son, and the
other bulls among monkeys whom you sent to search the south, king.' Hearing of
their boldness, he thought their task done. For this is how servants behave who
have done what they set out to do. The wise bull among monkeys told Rama, and
Rama too guessed that Maithili had been found.

"When they had rested, the monkeys led by Hanuman came to the lord of the
monkeys, in the presence of Rama and Lakshmana. When Rama saw Hanuman's gait and
the colour of his face, he was more sure than ever that Sita had been found,
Bharata. The monkeys led by Hanuman, their hearts full, bowed as was proper to
Rama, to Sugriva and to Lakshmana.

"Rama, bow and arrow in hand, said to them as they came, 'Will you give me life?
Have you done your task? Shall I rule the kingdom in Ayodhya again, when I have
killed my enemies in battle and brought back Janaka's daughter? Without freeing
Vaidehi and killing my enemies in battle, my wife carried off and myself
despised, I cannot bear to live.'

"When Rama had spoken, the son of the wind answered him, 'I bring you good news,
Rama. I have seen Janaki. We searched the south with its mountains, forests and
mines, and when the time had passed, we were tired, and we saw a great cave. We
went into it. It stretched for many yojanas, dark, thick with trees, deep, full
of insects. When we had gone a very long way we saw the light of the sun, and
there, inside it, a heavenly palace. It was the house, they say, of the daitya
Maya, Raghava. There an ascetic woman named Prabhavati was practising austerity.
We ate the food and the many kinds of drink she gave us, and our strength came
back. We went out of that place by the road she told us, and saw near the salt
water the mountains Sahya and Malaya, and the great mountain Dardura.

"'Then we climbed Malaya, and looked at the home of Varuna, the great ocean,
stretching many hundreds of yojanas, the home of whales, crocodiles and fish. We
were in despair, pained, weary, with no hope left for our lives. Thinking about
it in misery, we made up our minds to fast to death and sat down. Then, at the
end of our talk, there was talk of the vulture Jatayu.

"'Then we saw a bird like a mountain peak, terrible to look at and frightening,
like a second son of Vinata. He thought of eating us. He came up and said, "Ho!
Who is this who speaks of my brother Jatayu? I am his elder brother, named
Sampati, lord of the birds. Rivalling each other, we flew up to the court of the
sun. These wings of mine were burned, but Jatayu's were not. That was when I
last saw my dear brother, the lord of the vultures. My wings burned off, I fell
on this great mountain."

"'As he spoke, we told him his brother had been killed, and told him your
misfortune briefly. When he heard this very unwelcome news, Sampati was
downcast, king, and asked us again, tamer of enemies, "Who is this Rama? What of
Sita, and how was Jatayu killed? I want to hear all of it, best of monkeys." I
told him all of it in full, your misfortune and the reason for our fast.

"'The king of the birds raised us up with these words: "I know Ravana, and his
great city Lanka. I have seen it across the sea, in a valley of Mount Trikuta.
Vaidehi will be there. I have no doubt of it." When we heard his words we got up
quickly and took counsel about crossing the sea, scorcher of enemies. When no
one could bring himself to leap the sea, I entered my father and leapt the great
ocean, a hundred yojanas wide, killing a rakshasi of the waters.

"'There I saw Sita, the good woman, in Ravana's women's quarters, given to
fasting and austerity, longing to see her husband, her hair matted, her body
smeared with dirt, thin, wretched, an ascetic. I knew her for Sita by many
signs. I went up to the noble lady when she was alone and said, "Sita, I am
Rama's messenger, a monkey, the son of the wind. I have come here through the
sky, wanting to see you. The princes, the brothers Rama and Lakshmana, are well,
protected by Sugriva, lord of all the monkeys. Rama, with Saumitri, asks after
your welfare, Sita, and Sugriva, as his friend, asks after it too. Your husband
will soon come with all the monkeys. Trust me, lady. I am a monkey, not a
rakshasa."

"'Sita thought for a moment and answered me, "I know you are Hanuman, from what
Avindhya said. For Avindhya, strong-armed one, a rakshasa honoured by the old,
told me of Sugriva, surrounded by counsellors like you." Saying "Go," Sita gave
me this jewel, by which the faultless Vaidehi has kept herself alive all this
time. And as a token Janaki told this story: at the great mountain Chitrakuta,
tiger among men, you threw a reed at a crow. She told it so that you would know
it came from her.'

"When he had made himself known, and burned that city, he had come back. So Rama
honoured him who brought good news."

**Divergences.**

* **The burning of Lanka.** In the CE it is a single half-line at the very end of Hanuman's report: "when he had made himself known, and burned that city" (3.266.68). The details familiar from the Ramayana (his tail set alight, the city in flames) are not told here. See L-134.
* **The crow.** A rejected line (\*1273) adds that the crow lost an eye. The CE says only that Rama threw a reed at it (3.266.67).
* **Sampati.** A rejected line (\*1272) has Sampati lament that he cannot see his brother. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1270, \*1271) add small details. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.265.6: "like the planet Saturn come near Rohini", an evil conjunction.
* 3.265.7 *puṣpaketuśarāhataḥ*: "Struck by the arrows of the god with the flower banner", Kama.
* 3.265.14 *pañcamo lokapālānām*: "the fifth guardian of the world".
* 3.265.16: Mandodari, Ravana's wife, is named only here.
* 3.265.17 *tṛṇam antarataḥ kṛtvā*: "put a blade of grass between them".
* 3.265.27: "I will not come to you … while you do not want me." This agrees with the curse in 3.264.58–59.
* 3.266.3 *sahasā*: "suddenly woke him". "Suddenly" renders *sahasā*.
