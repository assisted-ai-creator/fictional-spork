# Maniman

Yudhishthira went up to him, whose sins were burned away by austerity, and
gladly bowed his head to him, saying his name. Then Krishna, Bhima and the
glorious twins bowed their heads to the royal seer and stood round him. So too
Dhaumya, who knew dharma, the Pandavas' family priest, came up as was proper to
that seer of strict vows.

The sage, who knew dharma, recognised the sons of Pandu, the best of the Kurus,
with his divine sight, and said, "Sit down." When the great ascetic had honoured
the wise bull of the Kurus, as he sat with his brothers, he asked after his
health.

"You do not set your heart on untruth, I hope, and you keep to dharma?" he said.
"Your care for your mother and father does not fail, Partha? All your elders,
the old and the learned, are honoured? You do not set your heart on evil deeds,
Partha? You know how to repay a good deed and how to set aside a bad one, best
of the Kurus, as is right, and you do not boast? The good are glad, I hope,
honoured by you as they deserve? Even living in the forest, you follow dharma
alone? Dhaumya is not grieved by your conduct, Partha? You follow the way of
your fathers and forefathers in giving, dharma, austerity, purity, straightness
and patience, Partha? You go by the road the royal seers have gone, Pandava?

"They say that when a son or a grandson is born in each family, the Fathers in
the world of the Fathers both grieve and laugh. 'What shall we have to suffer
for his bad deeds?' they think. 'And what good shall we win for his good deeds?'
Father, mother, the fire, the teacher and oneself as the fifth: the man who
honours these, Partha, has won both worlds.

"Seers who live on water and on air come flying through the sky to this best of
mountains at the joints of the moon. Kimpurushas can be seen on the mountain
peaks, king, lovers with their beloveds, devoted to each other. Many hosts of
gandharvas and apsarases can be seen, Partha, wearing clean clothes of silk. So
can hosts of vidyadharas, garlanded and fair to see, and hosts of great snakes,
suparnas, serpents and others. Above this mountain, at the joints of the moon,
the sound of kettledrums, tabors, conches and drums is heard. You must listen to
all of it from here, bulls of the Bharatas. You must never think of going there.
And you cannot go further than this, best of the Bharatas. For there the gods
take their pleasure, and it is a road men cannot tread.

"Here, Bharata, all creatures hate a man who is even a little restless in what
he does, and the rakshasas strike him. Beyond the peak of this mountain,
Yudhishthira, lies the road of the perfect siddhas and the divine seers. If
anyone goes on from here out of restlessness, Partha, the rakshasas kill him
with iron spears and other weapons, destroyer of enemies. Here, my son, at the
joints of the moon, Vaishravana is seen with his riches, drawn by men and
surrounded by apsarases. All creatures see him sitting on the peak, the lord of
all the rakshasas, like the risen sun. This mountain peak is the garden of the
gods, danavas and siddhas, and of Vaishravana, best of the Bharatas. At the
joints of the moon, my son, the sound of Tumburu's songs and Saman chants is
heard on Gandhamadana as he waits on the Giver of Wealth. All creatures often
see such wonders here at the joints of the moon, Yudhishthira, my son.

"Eat all the foods here, and the juicy fruits, and live here, best of the
Pandavas, until you see Arjuna. Once you have come here, my son, you must not be
restless in any way. Live here as you like, take your pleasure as you wish, and
then, best of those who bear weapons, you will rule the earth."

---

"All the great sons of Pandu had the courage of gods," said Janamejaya. "How
long did they live on the mountain Gandhamadana? And what did those great ones,
the heroes of the world, eat while they lived there? Tell me, best of men. And
tell me at length of Bhimasena's courage, what the strong-armed one did on that
Himalayan mountain. Was there no more fighting with the yakshas, best of
brahmins? Did they meet Vaishravana? For the Giver of Wealth comes there, as
Arshtishena said. I want to hear this at length, rich in austerity. I never have
enough of hearing what they did."

When the bulls of the Bharatas had heard this advice for their own good from
Arshtishena of matchless splendour, they always did as he told them. They ate
the food of sages and juicy fruits, and the meat of deer killed with clean
arrows, and honey of many kinds, fit for offering, from the back of the
Himalaya. So the Pandavas, bulls of the Bharatas, lived there. As they lived
there, listening to the many things Lomasha told them, the fifth year came.
Ghatotkacha had gone before this with all the rakshasas, saying, "I will be
there when there is work to do," lord.

Many months passed as the great ones lived in Arshtishena's hermitage, seeing
great wonders. As the Pandavas took their pleasure there, glad and blessed sages
and charanas came to see them, perfected, keeping their vows, and the best of
the Bharatas talked with them of divine things.

---

After some days Suparna suddenly carried off a mighty great snake that lived in
a great lake. The great mountain shook, and great trees were crushed. All
creatures saw that wonder, and the Pandavas too. Then the wind carried all kinds
of garlands, fragrant and fine, from the top of that best of mountains towards
the Pandavas. There the Pandavas, with their friends, and glorious Draupadi, saw
divine flowers of five colours.

Then Krishna spoke at the right time to strong-armed Bhimasena, as he sat at
ease in a lonely spot on the mountain. "Bull of the Bharatas, by the force of
Suparna's wind, that mighty breath, flowers of five colours are falling towards
the river Ashvaratha in sight of all creatures. At Khandava your brother, true
to his word, lord of men, held back gandharvas, snakes, rakshasas and Vasava. He
killed fierce sorcerers and won the bow Gandiva. Your splendour too is very
great, and so is the strength of your arms, unbearable, not to be withstood,
like the strength of Shatakratu. Let all the rakshasas, terrified by the force
of your arms, leave the mountain and go to the ten quarters, Bhimasena. Then let
your friends see the fine top of this best of mountains, with its bright
garlands, free of fear and bewilderment. My heart has been set on this a long
time, Bhima. I want to see the mountain top, trusting in the strength of your
arms."

Then the scorcher of enemies felt as if Draupadi had struck him, and the
strong-armed one could not bear it, as a good bull cannot bear a blow. The
glorious Pandava walked like a lion or a bull. He was noble, bright as gold,
proud, strong, spirited, high-minded and brave. His eyes were red and his
shoulders broad. He had the stride of a rutting elephant, the teeth of a lion
and great shoulders, and he stood tall as a young shala tree. The great one was
handsome in every limb, with a neck like a conch shell and mighty arms. He took
up his gold-backed bow, his sword and his quivers. Like a proud lion, like an
elephant in rut, free of fear and bewilderment, the mighty one rushed at the
mountain. All creatures saw him coming like the king of beasts, like an elephant
in rut, carrying arrows, sword and bow. To increase Draupadi's joy the Pandava
took his mace and entered the king of mountains, free of fear and bewilderment.
Never did weariness, cowardice, faintness or envy touch the Partha, son of the
Wind.

He came to a single path, rough and terrible to see, and the mighty one climbed
the peak, as tall as many palm trees. Gladdening the kinnaras, great snakes,
sages, gandharvas and rakshasas, the mighty one reached the top of the mountain.
There the bull of the Bharatas saw Vaishravana's dwelling, adorned with golden
buildings that looked like crystal. A very pleasant wind born of Gandhamadana
blew there, carrying every scent and delighting every creature. There were trees
of many kinds, beyond thought, very beautiful, bright, of many colours, bearing
bright clusters of flowers. The bull of the Bharatas saw the place of the lord
of the rakshasas, hung with nets of jewels, fine, bearing bright garlands.

With mace, sword and bow in his hands, having given up all care for his life,
strong-armed Bhimasena stood there unmoving, like a mountain. Then he blew his
conch, which made the hair of his enemies stand on end, and with the twang of
his bowstring and the slap of his palm he bewildered all the creatures there.
Then, their hair on end, yakshas, rakshasas and gandharvas rushed at that sound
towards the Pandava. Maces, iron bars, swords, spears, lances and axes shone,
held in the arms of the yakshas and rakshasas.

Then the fight began between them and him, Bharata. With broad-headed arrows of
terrible speed, Bhima cut down the spears, lances and axes the huge ones hurled.
The mighty one pierced with his arrows the bodies of the roaring rakshasas,
those in the sky and those on the ground. A great rain of blood poured down on
the mighty one, streams falling from the bodies of the rakshasas on every side.
Bodies and heads of yakshas and rakshasas were seen cut off in many ways by the
arrows loosed by the strength of Bhima's arms. All creatures saw the handsome
Pandava covered by the rakshasas, like the sun by masses of cloud. Like the sun
with its rays, the mighty, strong-armed one, true in courage, struck them all
with his arrows that killed enemies. Threatening him and roaring great roars,
none of the rakshasas saw Bhimasena falter. Their whole bodies wounded by
arrows, tormented by fear of Bhimasena, their great weapons scattered, they gave
a terrible cry of pain. Terrified by the firm bowman, they threw away their
maces and spears, their swords, lances and axes, and went off to the south.

There was a rakshasa there named Maniman, a friend of Vaishravana, broad-chested
and strong-armed, with spear and mace in his hands. The mighty one showed his
authority and his manhood. Seeing the others turned back, he said as if smiling,
"Many of you have been beaten in battle by one man, a human. When you reach
Vaishravana's dwelling, what will you tell the Lord of Wealth?"

When he had said this to all of them, the rakshasa turned back, and with spear,
lance and mace in his hands he ran at the Pandava. As he came on fast, like an
elephant in rut, Bhimasena struck him in the side with three calf-tooth arrows.
Maniman too, in a rage, took up a huge mace, whirled it and hurled it at
Bhimasena. It was huge, most terrible, like lightning in the sky, and Bhimasena
met it with many arrows sharpened on stone. All the arrows struck the mace and
flew back. Swift as they were, they could not check the speed of the mace. But
the mighty one of terrible courage knew the ways of fighting with the mace, and
he made the blow miss.

In that very moment the clever rakshasa hurled a most terrible spear of iron
with a golden shaft. With a terrible roar it pierced Bhima's right arm and fell
suddenly to the ground, most fierce, with flames of fire. Deeply pierced by the
spear, the Kaurava of boundless courage, the great bowman, skilled in fighting
with the mace, took up his mace. Taking that mace, made all of iron, Bhima
roared and rushed swiftly at mighty Maniman. Maniman too took a great blazing
spear and hurled it at Bhimasena with great force, roaring. The one skilled in
fighting with the mace broke the spear with the end of his mace and rushed at
him quickly, like Garuda at a snake. At the front of the battle the strong-armed
one leapt up into the sky, suddenly whirled his mace and hurled it with a roar.
Like Indra's thunderbolt hurled by Indra, as fast as the wind, it killed the
rakshasa, reached the earth and fell, like a spell of witchcraft. All creatures
saw that rakshasa of terrible strength struck down by Bhimasena, as a lion
strikes down the lord of the cows. When they saw him dead on the ground, the
night-rangers who were left gave a terrible cry of pain and went off to the
east.
