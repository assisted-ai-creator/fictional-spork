# Rama's Exile

"Lord, you have told the birth of Rama and the others, each in turn," said
Yudhishthira. "Now I want to hear why they left. Tell me, brahmin. How were the
heroic brothers Rama and Lakshmana, sons of Dasharatha, sent to the forest, and
glorious Maithili?"

"When his sons were born, King Dasharatha was happy," said Markandeya. "He
delighted in the rites, was devoted to dharma, and always waited on the old. In
time his sons grew up, full of energy, masters of the Vedas and their secrets
and of the science of the bow. When they had kept their studenthood and had
taken wives, king, Dasharatha was glad and happy.

"The eldest of them was Rama, and he delighted the people. The wise one, by his
charm, pleased his father's heart. Then the king, being wise, thought himself
old, and took counsel with his ministers and with his household priests, who
knew dharma, about consecrating Rama as crown prince, Bharata. All those best of
counsellors thought the time had come.

"His eyes were red-rimmed, his arms were strong and long, he walked like a
rutting elephant, his chest was broad and his hair dark and curling. The hero
blazed with splendour and was no less than Shakra in strength. He had mastered
all dharma and was like Brihaspati in understanding. All the people loved him,
and he was skilled in all learning. He had conquered his senses, and even his
enemies' eyes were charmed by the sight of him. He curbed the wicked and guarded
those who walked in dharma. He was steadfast, not to be overcome, a conqueror
never beaten. When King Dasharatha looked at that son, who increased Kausalya's
joy, he felt the greatest delight, joy of the Kurus.

"Thinking over Rama's virtues, the mighty king, full of energy, spoke gladly to
his household priest. 'Bless you. Tonight, brahmin, the Pushya star will come
into a holy conjunction. Let the things be gathered for me, and let Rama be
invited.'

"Manthara heard these words of the king, and at the right moment she went to
Kaikeyi and said, 'Today, Kaikeyi, the king has proclaimed your great
misfortune. An angry, savage snake is biting you, unlucky woman. Kausalya is the
lucky one, whose son is to be consecrated. Where is your good fortune, when your
son has no share in the kingdom?'

"When she understood these words, Kaikeyi put on all her ornaments and, with a
waist as slender as an altar, carrying the highest beauty, went to her husband
in private. As if laughing, with a bright smile, as if showing her love, she
said sweetly, 'You keep your promises. Grant me the one wish you once gave me,
king, and so free yourself from your obligation.'

"'I will give you a boon,' said the king. 'Take whatever you wish. Who that
should not be killed shall be killed today? Who that should be killed shall be
set free? To whom shall I give wealth today, and from whom shall it be taken?
Whatever wealth I have here, apart from what belongs to brahmins, is yours.'

"When she understood his words, she held the king to them, knowing her own
strength, and said to him, 'Let Bharata have the consecration you have prepared
for Rama, and let Raghava go to the forest.'

"When the king heard these unwelcome words, which would have terrible results,
he was stricken with grief and said nothing at all, best of the Bharatas. Then
heroic Rama, whose self was dharma, learned what had been said to his father,
and set out for the forest, so that the king should be true to his word.
Glorious Lakshmana followed him then with his bow, and so did his wife Sita,
bless you, Vaidehi, daughter of Janaka.

"When Rama had gone to the forest, King Dasharatha met the end of his body by
the law of the turning of time. When she learned that Rama had gone and that the
king was dead, Queen Kaikeyi sent for Bharata and said, 'Dasharatha has gone to
heaven, and Rama and Lakshmana are in the forest. Take the great kingdom, safe
and cleared of thorns.'

"He whose self was dharma said to her, 'Alas, you have done a cruel thing,
killing your husband and destroying this family out of greed for wealth. You
have brought disgrace down on my head, shame of the family. Have your wish,
mother!' And he wept.

"He cleared his conduct before all the people, and then followed his brother
Rama, longing to bring him back. Deep in grief, he sent Kausalya, Sumitra and
Kaikeyi ahead in carriages and went with Shatrughna, with Vasishtha and Vamadeva
and thousands of other brahmins, and with the people of the city and the
country, wanting to bring Rama back. He saw Rama with Lakshmana at Chitrakuta,
bow in hand, wearing the dress of an ascetic. Sent away by Rama, who was keeping
his father's word, he ruled the kingdom at Nandigrama, placing Rama's sandals
before him.

"Rama, fearing that the people of the city and the country would come again,
went into the great forest towards Sharabhanga's hermitage. He honoured
Sharabhanga, went into the Dandaka forest, and lived by the lovely river
Godavari.

"While Rama lived there, a great enmity arose with Khara, who lived in
Janasthana, because of what Shurpanakha did. Raghava, who loved dharma, killed
fourteen thousand rakshasas on earth to protect the ascetics. When he had killed
the very mighty Dushana and Khara, wise Raghava made the forest of dharma safe
again.

"When those rakshasas had been killed, Shurpanakha, her nose and lips cut off,
went back to Lanka, to her brother's house. The rakshasi came to Ravana,
fainting with grief, and fell at her brother's feet, her face caked with dried
blood. When Ravana saw her disfigured like that, he was beside himself with
anger. He leapt up from his seat in fury, grinding his teeth. He sent his
counsellors away and said to her in private, 'Who has done this to you, good
woman, without a thought for me, and in scorn of me? Who has taken hold of a
sharp spike and lies on it with his whole body? Who puts fire on his head and
sleeps soundly without a care? Who treads here on the most terrible venomous
snake? Who stands with his hand between the teeth of a maned lion?'

"As he spoke, flames of fire burst from the openings of his body, like a tree
burning at night from its own hollows. His sister told him all of it, Rama's
valour and the defeat of the rakshasas, with Khara and Dushana. He decided what
to do, comforted his sister, made arrangements in the city, and rose up into the
sky. He passed over Trikuta and the Black Mountain, and saw the deep waters of
the great ocean, the home of sea monsters. Crossing it, ten-faced Ravana came to
Gokarna, the dear and peaceful place of the great god who holds the trident.
There ten-faced Ravana went to Maricha, his former counsellor, who had taken to
austerity long before out of fear of Rama.

---

"When Maricha saw Ravana come, he was flustered, and honoured him with
courtesies, with fruits, roots and other things. When Ravana had rested and was
seated, the rakshasa sat beside him. Skilled in speech, he said courteous words
to the master of speech. 'Your colour is not as it should be. I hope all is well
in your city. I hope all your people serve you as before. And what business
brings you here, lord of the rakshasas? Know that it is done already, however
hard it may be.'

"Ravana told him all that Rama had done. When he had heard it, Maricha said to
Ravana briefly, 'Do not go near Rama. I know his strength. What great man could
bear the force of his arrows? That bull among men is the very reason I took to
the ascetic's life. Which wicked man has told you of this doorway to your ruin?'

"Ravana, angry, threatened him and said, 'If you do not do as I say, your death
is certain.' Maricha thought, 'Death at the hands of a better man is preferable.
Since death has come in any case, I shall do what he wants.' Then Maricha
answered the lord of the rakshasas, 'What help must I give you? I shall do it,
though against my will.'

"Dashagriva said to him, 'Go and tempt Sita. Become a deer with jewelled horns
and a coat dappled with jewels. When Sita sees you she will surely send Rama
after you. When Kakutstha has gone, Sita will be in my power. I shall take her
and carry her off, and then the fool will perish from the loss of his wife. Give
me this help.'

"When this had been said to him, Maricha made the water offering for himself,
and followed Ravana in misery as he went ahead. Then they went to the hermitage
of Rama, whose deeds were untiring, and the two of them did everything as they
had planned. Ravana became an ascetic, shaven-headed, with a water-pot and a
triple staff, and Maricha became a deer, and they came to that place.

"Maricha, in the form of a deer, showed himself to Vaidehi. Driven by fate, she
urged Rama to go after it. To please her, Rama quickly took up his bow, left
Lakshmana to guard her, and went off wanting the deer. With his bow, his quiver
bound on, his sword and his lizard-skin finger guards, Rama ran after the deer,
as Rudra ran after the star-deer. The rakshasa kept vanishing and showing
himself again, and drew him a long way. Then Rama understood. Knowing it to be a
night-rover, quick-witted Raghava took an unfailing arrow and killed the one in
the form of a deer.

"Struck by Rama's arrow, he took on Rama's voice and cried out in a voice of
pain, 'Ah, Sita! Lakshmana!'

"Vaidehi heard that pitiful cry and ran towards where the sound came from. Then
Lakshmana said to her, 'Enough of your fears, timid one. Who could overcome
Rama? In a moment you will see Rama come back, you of the bright smile.'

"At this, weeping, she suspected her brother-in-law, whose pure conduct was his
ornament, struck down as she was by a woman's nature. The good woman, faithful
to her husband, began to speak harshly to him. 'The time will never come, fool,
for what you long for in your heart. I would take a weapon and kill myself, or
fall from a mountain peak, or enter the fire, before I would leave my husband
Rama and ever go to you, a low man, as a tigress would go to a jackal.'

"When he heard such words, Lakshmana, who loved Raghava and whose conduct was
good, covered his ears and set off the way Raghava had gone. Taking up his bow,
he followed Rama's tracks.

"At that moment the rakshasa Ravana appeared, evil in a good form, like a fire
hidden under ashes, disguised in the dress of an ascetic, wanting to carry off
the faultless woman. When the daughter of Janaka, who knew dharma, saw him
arrive, she invited him to eat fruits, roots and the like. He scorned all that,
took his own form, and the bull among the rakshasas tried to win Vaidehi with
soft words. 'Sita, I am the king of the rakshasas, famed by the name of Ravana.
My city is lovely Lanka, across the great ocean. There you will shine among the
finest women with me. Be my wife, lovely-hipped one. Leave the ascetic Raghava.'

"When Sita Janaki heard these and other words, she covered her ears and said,
'Not so! The sky would fall with its stars and the earth break into pieces, fire
would turn cold, before I would leave the joy of the Raghus. How could a female
elephant who has been with a great elephant, a lord of the herd who roams the
forest with his temples broken open in rut, touch a boar? How could any woman
who has drunk mead and honeyed wine long for sour rice gruel? So I think.'

"When she had said this to him, she went back into the hermitage. Ravana ran
after the lovely woman and stopped her. He threatened her in a harsh voice until
she fainted, seized her by the hair and rose into the sky.

"Then Jatayu, the vulture who lived on the mountain, saw her being carried off,
the poor woman, weeping, 'Rama! Rama!'"

**Divergences.**

* **The length of the exile.** The Mahabharata's telling here gives no number of years. A rejected line (\*1246) has Kaikeyi ask for nine and five years. Elsewhere the CE says Rama lived fourteen years in the forest (12.29.53).
* **Shurpanakha's nose.** The text says only that she came to Lanka "her nose and lips cut off" (3.261.44). It does not say who cut them, or why. See L-155.
* **No line drawn.** Lakshmana covers his ears and leaves (3.262.29). He draws no protective line; the "Lakshmana line" is not in the Mahabharata. See L-155.
* **Rama's reply to Bharata.** Rejected passages (\*1248–\*1249) have Bharata tell Rama of his father's death, and Rama offer the water rites and send him back. Not narrated. The CE says only that Rama sent him away (3.261.38).
* Other rejected lines (\*1242–\*1245, \*1247, \*1250–\*1257) add Dasharatha's eight counsellors, more of Sita's oaths, and other details. Not narrated.
