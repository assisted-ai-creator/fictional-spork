# Kirmira

"I want to hear of the killing of Kirmira, steward," said Dhritarashtra. "Tell
me. How did Bhimasena meet the rakshasa?"

"Hear this deed of Bhima, whose deeds are beyond human," said Vidura. "I have
heard it again and again, when they were telling stories.

"When the Pandavas left here, king of kings, beaten at dice, they travelled for
three days and nights and came to the forest called Kamyaka. It was the dead of
night, a fearful hour, king, midnight, when the man-eating rakshasas of terrible
deeds go abroad. The ascetics and the other forest-dwellers always keep far away
from that forest, they say, for fear of the man-eaters.

"As they went in, Bharata, a terrible rakshasa appeared, blocking their way,
with blazing eyes and a firebrand in his hand. He spread out his huge arms, and
his mouth was fearful. He stood there, barring the path the pride of the Kurus
were taking. He bit his lips with his fangs, his eyes were coppery, and his hair
stood up blazing. He was like a cloud with cranes in it, with the rays of the
sun and a ring of lightning. He was working the magic of the rakshasas, and
roaring with a great roar, letting out a huge noise like a cloud full of rain.
At his roar the birds on every side took fright and flew off, crying, the birds
of the land together with the birds of the water. The forest was filled with
deer, leopards, buffaloes and bears running in terror, and at his roar it seemed
that the whole forest had set off. Struck by the wind of his thighs, the
creepers far away, with their copper-coloured shoots for arms, clung to the
trees. At that moment a very fierce wind blew, and the sky was covered with dust
and lost its stars. He was a great enemy, and the five sons of Pandu did not
know him, as the five senses do not know the matchless onset of grief.

"When he saw the Pandavas far off, dressed in black deerskins, he blocked the
entrance of the forest, like the mountain Mainaka. When lotus-eyed Krishna came
upon him, she was terrified, and shut her eyes in fear at a thing she had never
seen before. Her hair was still loose where Duhshasana's hand had tossed it.
Standing among the five of them, she was like a river troubled among five
mountains. As she fainted there, the five Pandavas held her, as the senses,
clinging to their objects, hold on to delight.

"Then mighty Dhaumya, while the sons of Pandu looked on, destroyed that terrible
rakshasa magic that had risen up, with various spells against rakshasas, well
recited. When his magic was destroyed, the rakshasa was seen, very strong, his
eyes wide with anger, a low creature able to take any shape he liked, like Death
at the end of time.

"Then King Yudhishthira, of long wisdom, said to him, 'Who are you? Whose are
you? What do you want done? Tell us.'

"The rakshasa answered Yudhishthira, the Dharma King. 'I am the brother of Baka,
and well known as Kirmira. I live without a care in this empty Kamyaka forest,
always making my meals of men I beat in fight. Who are you, who have come near
me here, to be my food? I will beat you all in fight and eat you, without a
care.'

"When he heard the wicked creature's words, Yudhishthira told him everything,
his family, his name and the rest, Bharata. 'I am the Pandava, the Dharma King,
if you have heard of me, with all my brothers, Bhimasena, Arjuna and the others.
Robbed of our kingdom, we have made up our minds to live in the forest, and we
have come to this terrible forest of yours.'

"Kirmira said to him, 'By good fortune the gods have brought me today what I
have wanted for so long. I have been wandering the whole earth, always with my
weapon raised to kill Bhimasena, and I have not come upon him. By good fortune I
have come upon him now, my brother's killer, whom I have wanted so long. For he
killed my dear brother Baka, at Vetrakiyagriha, king, disguised as a brahmin. He
relied on the power of spells, for he has no strength of his own. And the wicked
man killed my dear friend Hidimba, who lived in the forest, and carried off his
sister long ago. Now the fool has come into this deep forest of mine, at
midnight, when we go abroad. Today I will pay him back for that long-stored
enmity, and I will satisfy Baka with plenty of his blood. Today I will be free
of my debt to my brother and my friend, and I will find the greatest peace, when
I have killed this thorn in the side of the rakshasas. If Bhimasena escaped from
Baka before, I will eat him today, Yudhishthira, while you watch. I will kill
this Vrikodara, with all his great strength, eat him and digest him, as Agastya
did the great asura.'

"When he said this, the righteous Yudhishthira, true to his word, cried angrily,
'That will not be!' and rebuked the rakshasa.

"Then strong-armed Bhima quickly tore up a tree, ten fathoms high, and stripped
it of its leaves. And in the blink of an eye Arjuna, the victor, strung Gandiva,
as heavy as the crushing thunderbolt. But Bhima held Jishnu back, and ran at
that terrible rakshasa, crying, 'Stand! Stand!', Bharata.

"With this, the Pandava, in a fury, tightened his girdle, pressed his hands
together and bit his lips. Then, with the tree for a weapon, mighty Bhima rushed
at him. He brought it down with force on the rakshasa's head, like the staff of
Yama, as Maghavan brings down the thunderbolt. But the rakshasa did not flinch
in the fight. He hurled his blazing firebrand, like a flaming thunderbolt.
Bhima, the best of fighters, kicked the firebrand back with his left foot, and
it flew back at the rakshasa. Then Kirmira too suddenly tore up a tree, and
fought the Pandava in fury, like Yama with his staff in his hand.

"That fight with trees destroyed the forest, like the fight between the brothers
Valin and Sugriva long ago, each wanting the kingdom. The trees that fell on
their heads broke into many pieces, like blue and red lotuses on the heads of
two elephants in rut. Many trees there were shredded like munja grass, and lay
about the great forest like rags. That fight with trees went on for a good
while, lord of the people, between the chief of the rakshasas and the best of
men.

"Then the rakshasa, in fury, lifted a rock and hurled it at Bhima as he stood
there fighting, and Bhimasena staggered. The rakshasa rushed at him while he was
stunned by the blow of the rock, as Svarbhanu rushes at the sun, his arms
flinging off its rays. The two of them locked together, each dragging at the
other, and fought like two bulls. There was a terrible, tumultuous struggle
between them, like that of two proud tigers fighting with claws and teeth.

"Proud of his strength of arm, and stung by the insults of Duryodhana, Vrikodara
grew, as Krishna's eyes watched him. In his fury he fell on him and seized him
in his arms, as one elephant seizes another, its temples running with rut. The
mighty rakshasa seized him in turn. Then Bhimasena, the best of the strong,
flung him down by force. From the crushing of their arms, as the two strong ones
grappled, there came a terrible noise in the fight, like the splitting of
bamboos.

"Then Vrikodara threw him down by force, seized him round the middle, and shook
him hard, as a raging wind shakes a tree. Held by strong Bhima in the fight, the
weakened creature struggled as long as his breath lasted, and dragged at the
Pandava. When Vrikodara saw that he was worn out, he bound him with his arms, as
one binds a beast with a rope. Then he whirled him round and round for a long
time, as he roared a great roar, like a broken drum, struggling and senseless.

"When the son of Pandu knew that the rakshasa was failing, he seized him with
force in his arms, and killed him the way one kills a beast. Vrikodara pressed
his knee into the small of the vile rakshasa's back, and squeezed his throat
with his arms. Then, when all his limbs were numb and his eyes were rolling
horribly, he threw him down on the ground and said, 'You wicked creature, you
will not wipe away the tears of Hidimba and Baka. You have gone to the house of
Yama.'

"With these words the hero among men, his eyes rolling with anger, let the
rakshasa go. His clothes and ornaments had slipped from him, he twitched, his
mind was gone, and he was dead. When the rakshasa, who looked like a storm
cloud, had been killed, the princes put Krishna in front, praised Bhima for his
many virtues, and set out gladly for Dvaitavana.

"So Kirmira was killed in the fight, lord of men, by Bhima, at the word of the
Dharma King, Kaurava. Then the unconquered man, who knew dharma, rid the forest
of its thorn and made his home there with Draupadi. All those bulls of the
Bharatas comforted Draupadi, and in their gladness they praised Vrikodara
warmly. When the rakshasa had been crushed and destroyed by the strength of
Bhima's arms, the heroes entered the forest, which was safe now, its thorn
destroyed.

"As I travelled that way, I saw the wicked creature lying in the great forest,
dreadful to see, killed by Bhima's strength. And I heard there of this deed of
Bhima's, Bharata, from the brahmins who had gathered there and were telling it."

When he heard how Kirmira, the chief of the rakshasas, had been killed in the
fight, the king sank into thought, and sighed like a man in pain.

**Divergences.**

* **Where they went.** After the killing, the CE says the Pandavas "set out gladly for
  Dvaitavana" (3.12.68), and then that they lived "in that forest" (3.12.70). Both are kept as
  the text has them. The Pandavas' move to Dvaitavana is told later, at 3.24.
* The vulgate adds a line that Kirmira was born in the Paithina family, an unbeliever who
  reviled the Vedas (\*40). Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 3.12.1–74: Vidura tells the story, so it is given inside quotes.
* 3.12.14: the simile of the five senses and grief is kept.
* 3.12.15 *maināka iva*: "like the mountain Mainaka".
* 3.12.17: "like a river troubled among five mountains".
* 3.12.31 *vetrakīyagṛhe*: "at Vetrakiyagriha", the place where Baka lived (Book 1).
  *vidyābalam*: "the power of spells". This is Kirmira's claim.
