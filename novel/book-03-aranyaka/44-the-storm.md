# The Storm

The heroes had strung their bows. They carried quivers full of arrows and
swords, and had bound on their lizard-skin finger guards. Men of boundless
power, the best of all bowmen, they took the finest brahmins with them and set
out for Gandhamadana with the Panchali, king. On the mountain top they saw
lakes, rivers, mountains and forests, trees with wide shade, and places always
in flower and fruit, where hosts of divine seers go. The heroes, living on roots
and fruit, kept themselves in check and went through places high and low, rough
and narrow, seeing many kinds of beasts. The great ones entered the mountain
where kinnaras wander, where seers, siddhas and immortals live, dear to the
gandharvas and apsarases.

As the heroes entered the mountain Gandhamadana, a fierce wind and a great rain
arose, lord of the people. Then a great dust rose, full of leaves, and covered
the earth, the air and the sky with darkness. Nothing could be seen, for the sky
was hidden by dust, and they could not even speak to one another. Their eyes
struck blind by the darkness, they could not see one another, dragged by the
wind with its grit of stones, Bharata. There was a great noise of trees broken
by the wind, crashing to the ground, and of other things that grow from the
earth. Bewildered by the wind, they all thought, "Is the sky falling to the
ground? Are the mountains splitting?" Feeling with their hands for the nearest
trees, ant hills and hollows, they hid from the wind in fear.

Then mighty Bhimasena raised his bow, took Krishna with him and stood sheltering
by a tree. The Dharma King and Dhaumya hid in the great forest. Sahadeva took
the sacred fires and sheltered on the mountain. Nakula, the other brahmins and
the great ascetic Lomasha went to trees in fear and hid here and there. When the
wind dropped and the dust settled, rain came quickly in great drops. Then
streams mixed with stones poured down without pause, covering everything, driven
by the swift wind. Then waters running to the sea appeared on every side, muddy
and foaming, lord of the people. Carrying great floods, covered with foam like
rafts, they ran round with a great roar, dragging trees along. At last the rain
stopped and the wind grew calm. The water drained into the hollows and the sun
came out. They all came out slowly and gathered, Bharata, and the heroes set out
again for the mountain Gandhamadana.

---

As soon as the great Pandavas had set out, Draupadi, who was not used to going
on foot, sat down. Tired, full of sorrow, worn by that wind and rain, and
delicate as she was, the glorious Panchali fainted. As she was falling in her
faint, the black-eyed woman held herself up with her round, shapely arms against
her thighs. Holding her thighs, which were like an elephant's trunk, she
suddenly fell to the ground, trembling like a plantain tree. As the fine woman
fell, like a creeper catching, mighty Nakula ran and caught her.

"King, the daughter of the king of the Panchalas, the black-eyed one, has fallen
to the ground, worn out," said Nakula. "Look after her, Bharata. She who does
not deserve sorrow, who walks so gently, has come to the greatest sorrow.
Comfort her, great king, worn out by weariness."

At his words the king, in great sorrow, and Bhima and Sahadeva ran to her at
once. When the son of Kunti saw her pale and thin, the righteous one took her on
his lap and lamented in pain. "How can she, the lovely one, who deserves
happiness, used to well guarded houses and well spread beds, lie fallen on the
ground? How have the tender feet and the lotus face of this woman who deserves
the best turned dark today, because of me? What have I done, foolish lover of
dice that I am, wandering with Krishna in the forest full of beasts? 'The
Panchali will find happiness with the Pandavas for her husbands.' So her father,
King Drupada, gave the long-eyed one. And she has had none of it. Worn out by
weariness and grief, she lies fallen on the ground, because of the deeds of evil
me."

As the Dharma King Yudhishthira lamented like this, all the best of the
twice-born with Dhaumya came there. They comforted him and honoured him with
blessings, and they said mantras that kill rakshasas and performed the rites.
The great seers recited mantras for peace. The Pandavas stroked her again and
again with cool hands, and a cool wind mixed with water fanned her. So the
Panchali found ease and slowly came to her senses. Then they laid the poor woman
on a deerskin, and when she had come to herself, the ascetic woman, they let her
rest. The twins gently rubbed her feet, red-soled and marked with good signs,
with their hands hardened by the bowstring.

The Dharma King Yudhishthira comforted her too, and the best of the Kurus said
to Bhimasena, "There are many mountains, Bhima, rough and hard to cross with
snow. How will Krishna go through them, strong-armed one?"

"I will carry you myself, king," said Bhimasena, "and the princess, and the
twins, bulls among men. Do not be downcast, king of kings. Or else Ghatotkacha,
whom I fathered, who travels through the sky, as strong as I am, could carry us
all at your word, sinless one."

With the Dharma King's leave he thought of his rakshasa son. As soon as his
father thought of him, righteous Ghatotkacha stood before him with joined hands
and greeted the Pandavas and the brahmins, and they welcomed the strong-armed
one. Then he said to his father Bhimasena, true in his courage, "You thought of
me, and I have come quickly to serve you. Command me, strong-armed one. I will
do everything, no doubt of it." When Bhimasena heard this, he embraced the
rakshasa.

---

"He knows dharma, he is strong and brave, a bull among rakshasas, devoted to us,
your own son, Bhima," said Yudhishthira. "Let him carry his mother at once. By
your strength, Bhima of most terrible courage, I shall go unharmed with the
Panchali to Gandhamadana."

Understanding his brother's words, Bhimasena, tiger among men, gave the order to
his son Ghatotkacha, the scourge of enemies. "Son of Hidimba, your mother, whom
no one can defeat, is worn out. You go where you will, my son, and you are
strong. Carry her, you who travel the sky. Bless you. Put her on your shoulder
and go through the air in the midst of us, flying low, so that you do not hurt
her."

"I alone could carry the Dharma King and Dhaumya, the princess and the twins,"
said Ghatotkacha, "let alone today, when I have help."

When he had said this, Ghatotkacha carried Krishna, the hero, in the midst of
the Pandavas, and others carried the Pandavas. Lomasha, of matchless splendour,
went by the road of the siddhas by his own power, like a second sun. The
rakshasas of terrible courage took up all those brahmins at the command of the
lord of the rakshasas and went. So they went towards wide Badari, looking at
lovely forests and groves. Carried by those swift, mighty rakshasas, the heroes
went quickly, and the long road seemed short.

They saw lands full of hosts of mlecchas, with mines of jewels of every kind.
They saw the feet of mountains veined with ores of every kind. These were
crowded with vidyadharas, with monkeys and kinnaras, kimpurushas and gandharvas
on every side. They were full of networks of rivers, loud with the calls of
birds of every kind, full of beasts of every kind and adorned with monkeys. They
passed many lands, and the Northern Kurus too, and saw Kailasa, the finest of
mountains, full of wonders of every kind.

Near it they saw the hermitage of Nara and Narayana, with divine trees always in
flower and fruit. And they saw that lovely jujube tree, Badari. Its trunk was
round, and it was glossy, with thick shade, full of the highest beauty. It was
lovely with its glossy, thick, soft leaves, its wide branches spreading far,
full of splendour. It was heavy with divine, sweet fruits, always dripping
honey, divine, where hosts of great seers go, always full of flocks of birds of
every kind, merry and drunk. It grew in a place without gadflies or mosquitoes,
rich in roots, fruit and water, covered with dark green grass, where gods and
gandharvas go. It was a lovely place, its ground well levelled by nature, soft
and cool to the touch, free of thorns.

The great ones came to it with those bulls among brahmins, and all of them
slowly got down from the rakshasas' shoulders. Then the Pandavas, with the best
of the twice-born, saw that holy hermitage where Nara and Narayana lived, king.
It was free of darkness, holy, untouched by the sun's rays, free of the troubles
of hunger, thirst, cold and heat, a place that took away grief. It was crowded
with great seers, full of the glory of brahman, hard to enter, great king, for
men who have put themselves outside dharma. It was honoured with offerings and
oblations, divine, well swept and anointed, shining on every side with offerings
of divine flowers. It was full of wide fire halls, with fine ladles and vessels,
adorned with great water pots and baskets, a refuge for all creatures, loud with
the sound of the Veda. That divine hermitage, a place to rest in, took away
weariness. Its beauty was past telling, and it was adorned with the ways of the
gods. Great seers lived there, eating fruit and roots, self-controlled, dressed
in bark and black deerskins. They were like the sun and fire, their selves made
by austerity, seeking release, ascetics with controlled senses, one with
brahman, blessed, speakers of brahman.

Wise Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, full of splendour, restrained and pure,
went with his brothers to those seers. The great seers, gifted with divine
knowledge, seeing Yudhishthira arrive, all came to him gladly, pronouncing
blessings, always given to their recitation. Like fire, they gladly paid him
honour as the rules require and brought him water, flowers, roots and fruit,
pure. The son of Dharma, Yudhishthira, took the honour the great seers offered
him with love, with a controlled mind. With Krishna and his brothers, and with
the brahmins who had mastered the Veda and its limbs, the unshakable Pandava
gladly entered that holy place. It was like heaven, like Shakra's palace,
fragrant with divine scents, lovely and full of beauty.

There the righteous one saw the place of Nara and Narayana, honoured by gods and
divine seers, adorned by the Bhagirathi. Coming to the divine jujube dripping
honey, where hosts of great seers go, the great ones lived there with the
brahmins. They looked at Mainaka, full of birds of every kind, and the golden
peak, and the auspicious lake Bindusaras. They looked at the Bhagirathi with its
fine fords and its cool, clear water, auspicious, its bed of jewels and coral.
It was adorned with trees and strewn with divine flowers, and it gladdened the
heart. So the great Pandavas took their pleasure there. There the heroes, bulls
among men, lived with the brahmins, offering water again and again to the gods
and the Fathers. And there the Pandavas, tigers among men, bright as immortals,
delighted in watching Krishna at her many pleasures.
