# Nala Returns

"When Damayanti heard this," said Brihadashva, "she was deep in grief.
Suspecting that he was Nala, she said to Keshini, 'Go again, Keshini, and test
Bahuka. Stay near him without speaking, and watch what he does. Whenever he does
anything, lovely one, watch closely how he goes about it. And do not give him
fire, lovely one, even if he insists. If he asks, do not give him water. Take
care, and do not be quick about it. When you have watched all this, tell me what
he does, and whatever else you may see, tell me that too.'

"When Damayanti said this, Keshini went quickly. She watched the signs of the
master of horses and came back. She told Damayanti everything as it had
happened, the signs, divine and human, that she had seen in Bahuka.

"'He is very pure in all he does,' said Keshini. 'I have never seen or heard of
such a man anywhere, Damayanti. When he comes to a low doorway, he never bends.
When they see him, the doorways rise up as they please, so that he does not
stick. Even in a narrow place a great space opens for him. The king sent meat of
many animals there to be cooked for Rituparna. A pot was set there for washing
it, and when he looked at the pot, it was full. Then, when he had washed it,
Bahuka set it on the fire. He took a handful of grass, waved it, and set it in
place, and the fire suddenly blazed up. I saw that great wonder and came here
amazed.

"'And I saw another great wonder in him. He touches the fire and is not burned,
lovely one. Water flows for him as he wishes, poured out quickly. And I saw
another very great wonder. He took some flowers and slowly crushed them in his
hands, and as his hands crushed them, those flowers became more fragrant and
fresher than before. I saw these things, like wonders, and came back quickly.'

"When Damayanti heard of the doings of the king of good name, she thought that
Nala had come, known by his deeds and ways. Suspecting that her husband Nala had
taken the form of Bahuka, she wept and spoke gently to Keshini. 'Go again,
lovely one, while Bahuka is not looking, and bring me some of the meat he has
cooked in the kitchen.'

"Keshini went, and while Bahuka was busy she took some of the meat, very hot,
and quickly, eager to please, gave it to Damayanti at once, joy of the Kurus.
She had often eaten meat cooked by Nala before. She tasted it and knew that the
cook was Nala, and she cried out in great sorrow. Deeply distressed, she washed
her face, and then, Bharata, she sent the two children with Keshini.

"Bahuka knew Indrasena and her brother. The king ran to them, embraced them and
took them on his lap. When Bahuka had his two children, like the children of
gods, he was overcome with sorrow and wept aloud. The Naishadha showed his
feelings again and again. Then he suddenly let the children go, and said to
Keshini, 'These two are very like my own two children, good woman. That was why
I suddenly wept when I saw them. If you keep coming here, people may suspect
some wrong. We are guests from another country. Go, good woman, and bless you.'

---

"When Keshini had seen all the feeling of the wise king of good name, she came
quickly and told Damayanti. Then Damayanti, in her pain, eager in her suspicion
that he was Nala, sent Keshini again to her mother. 'I have tested Bahuka many
times, suspecting he is Nala. My only doubt is his looks. I want to find out for
myself. Either let him be brought in, mother, or give me leave to go to him. Let
it be arranged with my father's knowledge, or without it.'

"When the princess of Vidarbha said this, the queen told Bhima her daughter's
wish, and the king gave his consent. With her father's leave and her mother's,
bull of the Bharatas, she had Nala brought to the place where she was living.
When Damayanti saw Nala like that, the lovely woman was filled with bitter
grief. Then, great king, Damayanti, dressed in red-brown cloth, her hair matted,
covered with dirt and grime, spoke to Bahuka.

"'Have you ever seen, Bahuka, a man who knew dharma, who left his wife asleep in
the forest and went away? Who but the king of good name, Nala, would go away and
leave his dear, blameless wife in the empty forest, stupefied with weariness?
What wrong had I done that lord of the earth, that he left me in the forest,
carried off by sleep? I chose him long ago, putting aside the gods themselves.
How could he leave me, devoted to him, loving him, the mother of his children?
He took my hand before the fire. I kept to the geese's words. He promised, "I
will support you." Where has that truth gone?'

"As Damayanti said all this, tamer of enemies, tears of grief ran from her eyes
in a flood. When Nala saw those tears flowing from her eyes, so very dark, red
at the corners, he said in his grief:

"'That I lost my kingdom, timid one, I did not do myself. Kali did it, and it
was Kali that made me leave you. But you, best of those who keep dharma, struck
him long ago with your curse, as you grieved for me in the forest, in misery,
when I had no clothes. Because of your curse, Kali lived in my body, burning.
Burned by your curse, he was always like something set in a fire. He has been
beaten by my resolve and my austerity, and now our sorrow must come to an end,
lovely one. The wicked one has let me go and left, and so I have come here for
your sake, you of the full hips. I have no other purpose. But how could a woman
ever leave a loving and devoted husband and choose another, as you mean to,
timid one? Messengers travel the whole earth at the king's command: "Bhima's
daughter, they say, will choose a second husband, as she pleases, one who suits
her." As soon as he heard it, Bhangasvari hurried here.'

"When Damayanti heard Nala's lament, she joined her hands, trembling and afraid,
and spoke.

"'You should not suspect me of any wrong, good one. For I chose you, lord of the
Nishadhas, putting aside the gods. To bring you here, brahmins went out in every
direction, singing my words in verses to the ten quarters. Then a learned
brahmin named Parnada found you, king, in Rituparna's house in the land of the
Kosalas. When he had carried my words well and brought back your answer, I
thought of this plan to bring you back, Naishadha. For no one in the world but
you, lord of the earth, can go a hundred leagues with horses in a single day,
lord of men.

"'I touch your feet, lord of the earth, as I say this: I have never done
anything wrong, even in my mind. The Wind that moves always through this world,
the witness of all beings, let him take my life if I have done wrong. The sun
with its fierce rays moves always above the world. Let him take my life if I
have done wrong. The moon moves within all beings like a witness. Let him take
my life if I have done wrong. These three gods uphold the three worlds. Let them
tell the truth, or let them cast me off today.'

"When she said this, the Wind spoke from the sky. 'She has done no wrong, Nala.
I tell you the truth. King, Damayanti has guarded her rich treasure of virtue
well. We have been her witnesses and her guards for three years. This matchless
plan was made by her for your sake. For no man here but you could go a hundred
leagues in a day. Bhima's daughter is worthy of you, lord of the earth, and you
of Bhima's daughter. You need have no doubt here. Be joined with your wife.'

"As the Wind said this, a rain of flowers fell, the drums of the gods sounded,
and a gentle breeze blew. When King Nala, tamer of enemies, saw that great
wonder, Bharata, he put aside his doubt of Damayanti. Then the lord of the earth
put on that spotless garment, remembered the king of snakes, and took back his
own body.

"When she saw her husband in his own form, blameless Bhima's daughter embraced
the king of good name and cried aloud. King Nala, shining as before, embraced
Bhima's daughter too, and his two children, and greeted them as was fitting.
Then the lovely-faced woman laid her face on his breast and sighed, the
long-eyed one, overwhelmed by that sorrow. Still covered with dirt, the woman of
the bright smile held that tiger among men for a long time, bathed in tears.

"Then the mother of the princess of Vidarbha told Bhima gladly, king, all that
had happened between Damayanti and Nala. The great king said, 'Tomorrow I will
see Nala, when he has bathed and rested well, together with Damayanti.' Then the
two of them spent the night together, talking of all their old wanderings in the
forest, glad, king. In the fourth year, joined again with his wife, his every
wish fulfilled, he found the highest joy. And Damayanti, finding her husband,
was greatly refreshed, like the earth with its crop half grown when it gets
water. So, reunited, her weariness gone, her fever eased, her heart swelling
with joy, her wish fulfilled, Bhima's daughter shone like the night when the
cool-rayed moon has risen.

---

"When that night had passed, King Nala, finely dressed, went with the princess
of Vidarbha in the morning to see the lord of the earth. Nala bowed humbly to
his father-in-law, and after him the lovely Damayanti greeted her father. Bhima
received him with great joy, like a son. The lord honoured him fittingly and
comforted him, and Damayanti, devoted to her husband, with him. King Nala
accepted that honour as the rules require and told him of his own service, as
was proper.

"Then there was a great sound of joy in the city, from the delighted people,
when they saw Nala come back like that. The city was decked with flags and
banners and garlands. The royal roads were watered, swept and strewn with
flowers. At every door of the citizens flowers were heaped, and all the temples
of the gods were worshipped.

"Rituparna too heard that Nala had been disguised as Bahuka and was reunited
with Damayanti, and the king was glad. King Nala sent for him and asked his
forgiveness, and he, wise and sensible, asked Nala's forgiveness with good
reasons. The honoured lord of the earth, amazed, congratulated the Naishadha.
'By good fortune you are reunited with your wife. I hope I did you no wrong,
Naishadha, while you lived unknown in my house, lord of the Nishadhas. If I did
anything I should not have done, knowingly or unknowingly, you should forgive
me.'

"'You did me no wrong, king, not the least,' said Nala. 'And if you had, I would
not be angry. I should forgive you. You were my friend before, and my kinsman,
king. From now on you should bring me still more affection. I lived happily with
you, with everything I wished for, more happily than in my own house, king. And
this knowledge of horses of yours is still with me. I want to hand it over, if
you think fit, king.'

"With these words the Naishadha gave the art to Rituparna, and he received it
with the rites the rules prescribe. Then King Bhangasvari, having received the
secret of horses, took another charioteer and went back to his own city. When
Rituparna had gone, King Nala, lord of the people, stayed in the city of Kundina
for a time, not very long.

---

"When he had stayed a month, son of Kunti, the Naishadha took leave of Bhima and
went from the city towards the Nishadhas with a small following. He had one
bright chariot, sixteen elephants, fifty horses and six hundred foot soldiers.
Making the earth shake, as it were, the great-hearted lord of the earth came
quickly, fierce with purpose, and entered at speed.

"Then Nala, son of Virasena, went to Pushkara and said, 'Let us play again. I
have won a great deal of wealth. Damayanti, and whatever other wealth I have
won, this is my stake, and your kingdom, Pushkara, is yours. I have made up my
mind: let the game go on again. With one throw, bless you, let us stake our
lives too. When a man has won another's kingdom or wealth and taken it, he must
give him a return stake. That is called the highest law of wealth. If you do not
want that game, let the game of war go on. Let there be peace for you or for me,
king, by single combat. This kingdom is to be enjoyed by the family and must be
won back any way it can, by whatever means; so the elders teach. Make up your
mind today to one of the two, Pushkara: the gambling board or war. Let a bow be
bent.'

"When the Naishadha said this, Pushkara, as if laughing, sure of his own
victory, answered the lord of the earth. 'Good luck that you have won wealth for
a return stake, Naishadha. Good luck that Damayanti's bad deeds are at an end.
Good luck that you are still alive, king, with your wife, killer of enemies.
Decked out with this wealth when I win it, the princess of Vidarbha will surely
wait on me, as the apsaras wait on Shakra in heaven. I always remember you and
wait for you, Naishadha. I take no pleasure in playing with men who are not my
friends. When I have won lovely, blameless Damayanti today, I shall have done
all I need. For she is always in my heart.'

"When he heard those words of his, all that wild babble, Nala was angry and
wanted to cut off his head with a sword. But, smiling, his eyes red with anger,
the king said to him, 'Let us play. Why do you talk? You can talk when you have
won.'

"Then the game between Pushkara and Nala went on. With one throw, bless you,
Nala beat him, with his store of jewels and his treasury, and his life too,
which he had staked. When he had beaten Pushkara, the king laughed and said,
'This whole kingdom is mine, untroubled, its thorns removed. You cannot even
look at the princess of Vidarbha, you outcast among kings. You have become her
slave, fool, with all your followers. It was not you who did the deed by which I
was beaten before. Kali did it, and you do not know it, fool. I will never lay
on you a fault that another did. Live as you please. I spare your life. And my
love for you is just as before, hero, no doubt of it. My brotherly feeling for
you will never go. Pushkara, you are my brother. Live a hundred years.'

"So Nala, true in his courage, comforted his brother, embraced him again and
again, and sent him to his own city. Comforted like this by the Naishadha,
Pushkara joined his hands, bowed to the king of good name, and answered him,
king. 'May your fame never fail. Live happily for ten thousand years, you who
give me my life and a place to live, king.' Honoured so by the king, Pushkara
stayed a month and then went gladly to his own city, surrounded by his own
people. He went with a great army and well-trained servants, king, shining in
body like the sun, bull among men. When the splendid king had sent Pushkara off,
rich and unharmed, he entered his city, finely decked, and the lord of the
Nishadhas comforted the people of the city.

---

"When the city was at peace and glad, and a great festival had begun, the king
brought Damayanti with a great army. Her father Bhima, too, of boundless spirit
and terrible courage, the killer of enemy heroes, sent Damayanti off with
honour. When the princess of Vidarbha had come with her children, King Nala
lived in joy, like the king of the gods in Nandana. So he became famous among
the kings of Jambudvipa, and the glorious king lived again in the kingdom he had
won back. He sacrificed with sacrifices of every kind, as the rules require,
with rich fees.

"So too, king of kings, you will soon sacrifice with your friends. Nala,
conqueror of enemy cities, came to such sorrow through dice, best of men, bull
of the Bharatas, with his wife. Nala met that great and terrible sorrow all
alone, lord of the earth, and yet he rose again. But you, Pandava, take your
pleasure in this great forest with your brothers and with Krishna, thinking only
of dharma. You are always attended by blessed brahmins, masters of the Veda and
its limbs, king. What is there to lament? This story too is called the destroyer
of Kali. One like you, lord of the people, can take comfort from hearing it.
Think always of how unstable a man's fortunes are, and take comfort in their
rise and fall. Do not grieve.

"Those who tell this great story of Nala, and those who hear it often, will not
be visited by misfortune. Wealth will come to them, and they will be blessed.
Whoever hears this ancient, ever excellent story will have sons, grandsons and
cattle, and will be first among men. He will be healthy and happy, no doubt of
it.

"And the fear you see, that someone skilled at dice will challenge you again,
king, I will destroy for you. I know the whole secret of the dice, you whose
courage is true. Take it, son of Kunti. I am pleased with you, and I tell you
so."

Then the king, glad at heart, said to Brihadashva, "Blessed one, I want to learn
the secret of the dice truly."

Then the great ascetic gave the secret of the dice to the great-hearted Pandava,
and when he had given it, he went to Ashvashiras to bathe.

When Brihadashva had gone, Yudhishthira, steady in his vows, heard news from
brahmins and ascetics who came together from here and there, from the fords and
the fine mountains. Partha Savyasachin, the wise one, was practising fierce
austerity, living on air. "So strong-armed Partha has taken up an austerity hard
to achieve. No one has ever been seen before so fierce in austerity as Partha
Dhananjaya, the ascetic, firm in his vows, a sage who goes alone, splendid, like
Dharma in bodily form."

When he heard that he was burning in austerity in the great forest, the Pandava,
son of Kunti, king, grieved for his dear brother Jaya. With his heart burning,
looking for comfort in the great forest, Yudhishthira questioned the brahmins of
many kinds of learning.

**Divergences.**

* **The merit of reciting.** Rejected passages (\*358–\*360) expand the merit of hearing the tale and
  add a boon from Kali. The CE has only 3.78.10–13: the tale "is called the destroyer of Kali", and
  those who tell or hear it will prosper.
* **Yudhishthira learns the dice.** In the CE Brihadashva teaches Yudhishthira the secret of the dice
  here, so that he need not fear another challenge (3.78.14–17). See L-123.

* 3.73.9: the doorways rising for him are given as Keshini tells them.
