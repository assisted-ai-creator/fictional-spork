# Bhima's Challenge

When he heard Yajnaseni's words, Bhimasena, who could never bear an insult, came
up to the king, sighing. He said to him angrily, "Take the path of the kingdom,
the path of dharma, fit for good men. What is the use of our living in a grove
of ascetics, robbed of dharma, desire and wealth? It was not by dharma, nor
straightness, nor strength that Duryodhana took our kingdom, but by trickery
with dice. Our kingdom was taken as a jackal, a weak eater of leavings, takes
the meat of strong lions. Why, king, do you torment yourself in these hardships,
covered with a scrap of dharma, giving up wealth, the source of dharma and
desire?

"Because we did as you wished, our kingdom was taken while we watched, a kingdom
Shakra himself could not have taken, guarded by the bearer of Gandiva. Our power
was taken while we lived, because of you, as bel fruit is taken from the maimed,
or cows from the lame. Because you are known to be devoted to dharma, Bharata,
we have come into this great disaster, thinking it would please you. We waste
our friends and gladden our enemies, holding ourselves to your rule, bull of the
Bharatas. That we did not kill these sons of Dhritarashtra then and there,
because we took your rule, is the wrong that burns us.

"Look at this life of yours, king, like the life of the beasts. Cowards live
like this, not the strong. Neither Krishna nor Bibhatsu, nor Abhimanyu nor the
Srinjayas, nor I, nor the two sons of Madri, delight in it. You are worn thin by
vows, always saying, 'Dharma, dharma.' I hope, king, you have not fallen out of
weariness into a eunuch's life. Wretched men, who cannot win fortune, cherish a
weariness that is fruitless and destroys everything. You have sight and
strength, and see the manhood in yourself. You are so set on kindness, king,
that you do not see the harm.

"These sons of Dhritarashtra think us, who are patient and able, merely weak.
That is the sorrow, not death in battle. If we are all killed fighting, straight
and never turning back, that is better, for we would win the worlds after death.
Or if we kill these, bull of the Bharatas, and take the whole earth, that too is
good for us. In every way this is what we must do, keeping our own dharma,
wanting great fame, wanting to pay back the enmity. When men fight for their own
cause, knowing what should be done, when their kingdom has been taken by others,
they are praised and not blamed.

"The dharma that wastes one's friends and oneself, king, is called disaster. It
is not dharma. It is bad dharma. Dharma and wealth, my son, abandon the man who
is always bent on dharma and weak in it, as pleasure and pain abandon the dead.
The man whose dharma is only for dharma's sake, and who suffers for it, is not
wise. He does not know the meaning of dharma, as a blind man does not know the
light of the sun. The man whose wealth is only for wealth's sake does not know
wealth either. He is like a hired man who guards a forest. The man who seeks
wealth beyond all bounds, and does not follow the other two, should be killed by
all creatures, loathed like the killer of a brahmin. The man who always seeks
desire, and does not follow the other two, loses his friends and falls from
dharma and wealth. When his desires come to an end, the man without dharma and
wealth is sure to die, like a fish enjoying itself as the water dries up.

"So the wise are never careless of dharma and wealth, for they are the source of
desire, as the fire-stick is of fire. Wealth always has its root in dharma, and
dharma is held by wealth. Know that each is the womb of the other, like the
cloud and the ocean. The pleasure that comes from touching things and wealth is
desire, a thought of the mind. It has no body. The man who seeks wealth, king,
comes to great dharma. The man who seeks desire comes to wealth. He does not
come to anything beyond desire. For desire does not lead to another desire. It
is only the fruit, as the wise man knows, as ashes come from wood when it is
used. As the fowler kills these birds, king, so is the form of adharma among
those who harm creatures. The evil-minded man who, out of desire and greed, does
not see how dharma works, should be killed by all creatures, here and after
death.

"You surely know, king, that wealth is the gathering of goods. You know its
nature, and how much it changes. Its loss or destruction, by old age or by
death, men think is harm, and that is what we have now. The pleasure that comes
from the five senses, the mind and the heart, when they are busy with their
objects, is desire, I think, the finest fruit of deeds. So a man should see
dharma, wealth and desire each on its own. He should not be set on dharma alone,
nor on wealth alone, nor on desire alone. He should always follow them all.
Dharma in the morning, wealth at midday, desire at the end of the day: so a man
should follow them day by day. This is the rule the teachings lay down. Desire
first, wealth in the middle, dharma at the end: so a man should follow them in
the course of his life. This is the rule the teachings lay down. The wise man,
who knows the times, should divide dharma, wealth and desire rightly, best of
speakers, and follow them all in their time.

"Release is the highest good, king, for those who want happiness. Or getting, by
using one's wits and the means. So let it be done quickly, king, or let the
getting be won. For life is misery for the man in between, like the sick man's.
You know dharma and have always practised it. Your friends, who know you, urge
you to action. Giving, sacrifice, honouring the good, keeping the Vedas, and
straightness: this is the highest dharma, king, fruitful here and after death.
It cannot be practised without wealth, king, tiger among men, even if all the
other virtues are there. The world has its root in dharma, king, and nothing is
higher than dharma. But dharma can only be practised with great wealth, king.
And wealth can never be won by begging or by cowardice, king, nor only by
thinking of dharma.

"Begging, by which the brahmin succeeds, is forbidden to you. Strive for wealth
by force, bull among men. Begging is not laid down for the kshatriya, nor the
living of the vaishya or the shudra. His dharma above all is strength, born of
his own body. The wise say that dharma is great. Take up the great. You should
not stand in the low. Wake up, king of kings. You know the eternal dharmas. You
were born to deeds of violence, which men fear. The fruit that comes from
protecting the people is not blamed for you. This is the eternal dharma the
Placer laid down for you, king. If you stray from it, Partha, you will be
laughed at in the world. For men are not praised for straying from their own
dharma.

"So make your heart a kshatriya's, give up this slack mind, take up courage, son
of Kunti, and carry the load like a draught ox. No king who was only righteous
ever won the earth, king, or prosperity, or fortune. A petty man wins a kingdom
by trickery, by giving his word to many mean and greedy men, as the porcupine
gets its food. The asuras, the elder brothers, rich in everything, were beaten
by the gods by trickery, bull of the Pandavas. So, lord of the earth, knowing
that everything belongs to the strong, kill your enemies, strong-armed one,
using the greatest trickery.

"No archer in battle is Arjuna's equal, and no man ever will be, or my equal
with the mace. A man fights with his spirit, king, even a very strong one. Not
with size, nor with eagerness. Stand in your spirit, Pandava. Spirit is the root
of wealth, and all else is false. What is not held fast is like the shade of a
tree in winter. A man who wants the greater wealth must give up wealth, as with
seed, son of Kunti. Do not doubt it. But where loss is equal to gain, and there
is no increase, no trade should be made. That is like the scratching of a
donkey. In the same way, lord of men, the man who gives up a little dharma wins
great dharma. He is held to be wise.

"The wise split an enemy who has allies from his allies. When his allies have
split and left him, he is weak, and they bring him under their power. A man
fights with his spirit, king, even a very strong one. He does not win over all
the people by effort, nor by sacrifices. Even a strong enemy can be killed by
weak men joined together, as bees kill the honey-thief. As the sun guards all
creatures with its rays, king, and also eats them up, so be like the sun.

"We have heard that this too is an ancient austerity, king, the lawful guarding
of the earth that our grandfathers did. 'Light may leave the sun, and beauty
leave the moon.' That is what the world has decided, seeing this sorrow of
yours. Gatherings meet, king, full of talk, praising you and blaming the other.
What is more, king, the brahmins and your elders meet here and gladly speak of
how true you are to your word. They say you have never told a lie, not from
folly, meanness or greed, not from fear, desire or for the sake of wealth.
Whatever wrong a king does in winning the earth, he drives it all away later
with sacrifices with rich fees. By giving brahmins villages and cows by the
thousand, king, he is freed from all his sins, as the moon from darkness.

"Almost all the people of the city and the country, joy of the Kurus, old and
young together, praise you, Yudhishthira. 'Like milk poured into a dog's skin,
like the Veda in a shudra, like truth in a thief, like strength in a woman, so
is the kingdom in Duryodhana.' This saying has long gone round the world,
Bharata, and even women and children repeat it like a lesson.

"So mount your chariot, with all its gear, and go out quickly. Have the best of
the twice-born speak blessings, and go today to the city named after the
elephant. It will bring us wealth for a long time. Be surrounded by your
brothers, masters of weapons, with strong bows, heroes like poisonous snakes, as
the killer of Vritra is surrounded by the Maruts. Crush your enemies with your
fire, as the killer of enemies crushed the asuras. Take the fortune from the
sons of Dhritarashtra, mighty son of Kunti. No mortal could bear the touch of
the arrows loosed from Gandiva, feathered with vultures' feathers, like
poisonous snakes. There is no hero, no elephant, no good horse, Bharata, that
could bear the force of my mace when I am angry in battle. With the Srinjayas
and the Kekayas, and with the bull of the Vrishnis, how could we fail to win the
kingdom in battle, son of Kunti?"

---

"No doubt, Bharata, what you say is true," said Yudhishthira, "as you wear me
down, piercing me with the darts of your words. I do not blame you for it. It is
hard, but this disaster came on you through my own bad policy. I took up the
dice wanting to take the kingdom and the country from the son of Dhritarashtra.
Then a cheat, a gambler, played against me for Suyodhana, the son of Subala.
Shakuni, the mountain man, a great master of tricks, always casting heaps of
dice in the hall, played by tricks against me, who had none. Then I saw the
evil, Bhimasena. If I had watched Shakuni's dice rightly, falling odd and even
as he wished, I could have held myself back. But anger kills a man's steadiness.
A man cannot hold himself back by his manhood, my son, when he is bound by pride
and courage. I do not find fault with your words, Bhimasena. I think it was
bound to be so.

"When the king, the son of Dhritarashtra, wanting the kingdom, threw us into
disaster, and brought us to slavery, Bhimasena, Draupadi became our refuge. You
know it, and so does Dhananjaya, what the son of Dhritarashtra said to me when
we came back to the hall for the second game, for a single stake, before the
Bharatas. 'Prince Ajatashatru, you will live in the forest for twelve years,
openly, as you please. Then for another year you must go unknown, with all your
brothers, hidden in disguise. If the spies of the Bharatas hear of you living
so, my son, you must live as many years again. Make up your mind to that,
Partha, and give your word. Suppose my spies do not find you in this time, king,
and you are careful and deceive my men. Then I tell you truly, here in the
assembly of the Kurus, Bharata, these five rivers are yours. And if you beat us,
we brothers will all give up our pleasures for the same time and live so.' So
the king said, long ago, among the Kurus, and I said to him, 'So be it.'

"So we played the last game, and were beaten, and all went into exile. And so we
wander through these lands, through hard forests, in our hardship. And
Suyodhana, not wanting peace, fell into the power of his anger again. He roused
all the Kurus, and all who had come under his power. When I have made that
agreement in the presence of good men, who could break it here for the sake of a
kingdom? To a noble man, I think, it would be worse than death to rule the earth
by overstepping dharma.

"At the time of the game you took hold of your iron club, wanting to burn my
arms, and Phalguna held you back. If you had done a hero's deed then, what harm
would have been done then, Bhima? Why did you not speak like this before the
agreement was made, knowing your own manhood? Now that the time has come, why do
you tell me this so much later?

"This hurts me more, Bhimasena. I burn, as if I had tasted poison, when I think
how, seeing Yajnaseni dragged about, it was borne, Bhima. But it is not possible
now, hero of the Bharatas, when I have given my word among the Kuru heroes. Wait
for the time when happiness will rise, as the man who sows seed waits for the
fruit to ripen. A man may first be tricked by trickery, and then know that his
enmity has flowered and borne fruit, and take back a great prize by his manhood.
Then the hero truly lives in the world of the living. He wins complete fortune
in the world, and I think his enemies bow to him. His friends follow him with
great love, and live on him as the gods live on Indra.

"And hear my promise, which is true. I choose dharma above immortality and above
life. Kingdom and sons, fame and wealth, all together are not worth a sixteenth
part of truth."

**Divergences.**

* **Shakuni's trickery.** In the CE Yudhishthira himself says Shakuni "played by tricks against
  me, who had none", and that if he had watched Shakuni's dice rightly he could have held himself
  back (3.35.3–4). He names no loaded or magic dice. See ledger L-116.
* **The terms of the exile.** Yudhishthira repeats the terms of the second game in his own words
  (3.35.7–11). Duryodhana's promise, "these five rivers are yours", is in this telling only.

  cows from the lame".

  his.
