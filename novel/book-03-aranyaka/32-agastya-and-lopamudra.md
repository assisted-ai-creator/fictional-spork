# Agastya and Lopamudra

Then the king, the son of Kunti, who gave rich fees, set out again. He came to
Agastya's hermitage and stayed at Durjaya. There the king, the best of speakers,
asked Lomasha, "Why did Agastya put an end to Vatapi here? What was the power of
that daitya who killed men? And why did great Agastya's anger rise?"

"Long ago, joy of the Kauravas," said Lomasha, "there was a daitya called Ilvala
in the city of Manimati, and Vatapi was his younger brother. That son of Diti
said to a brahmin rich in austerity, 'Blessed one, give me a son equal to
Indra.' The brahmin did not give him a son like Vasava, and the asura was
furious with that brahmin. Whoever had gone to the house of Vaivasvata, if
Ilvala called him by name, would take a body again and be seen alive. So he made
the asura Vatapi into a goat and cooked him well. He fed him to that brahmin,
and then he called him. The great asura Vatapi split the brahmin's side, king,
and came out laughing, lord of the people. So the wicked daitya Ilvala fed
brahmins again and again, king, and killed them.

"At that very time the blessed Agastya saw his ancestors hanging head down in a
pit. He asked the hanging men, 'What are you doing here?' 'For the sake of
offspring,' those who speak of brahman answered. They told him, 'We are your own
ancestors. We have come to this pit and hang here, wanting a descendant. If you
would beget a fine child, Agastya, we would be freed from this hell, and you
would reach the goal of those who have a son.'

"The splendid man, devoted to truth and dharma, said to them, 'I will do what
you wish, Fathers. Let the fever in your minds be gone.' Then the blessed seer
thought about offspring, but he could see no woman fit to bear his child. So he
took from each creature its finest part, and from those matching parts he made
the finest of women. The great ascetic sage gave the woman he had made for
himself to the king of Vidarbha, who was pining for a child.

"She was born there, fortunate, like lightning among the clouds, and grew up
shining in body, with a lovely face. When the lord of the earth, the king of
Vidarbha, saw her as soon as she was born, he told the brahmins in his joy,
Bharata. All the brahmins welcomed her, lord of the earth, and the brahmins
named her Lopamudra. She grew quickly, great king, keeping her perfect beauty,
like a lotus in the water, like a bright tongue of fire. When she came of age,
king of kings, a hundred maidens, well adorned, and a hundred maids waited on
the lovely girl, obedient to her. Surrounded by a hundred maids and in the midst
of a hundred maidens, the splendid girl shone like Rohini in the sky, lord.
Though she was grown and had good character and conduct, no man asked for her,
for fear of that great sage. The truthful girl, lovelier than the apsarases,
pleased her father and her kin by her character. When her father saw the
princess of Vidarbha grown up like that, he thought in his mind, 'To whom shall
I give my daughter?'

---

"When Agastya thought her fit for the life of a householder, he came to the lord
of the earth of Vidarbha and said, 'King, my mind is set on marriage, for the
sake of a son. I ask you, lord of the earth, give me Lopamudra.'

"When the sage said this, the lord of the earth was stunned. He could not
refuse, and he did not want to give her. Then the king went to his wife and
said, 'This great seer is mighty. If he is angry, he could burn us with the fire
of his curse.' When Lopamudra saw the lord of the earth and his wife so unhappy,
she went to them at the right moment and said, 'You must not suffer because of
me, lord of the earth. Give me to Agastya, father, and save yourself through
me.'

"At his daughter's word the king gave Lopamudra to great Agastya, as the rules
require, lord of the people. When Agastya had his wife, he said to Lopamudra,
'Put off these costly clothes and ornaments.' Then the long-eyed woman with
thighs like plantain stems put off her lovely, costly, fine clothes. She took
rags, bark and deerskins, and the long-eyed woman shared her husband's vows and
way of life.

"Then the blessed best of seers went to Gangadvara and took up fierce austerity
with his devoted wife. She served her husband with love and great respect, and
the lord Agastya loved his wife dearly. After a long time, lord of the people,
the blessed seer saw Lopamudra, bright with austerity, when she had bathed after
her season. Pleased with her service, her purity and self-control, her grace and
her beauty, he called her to lie with him.

"Then the lovely woman joined her hands, as if shy, and said to the blessed one,
lovingly, 'Beyond doubt, a husband takes a wife for the sake of children. But,
seer, you should give me the love I have for you. On a bed like the one I had in
my father's palace, brahmin, you should come to me here. I want you wearing
garlands and adorned with ornaments, and I want to come to you as I please,
adorned with divine ornaments.'

"'I have no wealth such as your father has, Lopamudra, lovely, slim-waisted
one,' said Agastya.

"'You are able, lord, by your austerity, to gather in a moment all the wealth
there is in the world of the living,' said Lopamudra.

"'It is as you say,' said Agastya. 'But that would waste my austerity. Show me
how to do it so that my austerity is not lost.'

"'Little time is left of my season, you who are rich in austerity,' said
Lopamudra. 'And I will not come to you in any other way. Nor do I want to break
your dharma, you who are rich in austerity. But you should do this for me, as I
wish.'

"'If this is the wish you have set your mind on, fortunate one,' said Agastya,
'well then, I will go, good lady. Stay here and do as you please.'

---

"Then, Kauravya, Agastya went to beg for wealth from King Shrutarvan, whom he
thought richer than other kings. When the king knew it, he came with his
ministers to the border of his kingdom to meet the pot-born sage and received
him with honour. The lord of the earth brought him the guest water as was
proper, and with joined hands and a controlled mind asked why he had come.

"'Know that I have come in search of wealth, lord of the earth,' said Agastya.
'Give me a share, as you can, without harming others.'

"Then the king showed him his income and his expenses, in full. 'Knowing this,
wise one, take whatever wealth you think fit.' When the twice-born man, whose
mind was even, saw that the income and the expenses were equal, he thought that
taking anything would in every way harm living creatures.

"So he took Shrutarvan and went to Vadhryashva. He too received the two of them
at the border of his kingdom as the rules require. Vadhryashva offered them
guest water and water for their feet, and with their leave asked at the start
what they wanted. 'Know, lord of the earth, that we two have come here wanting
wealth,' said Agastya. 'Give us a share, as you can, without harming others.'
Then the king showed them his income and his expenses, in full. 'Knowing this,
take what is left over.' When the twice-born man, whose mind was even, saw that
the income and expenses were equal, he thought that taking anything would in
every way harm living creatures.

"Then Agastya, Shrutarvan and King Vadhryashva went to Trasadasyu, son of
Purukutsa, who was very rich. Trasadasyu, the best of kings, of the line of
Ikshvaku, came to the border of his kingdom with his mounts, great king, and
received them all as the rules require. When he had honoured them as was proper
and they were rested, he asked at the start what they wanted. 'Know, lord of the
earth, that we have come here wanting wealth,' said Agastya. 'Give us a share,
as you can, without harming others.' Then the king showed them his income and
his expenses, in full. 'Knowing this, take what is left over.' When the
twice-born man, whose mind was even, saw that the income and expenses were
equal, he thought that taking anything would in every way harm living creatures.

"Then all the kings looked at each other and said to the great sage, great king,
'There is this danava, brahmin, Ilvala, rich on the earth. Let us all go to him
today and beg for wealth.' They all liked the idea of begging from Ilvala, and
so together, king, they went to Ilvala.

---

"When Ilvala knew that the kings had come with the great seer, he went with his
ministers to the border of his kingdom and honoured them. Then that best of
asuras entertained them, they say, Kauravya, with his brother Vatapi, cooked.
Then all the royal seers were dismayed and lost heart when they saw the great
asura Vatapi cooked, made into a ram. Then Agastya, the best of seers, said to
the royal seers, 'Do not despair. I will eat the great asura.'

"The great sage sat down in the seat of honour, and Ilvala, lord of the daityas,
served him, as if laughing. Agastya ate the whole of Vatapi. When he had eaten,
the asura Ilvala called his brother. Then wind came out of great Agastya, and
Ilvala was dismayed when he saw that the great asura had been digested.

"With joined hands, with his ministers, he said, 'Why have you come? Tell me,
what can I do for you?'

"Agastya laughed and answered Ilvala, 'We all know you as a lord, asura, a
master of wealth. These men are not very rich, and I need a great deal of
wealth. Give us a share, as you can, without harming others.'

"Then Ilvala greeted the seer and said, 'If you know what I mean to give, then I
will give you wealth.'

"'To each of the kings you mean to give ten thousand cows, asura, and as much
gold, great asura,' said Agastya. 'To me you mean to give twice that, and a
golden chariot, and two horses swift as thought, great asura. Let the chariot be
tested at once. It is plainly golden.'

"When it was tested, son of Kunti, the chariot was golden. Then the daitya,
shaken, gave even more wealth. The two horses yoked to that chariot, Vivaja and
Suvaja, quickly carried the wealth to Agastya's hermitage, and all the kings
with Agastya, as if in the blink of an eye, Bharata. With Agastya's leave the
royal seers went away then. And the sage did all that Lopamudra wished.

"'You have done all I longed for, blessed one,' said Lopamudra. 'Now give me a
child, a son of great strength.'

"'I am pleased with you, lovely one, with your conduct, good lady,' said
Agastya. 'But hear what I have to tell you about your child. Shall you have a
thousand sons, or a hundred equal to ten each, or ten equal to a hundred each,
or one equal to a thousand?'

"'Let me have one son equal to a thousand, you who are rich in austerity,' said
Lopamudra. 'For one learned and good son is better than many who are not good.'

"'So be it,' the sage promised, and in due time he lay with her, full of faith,
and she of equal character and full of faith. When he had given her the child,
he went away into the forest. While he was in the forest, the child grew in the
womb for seven autumns. When the seventh year had passed, the great poet came
out, blazing as it were with power, Dridhasyu by name, Bharata, the glorious
one, already reciting the Vedas with their limbs and the Upanishads. The seer's
son was splendid, a great seer. While still a boy, the splendid child carried
loads of firewood to his father's house, and so he became Idhmavaha, 'the fuel
carrier'. When the sage saw him like that, he was glad, and his ancestors
reached the worlds they wished for, king.

"Agastya's hermitage is famous, full of flowers in every season. So Vatapi, of
the line of Prahlada, was destroyed by Agastya. This is his hermitage, king,
lovely and full of virtues. Here is the holy Bhagirathi. Bathe in it as you
wish."

**Divergences.**

* **"Vatapi, be digested."** In the CE Agastya eats Vatapi, wind comes out of him, and Ilvala sees his
  brother digested (3.97.6–7). Agastya's famous words, and his reply that the digested asura cannot come
  out, are rejected passages (\*489, \*490). So is Ilvala's attack on Agastya and his burning to ash by
  Agastya's "hum" (\*493). In the CE Ilvala pays up. See L-126.
* **The goat and the ram.** Ilvala cooks Vatapi as a goat for the brahmin (3.94.8) and as a ram for the
  kings (3.97.3); both are kept.

**Choices.**

* 3.94.17: the making of Lopamudra from the finest part of each creature is as the text gives it; the name
  itself is not explained in the CE.
* 3.95.9 *rambhorūḥ*: "with thighs like plantain stems".
* 3.95.13 *snātām*: "when she had bathed after her season".
* 3.96.2 *kumbhayonim*: "the pot-born sage", Agastya.
* 3.96.5 *āyavyayau*: "his income and his expenses". The three visits are told three times, as in the text.
* 3.97.15 *vivājaś ca suvājaś ca*: the horses Vivaja and Suvaja.
