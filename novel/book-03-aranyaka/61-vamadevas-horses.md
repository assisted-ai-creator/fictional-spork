# Vamadeva's Horses

"Then, when he has destroyed the thieves," said Markandeya, "he will duly give
this earth to the brahmins at a horse sacrifice, a great sacrifice. When he has
set up the fine bounds laid down by the Self-born, he whose deeds bring holy
fame will grow old and go to the forest. The men who live in the world will
follow his conduct. When the brahmins have destroyed the thieves, there will be
peace. Setting up black deerskins, spears, tridents and weapons in the countries
he has conquered, praised by the lords of brahmins and honouring the best of the
twice-born, Kalki, that tiger among brahmins, will roam the earth, always given
to killing robbers. "Ah, father! Ah, son!" So, crying out these terrible words,
the robbers will be brought to destruction.

"Then adharma will be destroyed and dharma will grow, Bharata, when the Krita
age has come, and people will perform the rites. There will be gardens and
shrines, tanks and wells, and sacrifices of many kinds in the Krita age.
Brahmins, good men, sages and ascetics, the hermitages with the heretics, and
the people and creatures will stand in truth. All the seeds that are sown will
grow, and in every season, king of kings, every crop will come. Men will be
devoted to giving, to vows and to observances. Brahmins will be given to
recitation and sacrifice, loving dharma and full of joy. Kings will rule this
earth by dharma. In the Krita age vaishyas will be given to trade, brahmins to
their six duties and kshatriyas to protection. Shudras will be given to the
service of the three classes.

"This is dharma in the Krita, in the Treta and in the Dvapara, and in the last
age, as I have told you. The count of the ages is known to the whole world,
Pandava. I have told you all of it, past and to come, remembering the ancient
lore spoken by the Wind and praised by the seers. As one who lives long, I have
seen and lived through the ways of the world many times, and I have told you of
them. Now, unfallen one, with your brothers, hear something more from me, to
free you from your doubts about dharma. You must always yoke yourself to dharma,
best of those who uphold it. A righteous king is happy here and hereafter. And
hear the good words I will tell you, sinless one. You must never slight a
brahmin. A brahmin who is angered could destroy the worlds by his vow."

When he had heard Markandeya's words, the wise chief of the Kurus, of the
greatest splendour, spoke words of the highest sense. "In what dharma should I
stand, sage, in protecting my people? How should I live so as not to fall from
my own dharma?"

"Be merciful to all creatures," said Markandeya, "kind, loving, free of envy,
devoted to protecting your people as if they were your own children. Practise
dharma, give up adharma, and honour the Fathers and the gods. Whatever you have
done by carelessness, overcome it well by giving. Enough of pride. Always hold
yourself in check. When you have conquered the whole earth, be happy and glad.
This is the dharma I have told you, as it has been and as it will be. There is
nothing on earth, past or to come, that you do not know. So do not take this
hardship to heart, my son. This is Time, strong-armed one, even for all the
dwellers in heaven. Creatures are bewildered, my son, driven on by Time. And do
not doubt what I have told you, sinless one. If you doubt these words, your
dharma will be lost. You were born in the famous line of the Kurus, bull of the
Bharatas. Do all this in deed, in mind and in word."

"The words you have spoken, best of brahmins, are delightful to hear," said
Yudhishthira. "I will do as you command, with care, lord. I have no greed, lord
of brahmins, no fear and no envy. I will do all that you have told me, lord."

When they heard the words of the great Pandava, king, the Pandavas were
overjoyed, together with the bearer of the Sharnga bow. And when they had heard
that fine tale of wise Markandeya's, they were filled with wonder at his telling
of the ancient lore.

---

The Pandava said to Markandeya, "Tell me more of the greatness of brahmins."
Then Markandeya told this.

"A king named Parikshit, born in the line of the Ikshvakus at Ayodhya, went
hunting. As he followed a deer on a single horse, the deer led him far away.
Then on the road, tired and overcome by hunger and thirst, he saw a dark grove
somewhere and went into it. Then, seeing a very lovely lake in the middle of
that grove, he went into it, horse and all. When he was refreshed, he put lotus
stalks and fibres in front of his horse and lay down on the bank of the pool.
Lying there, he heard a sweet song. When he heard it he thought, 'I see no sign
of men here. Whose song is this?'

"Then he saw a girl of the greatest beauty, picking flowers and singing. She
came walking near the king. The king said to her, 'Whose are you, fortunate
one?' She answered, 'I am a maiden.' The king said to her, 'I want you.' Then
the girl said, 'You can have me on one condition, and not otherwise.' The king
asked her the condition. Then the girl said, 'You must never show me water.' The
king said, 'Very well,' and he lay with her and stayed with her.

"As the king sat there, his army came after him. Following his track step by
step, they surrounded the king and stood there. When he had rested, the king
went with her in a closed litter. Reaching his own city, he stayed in secret
with her, taking his pleasure, and saw nothing else. Then his chief minister
asked the women who waited near him, 'What is going on here?' The women said,
'We see something new. No water is brought in here.' Then the minister had a
forest made without water, with fine trees and many roots, flowers and fruits,
went to the king in private and said, 'This is a fine forest, and there is no
water in it. Take your pleasure there.'

"At his word the king went into that forest with his queen. Once, as he was
taking his pleasure with her in that lovely forest, tormented by hunger and
thirst and very tired, he saw a bower of atimukta creepers. Going in with his
beloved, the king saw a pool, finely made with a floor of plaster and full of
clear water. As soon as he saw it he stood on its bank with the queen. Then the
king said to the queen, 'Go down into the water of the pool.' When she heard his
words she went down into the pool and sank, and did not come up again. The king
searched for her and could not find her.

"Then he had the pool drained. Seeing a frog at the mouth of a hole, he was
angry and gave the order, 'Let all the frogs be killed. Whoever wants something
from me must come to me with dead frogs as gifts.' Then, as the terrible killing
of frogs went on, fear came over the frogs in every quarter. In fear they told
the king of the frogs what had happened. Then the king of the frogs, in the
dress of an ascetic, went to the king. He came up to him and said, 'King, do not
give way to anger. Be gracious. You should not kill the frogs, who have done no
wrong. Do not seek to kill the frogs, unfallen one. Hold back your anger. There
are two verses about this: "The wealth of men who do not understand wastes away.
Promise not to kill them. Once you have come to anger, you will let it go.
Enough of doing adharma. What use to you are frogs killed?"'

"To him, as he spoke like this, the king, overcome with grief for the one he
loved, said, 'I cannot forgive it. I will kill them. These wicked ones have
eaten my beloved. The frogs must be killed by me in every way. You should not
stand in my way, wise one.' When he heard these words, the frog, his senses and
mind shaken, said, 'Be gracious, king. I am the king of the frogs, named Ayu.
She is my daughter, named Sushobhana. This is her bad conduct. She has deceived
many kings before.'

"The king said to him, 'I want her. Let her be given to me.' Then her father
gave her to the king, and said to her, 'Serve this king.' And he said to his
daughter, 'Because you have deceived kings, your children will not be devoted to
brahmins, because of your falseness.' The king, having won her, his heart bound
by the pleasures of love, as if he had won the lordship of the three worlds,
bowed to the king of the frogs, honoured him and said in a voice broken with
tears of joy, 'I am favoured.' And the king of the frogs took leave of his
son-in-law and went as he had come.

---

"Then after some time three sons were born to the king by her: Shala, Dala and
Bala. Then in time their father made Shala, the eldest, king, and went to the
forest, his mind set on austerity.

"Once Shala went hunting. Finding a deer, he chased it in his chariot. He said
to his charioteer, 'Drive me fast.' At this the charioteer said to the king, 'Do
not keep on. You cannot catch this deer, not even if you had the two Vamyas
yoked to your chariot.' Then the king said to the charioteer, 'Tell me about the
Vamyas, or I will kill you.' At this, afraid of the king and afraid of
Vamadeva's curse, he told the king, 'The Vamyas are Vamadeva's two horses, swift
as thought.' When he said this, the king said to him, 'Go to Vamadeva's
hermitage.'

"He went to Vamadeva's hermitage and said to the seer, 'Blessed one, a deer I
have wounded is running away. I want to catch it. Please give me the Vamyas.'
The seer said to him, 'I will give you the Vamyas. When your work is done, give
them back to me quickly.' He took the horses, took leave of the seer and set off
after the deer in the chariot yoked with the Vamyas. As he went he said to the
charioteer, 'These two jewels of horses are not fit for brahmins. They are not
to be given back to Vamadeva.' When he had said this, he caught the deer, came
back to his city and kept the horses in the inner palace.

"Then the seer thought, 'The young prince has found a fine mount and is enjoying
himself. He does not give them back to me. Ah, how bad this is!' Having made up
his mind, when a month was up he said to his pupil, 'Go, Atreya. Tell the king,
"If you have had enough, give back your teacher's Vamyas."' He went and said
this to the king. The king answered him, 'This is a mount for kings. Brahmins do
not deserve such jewels. And what work have brahmins for horses? Go back, and
good luck.' He went and told this to his teacher.

"When he heard these unwelcome words, Vamadeva, filled with anger, went to the
king himself and pressed him for the horses, and the king would not give them.

"'Give me my Vamyas, king,' said Vamadeva. 'You have done with them what no
others could have done. Let not Varuna kill you with his terrible nooses,
standing between brahmin and kshatriya.'

"'Two oxen of good habits, well tamed: that is the right mount for brahmins,
Vamadeva,' said the king. 'Go with them wherever you wish, great seer. The Vedas
carry such as you.'

"'The Vedas carry such as me in the world beyond, king,' said Vamadeva,
'whatever there is there. But in this world, these are my mount, king, and the
mount of others like me.'

"'Let four donkeys carry you,' said the king, 'or the finest mules, or bay
horses. Go with them. This is a kshatriya's mount. Know that the Vamyas are
mine, not yours.'

"'They say it is a terrible vow of a brahmin,' said Vamadeva, 'this, king, that
you live here on what is his. Let four great iron ones, terrible in form, with
sharp spears, carry you off.'

"'Those who know you to be a brahmin, Vamadeva,' said the king, 'and would kill
you in word, in mind or in deed, let them strike you and your pupil down here,
driven by my word, with sharp spears and swords in their hands.'

"'Brahmins are not to be called to account, king,' said Vamadeva, 'in word, in
mind or in deed. The wise man who follows brahman by austerity becomes the best
while he lives.'

"When Vamadeva had said this, king," said Markandeya, "rakshasas of terrible
form rose up. As they struck him with spears in their hands, the king cried
aloud, 'If the Ikshvakus, brahmin, or Dala, or my other subjects obey me, I will
not let go of Vamadeva's Vamyas. Such men as these are not righteous.' Even as
he said this, the lord of the earth, struck down by the yatudhanas, fell to the
ground. Then the Ikshvakus, knowing that the king had been struck down,
consecrated Dala.

"Then Vamadeva, that brahmin, went to the kingdom and said, 'King Dala, what
belongs to brahmins must be given. So it is seen in all the dharmas, king. If
you fear adharma, lord of men, give me my Vamyas today, quickly.' When he heard
Vamadeva's words, the king said in anger to his charioteer, 'Bring me one bright
arrow, smeared with poison, from my store, so that Vamadeva, pierced by it, will
lie there, torn by dogs, in pain.'

"'I know your son, lord of men,' said Vamadeva, 'ten years old, born of your
queen, Shyenajit. Driven by my word, kill him quickly, your beloved son, with
your terrible arrows.'

"At these words of Vamadeva's, king," said Markandeya, "the arrow of fierce
splendour, once loosed, killed the prince in the inner palace. When Dala heard
of it, he said, 'Ikshvakus, I will do what pleases you. Today I will crush and
kill this brahmin. Bring another arrow of fierce splendour. See my courage
today, lords of the earth.'

"'That terrible arrow smeared with poison, which you are fitting to the bow
against me,' said Vamadeva, 'you will not be able to loose, or even to fit, lord
of men.'

"'Ikshvakus, see, I am held fast,' said the king. 'I cannot loose this arrow.
Nor can I bring myself to destroy him. Let Vamadeva live long.'

"'Touch your queen with the arrow,' said Vamadeva, 'and then you will be freed
from that sin.'

"Then the king did so," said Markandeya, "and the princess said to the sage,
'Vamadeva, as was right, day after day, lying beside him, I urged this on him,
seeking kind words for the brahmins. So may I win a holy world, brahmin.'

"'You have saved the royal house, lovely-eyed one,' said Vamadeva. 'Choose a
boon, and I will give you one beyond compare. Rule these people of yours,
princess, and the great kingdom of the Ikshvakus, blameless one.'

"'I choose one boon only, blessed one,' said the princess. 'Let my husband be
freed from his sin today. And look with favour on him, with his sons and kin.
That is the boon I choose, best of the twice-born.'

"When he heard the princess's words, the sage said, 'So be it,' hero of the
Kurus," said Markandeya. "Then the king was glad, and he bowed and gave him back
the Vamyas."

---

The seers and the Pandavas asked Markandeya, "Is there anyone who was born
longer ago than you?"

He said to them, "There is indeed. A royal seer named Indradyumna, whose merit
was used up, fell from heaven, and was told, 'Your fame has been cut off.' He
came to me and asked, 'Do you know me?' I said to him, 'We are not alchemists
who keep ourselves young. We pursue our aims by tormenting the body. But there
is an owl named Prakarakarna in the Himalaya. He might know you. The Himalaya is
a long way off, and he lives there.' Then he became a horse and carried me to
where the owl was.

"The royal seer asked him, 'Do you know me?' The owl thought for a moment and
said, 'I do not know you.' At this the royal seer Indradyumna said to the owl
again, 'Is there anyone who was born longer ago than you?' At this he said,
'There is the lake called Indradyumna's Lake. A heron named Nadijangha lives in
it. He was born longer ago than I. Ask him.' Then Indradyumna took me and the
owl and went to that lake where the heron named Nadijangha was. We asked him,
'Do you know King Indradyumna?' At this he thought for a moment and said, 'I do
not know King Indradyumna.' Then we asked him, 'Is there anyone else who was
born longer ago than you?' He told us, 'There is a tortoise named Akupara who
lives in this very lake. He was born longer ago than I. If by any chance he
knows this king, let us ask Akupara.'

"Then the heron told the tortoise Akupara, 'We want to ask you something. Please
come.' When he heard this, the tortoise came up out of the lake to where we
stood on its bank. When he came, we asked him, 'Do you know King Indradyumna?'
He thought for a moment. His eyes filled with tears, his heart was shaken, and
trembling and almost fainting, with joined hands he said, 'How could I not know
him? He placed me in the fire altars a thousand times long ago. And this lake
was made by the cows he gave as sacrificial gifts as they crossed over it. And
here I live.'

"As soon as they heard what the tortoise said, a chariot of the gods appeared
from the world of the gods, and voices were heard, saying to Indradyumna,
'Heaven is ready for you. Take your due place. You have fame. Go without care.'

"The sound of a holy deed touches heaven and earth. As long as that sound lasts,
so long a man is called a man. Whoever among creatures has ill fame told of him
in the world falls to the lowest worlds, as long as that sound is told. So a man
on earth should always keep good conduct, and give up the worst conduct and
cling to dharma alone.

"When he heard this, the king said, 'Wait a while, until I have brought these
two old ones back to their places.' He brought me and the owl Prakarakarna back
to our proper places, and then, perfected, went in that same chariot to his
proper place. This is what I lived through and saw, as one who lives long,"
Markandeya told the Pandavas.

The Pandavas were pleased and said, "Good! You did a fine thing in bringing King
Indradyumna, fallen from the world of heaven, back to his own place in heaven."

He said to them, "And Krishna too, the son of Devaki, lifted the royal seer
Nriga out of hell, where he was sinking, and brought him back to heaven."
