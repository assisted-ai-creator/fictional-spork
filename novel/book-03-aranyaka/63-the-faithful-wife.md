# The Faithful Wife

Then King Yudhishthira, best of the Bharatas, asked Markandeya of great
splendour a question of dharma very hard to answer. "Blessed one, I want to hear
from you, brahmin, of the highest greatness of women, and the subtle dharma,
told as it truly is. For the gods can be seen before our eyes, brahmin seer,
best of good men: the sun and moon, the wind, the earth and fire, blessed one,
and father and mother, and the cows, good man, and whatever else is laid down,
joy of the Bhrigus. I think of all of them as elders, and so too women who are
faithful to one husband.

"The service of faithful wives seems to me hard to do. Tell us of the greatness
of faithful wives, lord, who hold their senses in check, restrain their minds,
sinless one, and stay thinking of their husbands as gods. This seems hard to me,
blessed one, lord, the service women give to their mothers and fathers and to
their husbands, brahmin. I see nothing harder than the most exacting dharma of
women. What good women always do with devotion, brahmin, is hard indeed, and
fathers and mothers too do something hard. Women who are faithful to one husband
and speak the truth, who carry a child in the womb for ten months: what is more
wonderful than that? Going through the greatest danger and pain beyond measure,
women bear sons with great suffering, lord, and nourish them with great love,
best of the twice-born. And those who, though living among all that is cruel and
despised, always do their own work: I think that too is hard.

"Tell me truly, brahmin, the conduct of the dharma of kshatriyas. Dharma is very
hard for a cruel, evil-minded man to reach, brahmin. This is the question I want
to hear answered, blessed one, best of those who know questions, best of the
Bhrigu line. I am eager to hear you, you of good vows."

"Yes, I will tell you all of it," said Markandeya, "this question so hard to
answer, truly, best of the Bharatas. Listen as I speak. Some think the mother
equal to the fathers, my son, and some think otherwise. The mother who brings up
her children does something hard. By austerity, by worship of the gods and
sacrifice, by bowing down and by patience, by charms and other means, fathers
seek sons. Having won with great hardship a son so hard to win, they are always
thinking, hero, 'What will he become?' Father and mother, Bharata, hope from
their sons for fame and glory and lordship, for offspring and for dharma. The
one who makes their hope bear fruit knows dharma. The one with whom father and
mother are always pleased, king of kings, has fame and eternal dharma here and
hereafter. For a woman there is no sacrifice at all, no rite for the dead and no
fast. By service to her husband she wins heaven. On this subject, King
Yudhishthira, hear with attention the fixed dharma of faithful wives.

---

"There was a certain excellent brahmin, Bharata, a student of the Veda, rich in
austerity, an ascetic of righteous character named Kaushika. That best of the
twice-born studied the Vedas with their limbs and the Upanishads. Once he stood
reciting the Vedas at the foot of a tree. Up in the tree a female crane was
perched, and she let her droppings fall on the brahmin. Seeing her, the brahmin
was angry and wished her harm. Overcome with fury, he looked hard at the crane.
At the brahmin's evil wish she fell to the ground. When he saw the crane fallen,
lifeless and senseless, the brahmin was burned with pity and grieved for her. 'I
have done a wrong thing, forced by passion and hatred.' When he had said this
many times, the learned one went to a village to beg.

"Going round the pure households in the village, bull of the Bharatas, he
entered a house where he had begged before. As he asked, 'Give,' a woman said to
him, 'Wait.' While the lady of the house was cleaning the vessel, king, her
husband suddenly came in, very hungry, best of the Bharatas. When the good woman
saw her husband she left the brahmin and gave her husband water for his feet and
to sip, and a seat. The black-eyed woman waited on her husband humbly, with food
and dainties and very sweet words.

"She always ate what her husband left, Yudhishthira. She thought of her husband
as a god and followed his mind. Not in deed, not in mind, did she eat or drink
before him. With her whole heart given to him, she delighted in serving her
husband. Of good conduct, pure and skilled, seeking the good of her household,
she always did what was good for her husband too. She was always devoted to
serving the gods, guests and servants, and her mother-in-law and father-in-law,
with her senses ever controlled.

"As she served her husband, the lovely-eyed woman saw the brahmin standing there
waiting for alms, and remembered him. The good woman was ashamed then, best of
the Bharatas, and the glorious one took the alms and went out to the brahmin.

"'What is this, lady?' said the brahmin. 'You told me, "Wait," fine woman, and
kept me here, and did not let me go.'

"Seeing the brahmin burning with anger, as if blazing with splendour, lord of
men, the good woman spoke to him gently. 'Please forgive me, brahmin. My husband
is my great god. He came home hungry and tired, and I waited on him.'

"'Brahmins are not greater,' said the brahmin. 'You have made your husband
greater. Living by the dharma of a householder, you scorn brahmins. Even Indra
bows to them, let alone men on earth. You are proud and do not know. You have
not heard the old ones. Brahmins are like fire. They could burn the earth.'

"'I do not scorn brahmins,' said the woman, 'wise ones equal to the gods. Please
forgive me this fault, sinless brahmin. I know the splendour of brahmins and the
great power of the wise. It was in anger that they made the ocean undrinkable,
with salt water. So too with the sages of blazing austerity and perfected
selves, whose fire of anger is not quenched even today in the Dandaka forest.
For scorning brahmins the wicked, cruel great asura Vatapi met the seer Agastya
and was digested. Many great powers are told of those who speak of brahman. The
anger of the great ones is very great, brahmin, and so is their grace.

"'But in this offence, sinless brahmin, please forgive me. The dharma that lies
in serving my husband is what I love, brahmin. Of all the gods, my husband is my
highest god. I would practise my dharma towards him above all, best of the
twice-born. See what the fruit of serving a husband is, brahmin. You burned the
crane in anger. I know that.

"'Anger is an enemy that lives in men's bodies, best of the twice-born. The one
who gives up anger and delusion, the gods know as a brahmin. The one who speaks
the truth here and pleases his teacher, and does no harm when he is harmed, the
gods know as a brahmin. The one who has conquered his senses, is devoted to
dharma, given to study of the Veda, pure, with desire and anger in his power,
the gods know as a brahmin. The one who knows dharma, is wise, to whom the world
is like himself, and who delights in every dharma, the gods know as a brahmin.
The one who teaches or studies, who sacrifices or sacrifices for others, or
gives as he can, the gods know as a brahmin. The best of the twice-born who
lives as a student and studies the Veda, and is watchful in his study, the gods
know as a brahmin. One should tell of what is good in brahmins. The minds of
those who speak the truth do not delight in untruth.

"'They say a brahmin's wealth is study, self-control, straightness and the
mastery of the senses, forever, best of the twice-born. Those who know dharma
say that truth and straightness are the highest dharma. The eternal dharma is
hard to know, but it rests on truth. Dharma should have the Veda as its
authority: that is the teaching of the old. Dharma is seen in many ways, and it
is subtle, best of the twice-born. You too know dharma, and you are given to
study of the Veda and are pure. But I think you do not know dharma truly,
blessed one.

"'There is a hunter who lives in Mithila, who serves his mother and father,
speaks the truth and has conquered his senses. He will tell you of dharma. Go
there, and good fortune be yours, best of the twice-born, as you wish. Please
forgive me all I have said that went too far, blameless one. For all who know
dharma hold that women must not be harmed.'

"'I am pleased with you, and good fortune be yours,' said the brahmin. 'My anger
is gone, fine woman. The reproach you spoke is the highest good for me. May it
go well with you. I will go, and I will do it, fine woman.'

"Sent away by her, the brahmin Kaushika went out and went to his own house,
blaming himself, best of men."

**Divergences.**

* **"I am not a crane."** In the familiar telling the housewife says, "I am not a crane, to be burned by your angry look." That line is only in rejected passages
  (\*1001, \*1003). In the CE she says only, "You burned the crane in anger. I know that" (3.197.30). See L-143.
* **The hunter's name.** Rejected lines (\*1004, \*1005) name him the Dharmavyadha, "the righteous hunter". In the CE she calls him only "a hunter who lives in Mithila"
  (3.197.41).
* **Her plea.** A rejected passage (\*999) has her beg forgiveness for a woman's foolishness. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.196.11: the sense of the verse about those "living among all that is cruel" is uncertain; the novel keeps the words.
* 3.196.15 *mātaraṃ sadṛśīṃ … pitṝn anye ca manyate*: "Some think the mother equal to the fathers, and some think otherwise"; the sense is uncertain.
* 3.196.20: "For a woman there is no sacrifice at all, no rite for the dead and no fast. By service to her husband she wins heaven." Markandeya's words, kept.
* 3.197.3 *balākā*: "a female crane".
* 3.197.9 *sahasā*: "suddenly", of the husband's coming in.
* 3.197.12: "She always ate what her husband left", as the text says.
* 3.197.24–26: the housewife's examples: the salt sea, the Dandaka forest and Vatapi (3.97).
