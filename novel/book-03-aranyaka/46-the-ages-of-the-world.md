# The Ages of the World

At these words, mighty, strong-armed Bhimasena bowed down to his brother with
love and a glad heart, and spoke gently to Hanuman, lord of the monkeys. "There
is no one more fortunate than I, for I have seen my elder brother. It is a great
kindness to me, and seeing you has filled me with contentment. Now I want you to
do me a kindness, noble one. I want to see the matchless form you had when you
leapt the ocean, the home of sea monsters, hero. That will content me, and I
will believe what you say."

At these words the splendid monkey laughed and said, "You cannot see that form,
and no one else can either. The state of time was different then, and it is not
so now. Time is one thing in the Krita age, another in the Treta, and another in
the Dvapara. This is a time of ruin, and I do not have that form today. The
earth, the rivers, the trees, the mountains, the siddhas, the gods and the great
seers all follow time, as things are in each age. Strength, size and power wane
and grow again. So do not ask to see that form, upholder of the Kuru line. I
follow the age, for time cannot be overcome."

"Tell me the count of the ages," said Bhima, "and the conduct in each age, and
the ways of dharma, pleasure and wealth, and size and strength, and being and
not being."

"The age called Krita, my son," said Hanuman, "is the one in which dharma is
eternal. In that best of ages everything is done already, and nothing needs to
be done. Dharmas do not fail there, and creatures do not waste away. So in time
it came to be called Krita, the Done, for its virtue. In the Krita age, my son,
there were no gods, no danavas, no gandharvas, yakshas, rakshasas or snakes, and
there was no buying and selling. There were no classes of Sama, Yajus and Rig
verses, and there was no work of men's hands. Fruit came by thinking of it, and
dharma was renunciation alone. In that age there was no sickness and no failing
of the senses. There was no spite, no weeping, no pride, no slander. There was
no quarrel, and how could there be sloth? There was no hatred, no malice, no
fear, no grief, no envy and no jealousy. The highest brahman, the supreme goal
of yogis, was there, and Narayana, the self of all creatures, was white then.

"In the Krita age brahmins, kshatriyas, vaishyas and shudras had the marks of
the Krita, and creatures were given to their own work. The classes had the same
refuge, the same conduct, the same knowledge and understanding, and with the
same acts they won their dharmas. They were joined in one Veda, with one mantra,
one rule and one rite. Though their dharmas were separate, they had one Veda and
kept one dharma. By work joined to the four stages of life and suited to the
time, without attachment to desire or fruit, they reached the highest goal. This
dharma, marked by the Krita and joined to the union of the self, stands on four
feet in the Krita age, eternal, for the four classes. That is the age called
Krita, free of the three qualities.

"Learn about the Treta too, in which sacrifice begins. Dharma loses one foot,
and the Unfallen One turns red. Men are given to truth and devoted to the dharma
of rites. Then sacrifices begin, and dharmas, and rites of many kinds. In the
Treta men act with purpose, and their fruit comes from rites and gifts. They do
not stray from dharma. They are given to austerity and gifts. In the Treta age
people kept to their own dharma and performed the rites.

"In the Dvapara age dharma runs short by two parts. Vishnu turns yellow, and the
Veda becomes fourfold. Then some know four Vedas, others three, others two or
one, and others none of the Rig verses. So, with the scriptures divided, the
rites are carried many ways, and creatures, given to austerity and gifts, become
full of passion. Because the one Veda was not known, many Vedas were made. And
as truth falls away here, few stand in truth. Many diseases came to those who
fell from truth, and desires, and calamities sent by the gods. Tormented by
them, men practise hard austerities. Some, wanting pleasures or wanting heaven,
spread their sacrifices. So when the Dvapara comes, creatures waste away through
adharma.

"In the Kali age, son of Kunti, dharma stands on one foot. When the dark age
comes, Keshava turns black. The conduct of the Veda comes to rest, and so do
dharma, sacrifice and rites. Plagues, diseases and sloth prevail, and faults
such as anger, calamities, cares and sicknesses. As the ages turn, dharma turns
back again, and as dharma turns back, the world turns back. When the world
wastes away, the powers that drive the world waste away. The dharmas made by the
decay of the age turn prayers upside down. This is the age called Kali, which
will begin before long. Even those who live long follow the course of the age.

"As for your curiosity to know me, tamer of enemies: what interest has a man who
knows in things that are no use? I have told you all you asked of the count of
the ages, strong-armed one. Go well. Go on your way."

---

"I will not go without seeing your former form," said Bhima. "If I deserve your
kindness, show me yourself."

At these words the monkey smiled and showed the form he had when he leapt the
ocean. Wanting to please his brother, he took on a very great body, and his body
grew hugely in length and breadth. Of boundless splendour, he covered the
plantain grove and reached the height of the mountain, and there the monkey
stood. His great body towered like a second mountain. His eyes were coppery, his
teeth sharp, and his face was drawn into a frown. The monkey stood filling the
quarters, lashing his long tail.

When Bhima, joy of the Kauravas, saw his brother's great form, he was amazed and
thrilled again and again. He looked at him, like the sun in splendour, like a
golden mountain, like the sky ablaze, and Bhima shut his eyes.

Hanuman spoke to Bhimasena as if smiling. "This is as much of my form as you can
see here, sinless one. I can grow still bigger, as far as my mind wants. And
among enemies, Bhima, my body grows greatly with my power."

When the son of the Wind saw Hanuman's wonderful, most terrible body, like the
Vindhya or Mandara, he was bewildered. With his hair on end, Bhima joined his
hands, with an undaunted heart, and answered Hanuman as he stood there. "I have
seen the vast measure of your body, lord. Draw yourself in, of your own will,
you of great valour. I cannot look at you, like the risen sun, boundless,
unassailable, like the mountain Mainaka. And I am greatly amazed today, hero,
that with you at his side Rama went against Ravana himself. You alone could have
destroyed Lanka with its warriors and its mounts by the strength of your arms.
For nothing is out of your reach, son of the Wind. Ravana with his hosts was no
match in battle for you alone."

At these words of Bhima, Hanuman, bull among monkeys, answered in a warm, deep
voice. "It is as you say, strong-armed Bharata. That lowest of rakshasas was no
match for me, Bhimasena. But if I had killed Ravana, the thorn of the worlds,
Raghava's fame would have been lost, and so I let it be. That hero killed the
lord of the rakshasas with his hosts and brought Sita back to his own city, and
his fame was established in the world.

"Now go, wise one, devoted to your brother's good, on a safe and happy road,
guarded by the Wind. This is the road to the saugandhika forest, best of the
Kurus. You will see the garden of the Giver of Wealth, guarded by yakshas and
rakshasas. Do not pick the flowers yourself by force. A man should honour the
gods above all. The gods show favour to devotion, bull of the Bharatas, with
offerings, fire offerings, bows and mantras. Do not be rash, my son. Keep to
your own dharma. Standing in your own dharma, understand the highest dharma and
follow it. For without knowing dharma and waiting on the old, not even men like
Brihaspati can know dharma. Where adharma goes by the name of dharma, and dharma
is called adharma, it must be told apart with care. That is where fools are
lost.

"Dharma is born of conduct, and from dharma the Vedas arose. From the Vedas
sacrifices came, and by sacrifices the gods are established. The gods are
sustained by the sacrifices laid down in the rules of the Veda and of conduct.
Men are sustained by the policies taught by Brihaspati and Ushanas, and by
trade, wages and commerce, farming and the rearing of cattle. The whole world is
sustained by livelihood, by these dharmas, through the twice-born. The three
Vedas, livelihood and the rule of the rod are the three sciences of those who
know. When they are rightly used, the world goes on its way. If there were no
rites of dharma on earth, without the dharma of the three Vedas and without the
rule of the rod, this world would have no bounds. If they did not follow the
dharma of livelihood, these creatures would perish. When these three dharmas are
well followed, creatures thrive.

"Dharma is the nectar of the twice-born, and each class has its own. Sacrifice,
study and giving are held to be common to the three. Sacrificing for others,
teaching and receiving gifts belong to brahmins. Protecting is the kshatriya's
part, and the vaishya's dharma is to rear. Serving the twice-born is called the
shudra's dharma. They do without begging, fire offerings and vows, and live in
the houses of those they serve.

"Here, son of Kunti, your dharma as a kshatriya is to protect. Take up your own
dharma, disciplined and with your senses controlled. A king who takes counsel
with the old, with good men who are wise and learned, stands firm and rules by
the rod. One given to vice is brought low. When a king rightly uses restraint
and favour, the bounds of the world are well kept. So he must always learn
through spies about the country and the fortress, and the forces of enemies and
allies, where they stand, how they grow and how they waste away. A king has four
means: wise counsel, courage, restraint and favour, and the skill that carries
out the work. Tasks are achieved by conciliation, gifts, division, the rod and
by letting be, used singly or together. All policy is rooted in counsel, and so
are spies, bull of the Bharatas. Well-counselled policy brings success. Take
counsel with those who know.

"He should not take counsel on secret matters with a woman, a fool, a greedy
man, a child, a shallow man, or anyone who shows signs of madness. He should
take counsel with the learned, have his work done by the able, and his policies
laid out by those who love him, and keep fools away from everything. He should
put the righteous to tasks of dharma, the learned to tasks of wealth, eunuchs
over the women, and cruel men to cruel tasks. In every task he should know the
judgement of his own people and of others about what should and should not be
done, and the strength and weakness of his enemies. When men are well set by
understanding, he should take the good to himself. He should have the unruly and
the lawless restrained. When a king rightly uses restraint and favour, the
bounds of the world are well kept.

"This is the terrible dharma laid down for you, Partha, hard to follow. Keep it,
disciplined, according to the share of dharma that is yours. As brahmins go to
heaven by austerity, dharma, self-control and sacrifice, and vaishyas reach a
good end by the dharma of gifts, hospitality and rites, so kshatriyas go to
heaven by restraining and protecting on earth. Wielding the rod rightly, free of
desire and hatred, without greed and without anger, they reach the world of the
good."

---

Then the monkey drew in the vast body he had grown at will, and embraced
Bhimasena again with both arms. As soon as his brother embraced him, Bharata,
Bhima's weariness left him, and everything turned in his favour. Then the monkey
spoke again, his eyes full of tears, his voice thick with feeling, out of
affection for Bhima.

"Go to your own dwelling, hero. Remember me when you tell stories. And do not
tell anyone, best of the Kurus, that I am here. This is the place, and the time,
for the wives of the gods and gandharvas to come here, sent from the house of
the Giver of Wealth, mighty one. My eyes too have borne their fruit. Touching a
human body with you, Bhima, I have been reminded of Raghava. So let seeing me
not be for nothing, son of Kunti, hero. Think of me as your brother, and choose
a boon, Bharata. If I must go to the city named for the elephant and kill the
petty sons of Dhritarashtra, I will do it at once. Or if I must crush that city
with a rock, I will do it today, as you wish, mighty one."

Hearing the words of the great one, Bhimasena answered Hanuman with a glad
heart. "You have already done it all for me, bull among monkeys. Good fortune be
yours, strong-armed one. I ask your forgiveness. Be kind to me. All the Pandavas
have a protector in you, mighty one, and by your power alone we will conquer all
our enemies."

At these words Hanuman said to Bhimasena, "As your brother and your friend, I
will do what pleases you. When you plunge into the army of your enemies, thick
with arrows and spears, and roar your lion's roar, hero, mighty one, I will
swell your roar with my own voice. Standing on Vijaya's banner, I will let out
terrible roars that will take the lives of your enemies." With these words he
vanished.

When that best of monkeys had gone, Bhima, the best of the strong, went on by
that road over wide Gandhamadana. He went remembering Hanuman's body and his
splendour, matchless on earth, and remembering the greatness and power of the
son of Dasharatha. Longing for the saugandhika forest, he stirred up the lovely
woods and groves. He saw forests in flower, bright with open lotuses, and herds
of rutting elephants wet with mud, like masses of rain clouds, Bharata. Deer
with restless eyes, with their does, mouthfuls of grass in their mouths, saw the
glorious one on the road as he went quickly by. Bhimasena, fearless in his
courage, plunged into the mountain, the home of buffalo, boars and tigers.

In the forest the trees, shaken by the wind, their branches bowed with flowers,
soft with copper-red shoots, seemed to beg him to stay. On the way he passed
lotus pools with lovely fords and groves, visited by drunken bees, their lotuses
like joined hands. His mind and eyes caught on the flowering mountain slopes,
and with Draupadi's words for food on the road, Bhima went faster still.

As the day turned, in a forest where deer were scattered, he saw a wide river
with clear golden lotuses. It was full of drunken ducks and bright with
chakravaka birds, and it was like a garland of clear lotuses made for that
mountain. In that river the great-spirited one saw a great forest of saugandhika
lotuses, bright as the rising sun, a delight to see. When he saw it the son of
Pandu felt in his heart that he had what he wanted, and in his heart he went to
his beloved, worn by her life in the forest.

**Divergences.**

* **Bringing Duryodhana bound.** A rejected line (\*750) has Hanuman offer to bring Duryodhana bound to Bhima. In the CE he offers only to kill the
  sons of Dhritarashtra or crush the city with a rock (3.150.8–9). See L-135.
* **The tail.** A rejected passage (\*753) has Hanuman say he has placed his whole life-breath in his tail. Not narrated.
* **Rama as Vishnu.** A rejected line (\*748) praises Rama as Vishnu, the sun to Sita's lotus face. Not narrated. Hanuman does call Rama Vishnu in
  human form in the CE (3.147.28).
* **The four means.** A rejected line (\*744) says how each means is to be used on friends, brahmins, enemies and subjects. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.148.10–11 *kṛtam eva na kartavyam*: "everything is done already, and nothing needs to be done". *kṛta* means "done", which gives the age its
  name; the gloss "the Done" is given once.
* 3.148.13 *abhidhyāya phalam*: "Fruit came by thinking of it".
* 3.148.16, 23, 26, 33: Narayana is white in the Krita, the Unfallen One (*acyuta*) red in the Treta, Vishnu yellow in the Dvapara and Keshava black in
  the Kali. The names follow the text.
