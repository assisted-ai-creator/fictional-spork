# The Cattle Station

"So those best of men lived in the forest," said Janamejaya, "their bodies worn
by cold and heat, wind and sun. When they had come to that lake and that holy
forest, what did the Parthas do next?"

When the sons of Pandu had come to that lake, they sent their people away and
saw to their needs, and then they wandered through the lovely forests and
mountains and the country along the rivers. As the heroes lived in the forest
like this, ancient men who knew the Veda came to them, given to study and rich
in austerity, and those best of men honoured them.

Then one day a brahmin skilled in telling stories came among the Kauravas on
earth. When he had met them, as it happened, he went to King Vaichitravirya. He
sat down and was honoured in return by the old king, the best of the Kurus.
Urged by him, he told of the sons of Dharma, of the Wind and of Indra, and of
the twins. He told how they were thin, their bodies worn by wind and sun, fallen
into the mouth of fierce sorrow. He told of Krishna too, who had heroes for
protectors and yet seemed to have none, full of all the marks of hardship.

When he heard the brahmin's story, King Vaichitravirya was burned with pity,
hearing that the sons and grandsons of kings living in the forest had fallen
into a river of sorrow. His heart struck down with misery, choked with sighs and
tears, he somehow steadied his voice and spoke about the Parthas, thinking over
all that had come from himself.

"How does the eldest of my sons, the Dharma King, truthful, pure, of noble
conduct, Ajatashatru, lie on the ground, who used to sleep on heaps of ranku
deerskins? He is like Indra himself. Every day crowds of Magadha bards and sutas
used to wake him with their praise. Now he is surely woken at the end of the
night by flocks of birds, lying on the ground. How does Vrikodara, his body worn
by wind and sun, flooded with anger, lie on the earth in front of Krishna, his
body unused to it? So too Arjuna, tender and proud, under the command of the
Dharma King, with every limb in pain, surely does not sleep at night for rage.
Seeing the twins, Krishna, Yudhishthira and Bhima cut off from happiness, he
surely sighs like a snake of fierce venom and does not sleep at night for rage.
The twins too, unhappy, who deserve happiness, as handsome as immortals in
heaven, surely lie awake without peace, held back by dharma and truth.

"The Wind's son, equal to the Wind in strength and stronger, bound fast by the
noose of dharma, surely sighs and bears his rage. Rolling on the ground, wanting
to kill my sons, held back by truth and dharma, he waits for the time, greater
in battle than any other. The harsh words Duhshasana spoke when Ajatashatru was
beaten by a trick have entered Vrikodara's body. They burn in his vitals as fire
burns fuel. The son of Dharma will not think of evil, and Dhananjaya follows
him. But Bhima's anger grows with the forest life, like fire with the wind. Torn
by that anger, the hero presses hand on hand and breathes out hot and terrible
breaths, as if burning my sons and grandsons. The bearer of Gandiva and
Vrikodara, furious, like the Ender at the time of death, scattering arrows
bright as thunderbolts, would leave nothing of the enemy army in battle.
Duryodhana, Shakuni, the suta's son and foolish Duhshasana see the honey but not
the fall, not Vrikodara and Dhananjaya.

"When a man has done a deed, good or bad, he waits for its fruit, the doer, and
he is joined to that fruit whether he will or no. How can a man be freed from
it? When the field is well ploughed and the seed sown, and the god rains in due
season, if there is no fruit, how can it succeed, except by fate? That is what I
think. The one whose dice were in his mind did what was not good, and the
Pandava did what was good, and I followed my wicked son, and so this is the time
of the end of the Kurus. The wind will surely blow, even unbidden. The woman
with child will surely give birth. Surely at the start of day night is
destroyed, and at the start of night the day. Why is a thing done, and why would
others not do it? Men would never give away their wealth. And when the time of
gain has come, loss may follow. How could that be, and where would it come from?
It must be guarded, so that it does not break or leak or spill. Unguarded, it
would break into a hundred pieces. Surely no deed done in the world is lost.

"See the courage of Dhananjaya, who went even from the forest to Shakra's world.
He learned the four kinds of divine weapons and came back to this world. What
man, having gone to heaven in his own body, would want to come back, except one
who sees that many Kurus, struck by Time, are about to die? Savyasachin Arjuna
is the archer, and that bow Gandiva is the best in the world, and his are those
divine weapons. Who could bear the splendour of those three?"

When they heard the king's words, Duryodhana and the son of Subala went to Karna
in private and told him all of it, and the fool was not pleased either.

---

When Karna, with the son of Subala, had heard Dhritarashtra's words, he said
this to Duryodhana at the right time. "You have exiled the heroic Pandavas by
your own courage, Bharata. Enjoy this earth alone, as the slayer of Shambara
enjoys heaven. The kings of the east and the south, of the west and the north,
have all been made to pay you tribute, lord of men. The fortune that once blazed
as it served the Pandavas, king, you and your brothers have won today. Not long
ago, king, we saw that fortune blazing in Yudhishthira when he had gone to
Indraprastha, and we were thin with grief. You have taken it from King
Yudhishthira by the strength of your wits, strong-armed one, and now it is seen
blazing in you. So too all the kings, slayer of enemy heroes, stand under your
command, saying, 'What shall we do?' Today the whole earth is yours, king,
clothed by the ocean, the goddess with her mountains and forests, her villages,
towns and mines, with her many forests and lovely cities.

"Saluted by brahmins, king, honoured by kings, you shine by your manhood like
the rayed sun among the gods in heaven. Surrounded by the Kurus, king, you shine
like the king of the stars, like King Yama among the Rudras, like Vasava among
the Maruts. Let us see the Pandavas, living in the forest, stripped of fortune,
who did not respect your commands and were never afraid of you. For we hear,
great king, that the Pandavas are living by the Dvaitavana lake with the
forest-dwelling brahmins. Go there, great king, with the highest glory,
scorching the sons of Pandu with your splendour like the rayed sun. See the sons
of Pandu, king, fallen from the kingdom, stripped of fortune and without means,
while you stand in the kingdom, wrapped in fortune, with all your aims achieved.
Let the Pandavas look on you, of great family, set in great good fortune, as
they looked on Yayati, son of Nahusha.

"The fortune that friends and enemies see blazing in a man is the fortune that
counts, lord of the people. The man who stands on level ground and looks down on
his enemies in their hard places, like one on a mountain looking at those on the
plain, what greater happiness is there than that? A man finds no such joy in
winning sons or wealth, or even a kingdom, tiger among kings, as in seeing the
misfortune of his enemies. What happiness would he not have, who, with his aims
achieved, looked on Dhananjaya in the hermitage dressed in bark and deerskin?
Let your wives, well dressed, see Krishna unhappy, dressed in bark and deerskin,
and let her be weary of it again, and blame herself and her life, stripped of
wealth. Her distress in the middle of the hall cannot have been as great as it
will be when she sees your wives well adorned."

When they had said this to the king, Karna and Shakuni both fell silent at the
end of their words, Janamejaya.

---

When he heard Karna's words, King Duryodhana was glad, and then downcast again,
and he said, "What you say, Karna, is all in my mind already. But I shall not
get leave to go where the Pandavas are. King Dhritarashtra laments over those
heroes, and thinks the Pandavas greater than us because of their austerity and
yoga. Or the king might understand what we mean to do, and, guarding the future,
he would not give us leave. For there is no other business at Dvaitavana except
the destruction of my enemies living in the forest. You know what the steward
said to me and you and the son of Subala when the time of the dice had come.
Thinking over those words and all the other laments, I cannot decide whether to
go or not.

"It would be a great joy to me too to see Bhima and Phalguna suffering in the
forest with Krishna. I would not find such delight in winning the earth as in
seeing the sons of Pandu dressed in bark and deerskin. What could be greater,
Karna, than to see Drupada's daughter Draupadi in the forest in ochre clothes?
If the Dharma King and Bhimasena the Pandava saw me with the highest glory, my
life would have its fruit. But I see no way by which we could go to that forest,
and by which the king would give me leave to go. With the son of Subala and
Duhshasana, find some clever way by which we can go to that forest. I too will
make up my mind today whether to go or not, and I will go to the king early in
the morning. When I am sitting there with Bhishma, best of the Kurus, you and
the son of Subala must tell whatever way you have found. Then, hearing Bhishma
and the king on our going, I will make my resolve, and win over the
grandfather."

"So be it," they all said, and they went to their dwellings. When the night was
over, Karna came to the king. Laughing, he said to Duryodhana, "I have found a
way. Hear it, lord of the people. The cattle stations at Dvaitavana are all
waiting for you, lord of men. We will go on the pretext of the cattle count, no
doubt of it. It is always proper to go to the cattle count, lord of the people,
and so your father should give you leave, king."

As the two of them were talking about their decision on the cattle count, the
king of Gandhara, Shakuni, said, as if laughing, "I have found a way to go that
is free of trouble. The king will give us leave, and even urge us on. The cattle
stations at Dvaitavana are all waiting for you, lord of men. We will go on the
pretext of the cattle count, no doubt of it."

Then they all laughed and clapped hands with one another, and having decided on
it, they went to see the best of the Kurus.

---

Then they all saw Dhritarashtra, Janamejaya. They asked after the king's
welfare, and the king asked after theirs, Bharata. Then a cowherd named Samanga,
whom they had primed beforehand, told Dhritarashtra that the cattle were near.
Immediately after, Radheya and Shakuni said to Dhritarashtra, best of kings,
lord of men, "The cattle stations are now in lovely country, Kaurava. The time
for the count has come, and for branding the calves too. And this is the right
time for your son to hunt, king. You should give Duryodhana leave to go."

"Hunting is fine, my son," said Dhritarashtra, "and so is looking after the
cows. But I remember that one should not trust cowherds. And we hear that those
tigers among men are near there. So I do not give you leave to go there
yourselves. They were beaten by a trick, and they are worn thin in the great
forest. They are always given to austerity, Radheya, and they are able, great
chariot fighters. The Dharma King would not grow angry, but Bhimasena cannot
bear things, and Yajnasena's daughter is pure fire. You, full of pride and
folly, might do them some wrong, and then they would burn you up, for they have
the power of austerity. Or the heroes, armed, flooded with anger, their swords
bound on together, might burn you with the fire of their weapons. Or if you set
on them somehow because you are many, that would be most ignoble, and I think it
impossible. For strong-armed Dhananjaya has lived in Indra's world, won divine
weapons, and come back to the forest. Bibhatsu conquered the earth long ago
before he had won his weapons. Now that he has them, the great chariot fighter
might kill you, might he not? Or if you listen to me and are on your guard
there, you will live in fear, and trusting no one, you will be unhappy there. Or
some soldiers might do Yudhishthira wrong, and that thoughtless deed would bring
blame on you. So let trusted men go for the count. I do not like your going
there yourself, Bharata."

"The eldest Pandava knows dharma," said Shakuni, "and he promised in the
assembly that they must live twelve years in the forest, Bharata. All the
Pandavas follow him, walking in dharma, and Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, will not
be angry with us. We have a great longing to go hunting, and we want to do the
count, not to see the Pandavas. Nothing ignoble will be done there, and we will
not go where their dwelling is."

At these words of Shakuni, Dhritarashtra, lord of men, gave Duryodhana and his
counsellors leave, though not willingly. With leave given, the son of Gandhari,
best of the Bharatas, set out with Karna, surrounded by a great army. With him
went Duhshasana and the son of Subala, the gambler, his other brothers, and
thousands of women. As the strong-armed one set out to see the Dvaitavana lake,
all the townspeople followed him to that forest with their wives. There were
eight thousand chariots, thirty thousand elephants, many thousands of foot
soldiers and nine thousand horses, carts, shops and courtesans, merchants and
bards, and men given to hunting, in hundreds and thousands. As the king set out
there was a very great noise, like that of a mighty wind in the rains, lord of
the people. King Duryodhana camped a gavyuti away, some four miles, and then set
out with all his mounts towards the Dvaitavana lake.
