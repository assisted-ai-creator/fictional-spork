# The Sun's Gift

"So the Parthas were beaten at dice," said Janamejaya, "and enraged by the
wicked sons of Dhritarashtra and their ministers, who tricked them, best of the
twice-born. They were made to listen to harsh words that bred the worst enmity.
What did my forefathers the Kauravyas do then? When they had lost their power
and fallen suddenly into misery, how did the Parthas, whose splendour matched
Shakra's, pass their time in the forest? Who followed them, when they had come
to the worst of misfortune? What did the great men eat, how did they live, and
where did they stay? How did those great heroes, the killers of enemies, pass
twelve years in the forest, best of brahmins? And how did the princess, the
finest of all women, devoted to her husbands, fortunate and always truthful, who
had never deserved sorrow, bear the cruel life of the forest? Tell me all this
in full, rich in austerity. I want to hear what they did, those men of great
wealth and splendour, as you tell it, brahmin, for I am eager to know."

So the Parthas, beaten at dice and enraged by the wicked sons of Dhritarashtra
and their ministers, went out of the city named after the elephant. They left by
the gate of Vardhamanapura and set out northwards, armed, with Krishna.
Indrasena and the others, their servants, some fourteen of them, followed them
in swift chariots, bringing all the women.

When the townspeople learned that they were going, they were stricken with
grief. They met together and, putting aside their fear, blamed Bhishma, Vidura,
Drona and Gautama again and again. They said to one another, "Our whole family
is finished, and we are finished, and our homes. For wicked Duryodhana, guarded
by the son of Subala, and by Karna and Duhshasana, wants this kingdom. If this
wicked man, with wicked helpers, means to be king, there is no family left, no
good custom, no dharma. How can there be wealth, or happiness? Duryodhana hates
his elders. He has cast off good conduct and his friends. He is greedy for
wealth and proud, a low man, pitiless by nature. The whole earth is nothing,
where Duryodhana is king. Let us all go where the Pandavas are going. They are
kind and great-souled, they have conquered their senses and their enemies, they
are modest and famous, and devoted to dharma and good conduct."

So saying, they followed the Pandavas. When they had caught up with them they
all joined their hands and said to the sons of Kunti and Madri, "Where will you
go, bless you, and leave us to our sorrow? We will follow you, wherever you go.
We are deeply troubled to hear that you were beaten by unfair means, by enemies
who have no pity. You should not leave us behind. We are your loyal friends,
devoted to you, and we always delight in your good. Let us not all be ruined in
a kingdom ruled by a bad king.

"Hear us, bulls among men, as we speak of good and evil, and of how a man's
company rubs off on him for good or ill. As a perfume scents cloth, water,
sesame seeds and earth, when flowers are laid among them, so the qualities that
come from company rub off. Keeping company with fools is the source of the net
of delusion. Keeping company with good men is the source of dharma, day after
day. So a man should keep company with the wise and the old, with men of good
nature and austerity, and with the good who are bent on peace. A man should wait
on those in whom three things are pure, their birth, their learning and their
deeds, and sit with them, for that is weightier even than the teachings. Even if
we do nothing ourselves, we will gain merit among good men of holy ways, and we
would gain evil from serving the wicked. From seeing the wicked, from touching
them, from talking with them and sitting with them, men lose their good conduct
and dharma, and they do not prosper. A man's understanding sinks when he keeps
company with low men. With middling men it becomes middling, and with the best
it becomes the best.

"There are good qualities spoken of in the world, which come from dharma, desire
and wealth. They arise from the ways of the world and of the self, the Vedas
teach them and the cultured approve them. All of them are found in you, all
together and one by one. We want to live among men of virtue, for we long for
what is good."

"We are blessed," said Yudhishthira, "that the people, with the brahmins at
their head, bound to us by love and pity, speak of virtues in us that we do not
have. So I, with my brothers, ask this of you all, and you must not do
otherwise, out of your love and kindness for us. Bhishma, the grandfather, the
king, Vidura, my mother, and most of my friends are in the city named after the
elephant. They are overwhelmed with grief and pain. You must all take care of
them together, for our sake. You have come a long way. Go back now, by the love
you bear us. Turn your hearts kindly towards my own people, whom I leave with
you as a trust. This is what matters most to me, of all I have to do. It is what
I hold in my heart. If you do it well, that will content me, and it will be the
honour you do me."

When the Dharma King spoke to them like this, the people raised a terrible cry
of grief. "Alas, king!" they cried. Remembering Partha's virtues, in pain and
deep distress, they took leave of the Pandavas, and turned back against their
will.

When the townspeople had gone back, the Pandavas mounted their chariots and went
to the great banyan tree called Pramana on the bank of the Ganga. They reached
the banyan at the end of the day, and there the heroes passed the night. They
touched the pure water, and they passed that night on water alone, worn out with
grief. Some brahmins had followed them there out of love, some who kept the
sacred fires and some who did not, with their pupils and their kin. The king
shone in the midst of those men who spoke of brahman. When they had lit their
fires, in that lovely and terrible hour of dusk, there was talk, with the
chanting of the Veda first. The best of the brahmins, with voices as sweet as
swans', comforted the best of the Kurus, and passed the whole night with him.

---

When the night was over and dawn came, the brahmins who lived on alms stood
before the Pandavas, whose deeds are never strained, as they made ready to go
into the forest. Then King Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, spoke to them.

"We have been robbed of everything, of our kingdom and our fortune. In sorrow we
are going into the forest, to live on fruit, roots and game. The forest is full
of dangers, full of wild beasts and snakes. I think you will surely suffer
there. To make brahmins suffer would bring down even the gods, let alone me. Go
back from here, brahmins, wherever you please."

"We are ready to go the way you go, king," said the brahmins. "You should not
abandon us, who are devoted to you and see the true dharma. Even the gods take
pity on those devoted to them, above all on brahmins who keep to good conduct."

"I too have the greatest devotion to brahmins, always, twice-born ones," said
Yudhishthira. "But the loss of my helpers weighs me down. My brothers, who might
have brought me fruit, roots and game, are dazed by the pains of grief, by the
dragging of Draupadi and by the loss of the kingdom. They are in distress, and I
cannot load these burdens on them here."

"Do not let the worry of feeding us trouble your heart, king," said the
brahmins. "We will gather our own forest food and follow you. We will bring you
good fortune by our meditation and our prayers, and we will enjoy ourselves with
you in the forest with pleasant talk."

"That is so, no doubt," said Yudhishthira. "I would enjoy myself with brahmins.
But in my reduced state I see it as a reproach to myself. How can I watch all of
you, who do not deserve it, fetching your own food and suffering for your
devotion to me? Shame on the wicked sons of Dhritarashtra!"

With these words the king sat down on the ground in sorrow. Then a learned
brahmin named Shaunaka, who delighted in the inner self and was skilled in yoga
and Samkhya, said this to the king.

"Thousands of causes for grief and hundreds of causes for fear come every day to
the fool, but not to the wise man. Wise men like you do not cling to acts that
go against knowledge, that are full of faults and destroy what is good. The
eightfold understanding, they say, destroys all that is not good, and it is
joined to what is heard and remembered. It is established in you, king. Men like
you do not sink under pains of body or mind, when wealth is hard to come by, in
straits, or when their own people meet with disaster.

"Listen, and I will tell you the verses the great Janaka once sang, which steady
the self. The world is tormented by two kinds of pain, one from the mind and one
from the body. Hear the way to calm them, at length and in short.

"Pain of the body comes from four causes: illness, contact with what is
unpleasant, toil, and the loss of what is pleasant. Both kinds, sorrow of the
mind and illness of the body, are calmed by two means: by quick remedy, and by
never dwelling on them. So wise physicians first calm the mind of a patient,
with pleasant talk and with the pleasures they bring. For pain of the mind
torments the body, as a red-hot ball of iron heats the water in a pot. So a man
should calm the pain of the mind with knowledge, as water puts out a fire. When
the pain of the mind is calmed, the body's pain is calmed too.

"The root of the mind's pain is found to be attachment. Through attachment a
creature clings and comes to pain. Sorrows have their root in attachment, and
fears are born of attachment. Grief and joy, and toil too, all come from
attachment. From attachment came the craving of the senses, and craving for
their objects. Both do harm, and the first is held to be the worse. As a fire in
the hollow of a tree burns the whole tree down to the roots, so even a little of
the fault of passion destroys a man who seeks dharma. It is not the man who is
parted from things who has renounced them. It is the man who sees the fault in
being joined to them who turns from passion, free of hatred and owning nothing.
So a man should put away by knowledge his attachment to his own side, to his
friends, to his heap of wealth and to his own body. Attachment does not cling to
the foremost men, who are full of knowledge, who know the teachings and have
mastered themselves, any more than water clings to a lotus leaf.

"A man overcome by passion is dragged about by desire. Wanting is born in him,
and then craving follows. Craving is the most wicked thing of all, always
troubling men, full of adharma, terrible, bound up with evil. Fools find it hard
to give up. It does not grow old when the body grows old. It is a disease that
lasts till death. The man who gives up craving is happy. Craving has no
beginning and no end. It lives inside men's bodies, and once it has arisen it
destroys them, like a fire that is born without a womb. As firewood is destroyed
by the fire that comes out of itself, so a man who has not mastered himself is
destroyed by the greed born with him.

"Men of wealth are always afraid, of the king, of water, of fire, of thieves,
even of their own kin, as every living thing is afraid of death. As a piece of
meat is eaten in the air by birds, on the ground by beasts and in the water by
fish, so is a rich man eaten by everyone. For some men wealth becomes ruin. A
man who clings to the good that wealth brings does not find the good. So every
coming of wealth feeds the delusion of the mind. Meanness, pride and arrogance,
fear and anxiety: the wise know these as the pains that wealth brings to
embodied creatures. There is pain in earning wealth, in guarding it, in losing
it. There is pain in its loss and in its spending, and men are killed for the
sake of wealth. Wealth is hard to give up, and when it is guarded it brings no
happiness. It is won with pain. A man should not think of its loss.

"Fools are bent on discontent. The wise find contentment. There is no end to
thirst, and contentment is the highest happiness. So the wise see contentment as
the only wealth here. Youth, beauty, life, a heap of riches, power, living with
those one loves: none of these lasts, and the wise man does not crave them. So a
man should give up his hoards. Who would bear the trouble that comes from them?
No one with a hoard is ever seen free from trouble. So righteous men praise the
man who does not strive for wealth. It is better not to touch the mud at all
than to wash it off. So, Yudhishthira, you should not long for wealth. If you
want dharma, free yourself from the wish for wealth."

"I do not want wealth for the sake of enjoying it, brahmin," said Yudhishthira.
"I want it to support the brahmins, not out of greed. How can a man like me, who
lives the life of a householder, fail to support and protect those who follow
him? A householder must share with all creatures, and he must give to those who
do not cook for themselves. Grass for a seat, ground to sit on, water, and a
kind word, the fourth: these are never lacking in the houses of the good. A bed
must be given to the sick, a seat to the man who is standing and tired, water to
the thirsty and food to the hungry. A man should give his eyes, give his mind,
give kind words. He should rise and go to meet a guest, and honour him as is
proper. The sacred fire, the ox, kinsmen, guests and relations, sons, wives and
servants will burn a man up if they are not honoured.

"A man should not cook food for himself alone, or kill animals for nothing. He
should not eat himself what he has not first offered in the proper way. He
should scatter food on the ground for dogs, for those who cook dogs, and for
birds. This is called the offering to all the gods, and it is done morning and
evening. So a man should always eat what is left, and eat the food of the
immortals. What is left when the servants have eaten is called the leavings, and
what is left of the sacrifice is the food of the immortals. They say the highest
dharma belongs to the man who lives this way, as a householder. What do you
think, brahmin?"

"Alas, what a great pity, that this world is upside down," said Shaunaka, "so
that the good man is ashamed of what makes the wicked man glad. For the sake of
his member and his belly, the fool makes much of the leavings, overcome by
delusion and passion, a slave to the objects of the senses. Even when he
understands, a man is carried off by the senses, which carry everything off, as
a driver who has lost his wits is carried off by wild, bolting horses. When the
six senses meet their objects, the mind appears in them, born of past intention.
When a man's mind is driven towards the objects of the senses, eagerness arises
in him and he acts. Then, pierced by the arrows of the senses' objects, by
desire whose strength is intention, he falls into the fire of greed, as a moth
falls into the flame out of greed for the light. Then, lord of the people,
bewildered by pleasures and by food, he sinks into the mouth of the great
delusion and does not know himself. So he falls into the round of rebirth, into
one womb after another, turned like a wheel by ignorance, by deeds and by
craving. He wanders among all beings, from Brahma to a blade of grass, born
again and again, in the water, on the land and in the air.

"That is the way of the ignorant. Now hear from me the way of the wise, of men
who delight in dharma and the good, and delight in release. The Vedic word says,
'Do works, and give them up.' So a man should practise all these dharmas without
pride. Sacrifice, study, giving, austerity, truth, forgiveness, restraint and
freedom from greed: this is known as the eightfold path of dharma. The first
four of these lie on the road of the fathers. A man should do them because they
must be done, and not out of pride. The last four are the road of the gods,
always followed by the good. The man whose self is pure should walk this
eightfold path.

"Those who want to conquer the round of rebirth act in this way. They act with
right resolve, with right control of the senses, with right keeping of vows.
They act with right service of their teachers, with right eating, with right
study of the teachings, with right renouncing of works, and with right stilling
of the mind. Free of love and hate, the gods have gained their lordship in this
way. The Rudras, the Sadhyas, the Adityas, the Vasus and the two Ashvins sustain
these creatures by the lordship that yoga gives. So you too, son of Kunti,
should take up full calm, and seek perfection by austerity, and the perfection
of yoga, Bharata. You have gained the perfection that comes from your father and
mother, and from your deeds. Seek perfection by austerity, to support the
brahmins. For the perfected do whatever they wish, by their power. So take up
austerity, and do what your heart desires."

---

When Shaunaka had said this, Yudhishthira, the son of Kunti, went to his family
priest and said among his brothers, "These brahmins, masters of the Veda, are
following me as I set out. I cannot support them, for I am beset by many
sorrows. I cannot abandon them, and I have no means to give. What should I do
here? Tell me, my lord."

Dhaumya, the best of those who uphold dharma, thought for a moment, looking for
the way by dharma. Then he said to Yudhishthira, "Long ago, when creatures were
first made, they were sorely tormented by hunger. Then the sun took pity on
them, like a father. Going on his northern course, he drew up the fiery juices
with his rays, and turning back on his southern course he entered the earth.
When the earth had become a field, the lord of plants drew up the sun's heat
from the sky and with the rain brought forth the plants. The sun, having entered
the earth and been sprinkled with the moon's rays, gives birth to the plants,
pure, with their six tastes, and that is food for creatures on earth. So food,
which keeps creatures alive, is made of the sun. He is the father of all
creatures, so go to him for refuge. Great kings, purified by their birth and
their deeds, have saved all their people by taking up great austerity. Bhima,
Kartavirya, Vainya and Nahusha saved their people from disaster, set firm in
austerity, yoga and meditation. So you too, righteous one, purified by your
deeds, take up austerity by dharma, and support the twice-born, Bharata."

When Dhaumya had said this, words fit for the time, the Dharma King, pure in
himself, took up the highest austerity. He worshipped the sun with offerings of
flowers and other gifts. Then the righteous man took up yoga, living on air,
master of his senses. He touched the water of the Ganga, and stood there,
holding his breath.

"How did Yudhishthira, the king, the bull of the Kurus, worship the sun of
wonderful power for the brahmins' sake?" asked Janamejaya.

"Listen closely, king," said Vaishampayana, "be pure and attentive. Give me a
moment, king of kings, and I will tell you all of it. Listen, wise one, to the
holy hundred and eight names, as Dhaumya taught them to the great Partha."

Surya, Aryaman, Bhaga, Tvashtri, Pushan, Arka, Savitri and Ravi. The Rayed One,
the Unborn, Time, Death, the Placer, the Maker of Light. Earth, water and fire,
space and wind, the last resort. Soma, Brihaspati, Shukra, Budha and Angaraka.
Indra, Vivasvat, the Blazing-Rayed, the Pure, Shauri, Shanaishchara. Brahma,
Vishnu and Rudra, Skanda, Vaishravana and Yama. The fire of lightning, the fire
of the belly, the fire of the fuel, the lord of splendours. The One whose Banner
is Dharma, the Maker of the Veda, the Limb of the Veda, the Bearer of the Veda.
The Krita, the Treta, the Dvapara and the Kali, the refuge of all the immortals.
The kala, the kashtha and the muhurta, the fortnights, the months and the
seasons. The Maker of the Year, the Ashvattha tree, the Wheel of Time, the
Shining One. The Person, the Eternal, the Yogi, the Manifest and Unmanifest, the
Everlasting. The Overseer of the Worlds, the Overseer of Creatures, the Maker of
All, the Dispeller of Darkness. Varuna, the Ocean, the Ray, the Cloud, the Giver
of Life, the Slayer of Enemies. The Refuge of Beings, the Lord of Beings, the
One Whom All Beings Serve. The Jewel, the Golden One, the Source of Beings, the
Giver of Desires, the One Who Faces Every Way. Victory, the Vast, the Giver of
Boons, the Swift, the Sustainer of Breath. Dhanvantari, the Comet, the First
God, the Son of Aditi. The Twelve-Selved, the Lotus-Eyed, the Father, the
Mother, the Grandfather. The Door of Heaven, the Door of Birth, the Door of
Release, the Third Heaven. The Maker of Bodies, the Tranquil Self, the Self of
All, the One Who Faces All Ways. The Self of the Moving and the Unmoving, the
Subtle Self, the One whose body is friendship.

These are the hundred and eight holy names of the great sun, who is to be
praised. The great Shakra spoke them. From Shakra they came to Narada, and after
him to Dhaumya. Yudhishthira received them from Dhaumya, and he gained all he
wished for.

*He is served by the hosts of the gods, the fathers and the yakshas, praised by
the asuras, the night-walkers and the siddhas. His light is like fine gold and
like fire. Hold the sun, the maker of light, in your mind as well.*

*The man who recites this at sunrise, with a still mind, will win sons, and
heaps of wealth and jewels. He will always remember his past births, and win the
highest memory and wisdom.*

*The man who recites this praise of the best of gods, pure and glad and with a
still mind, is freed from the ocean of the forest fire of grief, and gains
whatever his heart desires.*

---

Then the sun, well pleased, showed himself to the Pandava, blazing in his own
form like a fire. "You will gain all that you wish for, king," he said. "I will
give you food for seven years and five. Fruit, roots, game and greens, all four
kinds of food that are prepared in your kitchen, will not run out for you, and
wealth of every kind as well." So saying, he vanished.

When he had received the boon, the son of Kunti, who knew dharma, came up out of
the water. He clasped Dhaumya's feet, and the unfallen one embraced his
brothers. The lord went to Draupadi, and while she watched, the Pandava cooked
food in the kitchen. The forest food of the four kinds, once cooked, multiplied,
and grew, and did not run out. With it he fed the twice-born. When the brahmins
had eaten, and he had fed his younger brothers too, Yudhishthira ate what was
left, which is called the leavings. When she had fed Yudhishthira, Parshati ate
what remained.

So, having won from the sun all he wished for, the lord, as bright as the sun,
gave the brahmins what they desired. With the family priest at their head, the
rites of sacrifice went on, on the proper days, under the proper stars and at
the changes of the moon, as the rules and sacred words require. Then, when
Dhaumya had performed the rites for a safe journey, the Pandavas, surrounded by
crowds of twice-born, set out with him for the Kamyaka forest.

**Divergences.**

* **No copper pot.** In the CE the sun promises that the four kinds of food cooked in their
  kitchen will not run out for twelve years (3.4.2–3). He gives no vessel. The copper pot that
  feeds everyone "until the princess of Panchala has eaten" is \*21, a rejected line. In the CE
  Draupadi simply eats last (3.4.7). See ledger L-23.
* **Yudhishthira's hymn.** The vulgate gives Yudhishthira a long hymn of his own to the sun
  (App. I No. 1), and has Dhaumya teach him an eight-syllable mantra (\*15). Not in the CE, where
  Dhaumya teaches him the hundred and eight names.
* The vulgate adds the sun's promise that he will regain his kingdom in the fourteenth year
  (\*22). Not in the CE.
