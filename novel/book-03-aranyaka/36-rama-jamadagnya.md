# Rama Jamadagnya

Then the Pandava set out from the Kaushiki, Janamejaya, and went in order to all
the holy places. Coming to the sea where the Ganga meets it, king, he bathed in
the midst of five hundred rivers. Then the hero, the lord of the earth, went
with his brothers along the shore of the sea towards the Kalingas, Bharata.

"These are the Kalingas, son of Kunti," said Lomasha, "where the river Vaitarani
is, where even Dharma sacrificed, coming to the gods for refuge. This northern
bank is always frequented by brahmins, fit for sacrifice, adorned with a
mountain, and used by the seers, who went to heaven by the even road of the
gods. Here too other seers sacrificed long ago. Here, king of kings, Rudra
seized the sacrificial beast at a sacrifice. 'This is my share,' Rudra said,
lord of men. When the beast was taken, bull of the Bharatas, the gods said to
him, 'Do not seize what belongs to others. Do not swallow up all the rites.'
Then they praised Rudra with lovely words, satisfied him with an offering, and
honoured him. Then he let the beast go and went by the road of the gods. There
is a verse here about Rudra. Hear it, Yudhishthira. 'Out of fear of Rudra the
gods set aside for him for ever the finest share, fresh, above all other
shares.' The man who touches the water here singing this verse, for him the road
of the gods, and his eye, grow bright."

Then all the Pandavas and Draupadi went down into the Vaitarani, blessed ones,
and offered water to the Fathers.

"Only by touching the water of this river, blessed one, you who are rich in
austerity," said Yudhishthira, "I have gone beyond the world of men, see,
Lomasha. I see all the worlds, by your grace, you of good vows. That is the
sound of the great Vaikhanasas at their prayers."

"That sound you hear, Yudhishthira, is three hundred thousand leagues away,"
said Lomasha. "Be silent, lord of the people. This lovely forest that shines
here is Svayambhu's, king, where mighty Vishvakarman sacrificed, son of Kunti.
At that sacrifice Svayambhu gave the earth, with its mountains and woodlands, as
the fee to great Kashyapa. As soon as she was given, son of Kunti, the earth
sank, and in her anger she said to the lord of the world, 'Blessed one, you must
not give me to any mortal. This gift of yours is empty. I will go to Rasatala.'
When the blessed seer Kashyapa saw her sinking, he calmed the earth, lord of the
people. Pleased by his austerity, Pandava, the earth came up again out of the
water and stood there, shining, in the form of an altar. This is that altar,
king, shining, marked by its shape. Climb it, great king, and you will be
mighty. And I will say the blessing for your crossing, so that you may climb it
today. For when a mortal touches this altar, it goes into the sea, Ajamidha. Say
these true words, Pandava, and climb the altar quickly: 'Agni, Mitra, the womb,
the waters and the goddesses, you are Vishnu's seed and the navel of the
deathless.'"

Then, when the blessing had been said, great Yudhishthira went to the sea. He
did all that Lomasha told him, came to Mahendra, and spent the night there.

---

The lord of the earth stayed there that one night, and with his brothers he paid
the highest honour to the ascetics. Lomasha told him the names of all the
ascetics there, Bhrigus, Angirases, Vasishthas and Kashyapas. The royal seer met
them, greeted them with joined hands, and asked the hero Akritavrana, Rama's
follower, "When will the blessed Rama show himself to the ascetics? I want to
see the Bhargava in the same way."

"Your coming is already known to Rama, who knows himself," said Akritavrana. "He
is fond of you, and he will show himself to you soon. The ascetics see Rama on
the fourteenth and the eighth day of the fortnight. When this night has passed,
it will be the fourteenth."

"You follow the hero Jamadagnya, the mighty one," said Yudhishthira. "You have
seen with your own eyes all he did in the past. So tell me how Rama conquered
all the kshatriyas in battle, and how, and why."

"In Kanyakubja there was a great king, very mighty, famous in the world as
Gadhi," said Akritavrana. "He went to live in the forest. While he lived in the
forest a daughter was born to him, like an apsaras, and Richika Bhargava asked
for her, Bharata. The king said to that brahmin of strict vows, 'There is a
custom in our family, set by our forefathers. Know that the bride price is a
thousand swift white horses, each with one black ear, best of the twice-born.
But I cannot say to you, blessed Bhargava, "Give it." And my daughter must be
given to a great man like you.'

"'I will give you a thousand swift white horses, each with one black ear,' said
Richika. 'Let your daughter be my wife.'

"He promised this, king, and said to Varuna, 'Give me a thousand swift white
horses, each with one black ear, for a bride price.' Varuna gave him a thousand
horses then. The place where those horses rose up is known as Ashvatirtha. At
Kanyakubja on the Ganga, Gadhi gave him his daughter Satyavati, and the gods
were the bride's party, when they had seen the thousand horses he had won. When
he had won his wife by dharma, Richika, the best of the twice-born, took his
pleasure with the slim-waisted woman as he wished.

"When the marriage was done, king, Bhrigu, the best of the Bhrigus, came to see
his son and his wife, and was glad when he saw his son. The husband and wife
honoured him, their elder, whom the hosts of gods honour, as he sat, and stood
by him with joined hands. Then the blessed Bhrigu, glad, said to his
daughter-in-law, 'Choose a boon, fortunate one. I will give you what you wish.'
She asked her elder's favour for a son, for herself and for her mother, and he
granted it.

"'At your season, when you and your mother have bathed for the rite of a son,'
said Bhrigu, 'you must each embrace a tree, she a fig tree and you an udumbara.'
But when they embraced them, king, they did it the wrong way round. One day
Bhrigu came and knew they had done it the wrong way round. Then the splendid
Bhrigu said to his daughter-in-law Satyavati, 'Your son will be a brahmin who
lives like a kshatriya. Your mother's son will be a great kshatriya who behaves
like a brahmin, mighty, keeping to the way of the good.' Then she begged her
father-in-law again and again, 'Let my son not be like that. Let my grandson be
so, if it must be.' 'So be it,' he said, and she was glad, Pandava. When her
time came, she bore a son, Jamadagni, the joy of the Bhargavas, full of
splendour and energy. As he grew, the splendid boy surpassed many seers in
learning the Veda, Pandava. The whole science of the bow came to him of itself,
bull of the Bharatas, and the four kinds of weapons, and he had the splendour of
the sun.

---

"Jamadagni, of great austerity, given to learning the Veda, practised austerity
and brought the gods under his power by his discipline. He went to King
Prasenajit, king, and asked for Renuka, and the king gave her to him. When the
joy of the Bhargavas had Renuka for his wife, he lived in his hermitage with her
and practised austerity, and she was devoted to him. She bore him four sons, and
Rama was the fifth. He was the youngest of them all, but not the least.

"One day, when all the sons had gone out to gather fruit, Renuka, firm in her
vows, went to bathe. As she came along, by chance, king, Renuka saw a king named
Chitraratha of Marttikavata, garlanded with lotuses, playing in the water with
his wife. Seeing how splendid he was, Renuka longed for him. From that straying
she came back into the hermitage frightened, out of her mind, wet in the water,
and her husband understood. When he saw that she had fallen from steadiness and
had lost the glow of brahman, the splendid, mighty man cried shame on her.

"Then the eldest son of Jamadagni, named Rumanvant, came, and Sushena, Vasu and
Vishvavasu. The blessed one urged them one after another to kill their mother.
They were bewildered and out of their minds and said nothing. Then in anger he
cursed them, and they lost their minds and quickly became dull as beasts and
birds. Then Rama came to the hermitage last, the killer of enemy heroes.
Jamadagni, of great austerity, said to him in his great anger, 'Kill this wicked
mother of yours, and do not grieve, my son.' Then Rama took up his axe and cut
off his mother's head.

"Then, great king, the anger of great Jamadagni suddenly left him, and he said,
pleased, 'My son, at my word you have done a hard thing. Choose whatever wishes
your heart longs for, you who know dharma.' He chose that his mother should rise
again and not remember being killed, and that he should not be touched by that
sin. He chose that his brothers should return to their natural state, that he
should have no rival in battle, and long life, Bharata. And Jamadagni of great
austerity gave him all those wishes.

"Once, when his sons had gone out in the same way, lord, the hero Kartavirya,
lord of Anupa, came. When he reached the hermitage, the seer's wife honoured
him. Drunk with the madness of battle, he did not welcome that honour. By force
he carried off from the hermitage the calf of the cow for the offerings as she
cried out, and broke down the great trees. When Rama came, his father told him
himself. When Rama saw the cow lowing, anger took him. In the grip of fury he
ran at Kartavirya. Then, pressing forward in battle, the Bhargava, killer of
enemy heroes, took up his lovely bow and cut off with sharp arrows his arms,
thick as iron bars, a thousand of them, king.

"Then Arjuna's heirs, enraged with Rama, attacked Jamadagni in the hermitage
while Rama was away. They killed the mighty ascetic as he did not fight, crying
out again and again, 'Rama! Rama!', like a man with no protector. When they had
killed Jamadagni with arrows, Yudhishthira, the sons of Kartavirya went back as
they had come, tamers of enemies. When they had gone and Jamadagni lay like
that, the joy of the Bhrigus came to the hermitage with firewood in his hand.
When the hero saw his father fallen into the power of death, who had not
deserved it, he lamented in great sorrow.

"'It was my fault, father,' said Rama. 'Those low fools, the heirs of
Kartavirya, have killed you with arrows like a deer in the forest. How could
such a death be right for you, father, who knew dharma, who walked the good
road, and did no wrong to any creature? What evil have they not done, who killed
you with hundreds of sharp arrows, an old man standing in austerity, who did not
fight? What will those shameless men say to their ministers and friends, when
they have killed a man who knew dharma, alone, who did not fight?'

"When he had lamented like this pitifully and in many ways, king, the great
ascetic performed all the rites for the dead for his father. Rama, conqueror of
enemy cities, burned his father in the fire, and vowed to kill all the
kshatriyas, Bharata. Enraged, very mighty, brave, taking up his weapon, alone,
like Death, the mighty one killed the sons of Kartavirya. And all the kshatriyas
who followed them, bull among kshatriyas, Rama, the best of fighters, crushed
too. Thrice seven times the lord made the earth empty of kshatriyas, and at
Samantapanchaka he made five lakes of blood. The upholder of the Bhrigu line
offered water in them to his fathers. He saw Richika before his eyes, and
Richika held him back.

"Then mighty Jamadagnya satisfied the king of the gods with a great sacrifice
and gave the earth to the priests. He gave great Kashyapa a golden altar, ten
fathoms long and nine high, lord of the people. With Kashyapa's leave the
brahmins divided it into pieces, and so they are known as the Khandavayanas,
king. When he had given the earth to great Kashyapa, the one of boundless
courage lived on this Mahendra, the king of mountains. So his enmity with the
kshatriyas of the world came about, and so the earth was conquered by Rama of
boundless splendour."

Then on the fourteenth day, as agreed, great-hearted Rama showed himself to
those brahmins and to the Dharma King and his younger brothers. The king of
kings, the lord, with his brothers, honoured him, and the best of kings paid the
highest honour to the twice-born. When he had honoured Jamadagnya and been
honoured by him, the lord stayed that night on Mahendra and set out facing
south.

---

As he went, the king of great power saw holy and lovely fords, all adorned with
brahmins, here and there along the sea, Bharata. The son of Pandu, son and
grandson of kings, bathed in them with his brothers, son of Parikshit, and went
to the most holy and praised river that runs to the sea. The great one bathed
there too and offered water to the Fathers and the gods. He gave wealth to the
chief brahmins and went to the Godavari, which runs to the sea. Then, free of
sin, among the Dravidas he came to the sea, holy to the world, king. The hero
saw Agastyatirtha, pure and holy, and the Fords of the Women. There the son of
Pandu heard of that deed of Arjuna, the finest of bowmen, which others could not
bear, and, honoured by hosts of great seers, he felt great joy. His body bathed
in those fords, with Krishna for his companion and with his younger brothers,
the lord of kings on the earth delighted in them, honouring Arjuna's courage.
Then, giving thousands of cows at those fords, he gladly told with his brothers
of Arjuna's gift of cows.

So, king, going in order to those holy fords of the sea and many others, his
wishes fulfilled, he saw most holy Shurparaka. Crossing a stretch of the ocean
there, he came to a forest famous on earth, where the gods had practised
austerity long ago, and holy kings had sacrificed. There the long, strong-armed
king saw the altar of Richika's son, the finest of bowmen, surrounded by hosts
of ascetics, to be worshipped by doers of merit. Then the great lord of the
earth saw the holy, lovely shrines of the Vasus, the hosts of the Maruts and the
two Ashvins, of Vaivasvata, the Adityas and the lord of wealth, of Indra, Vishnu
and the lord Savitar. He saw those of Bhaga, the Moon and the Sun, of the lord
of the waters and the hosts of the sadhyas, of Dhatar, the Fathers, and Rudra
with his hosts, king. He saw those of Sarasvati and the hosts of siddhas, of
Pushan, and of the other immortals. He kept fasts of many kinds in them, gave
jewels of great worth, bathed in all the fords, and came back to Shurparaka.

From that ford of the sea he set out again with his brothers and came to
Prabhasa, a ford famous on the earth among great brahmins. The broad, red-eyed
king bathed there with his younger brothers and offered water to the hosts of
the gods and to the Fathers, and so did Krishna, and those brahmins with
Lomasha. For twelve days, living on water and air, bathing by night and by day,
lighting fires on every side, the best of those who uphold dharma practised
austerity.

Rama and Janardana heard that he was practising fierce austerity there, and the
two, the best of all the Vrishnis, came to Yudhishthira Ajamidha with their
army. When the Vrishnis saw the sons of Pandu lying on the ground, their bodies
smeared with dirt, and saw Draupadi, who did not deserve it, they cried out in
great sorrow. Then Yudhishthira, undaunted, went to Rama and Janardana, to the
son of Krishna, to Samba and to the grandson of Shini, and to the other
Vrishnis, and honoured them as dharma requires. They too honoured all the
Parthas, and were honoured by the sons of Pandu, and sat around Yudhishthira,
king, as the hosts of the gods sit around Indra. With great trust he told
Krishna all that his enemies had done, and of their life in the forest. He told
him that Partha, the son of the king of the immortals, had gone to Indra for
weapons. When they heard his words, they believed them, and when they saw how
very thin the Pandavas were, the great Dasharhas shed tears born of sorrow and
pain.

**Divergences.**

* **Kartavirya and the cow.** In the familiar version Kartavirya steals Jamadagni's wish-granting cow. In
  the CE he takes the calf of the cow kept for the offerings, as she cries out (3.116.21).
* **The name.** The CE calls him Rama, Jamadagnya or Bhargava. It does not call him Parashurama. See L-62.
* **The rice dishes.** A rejected passage (\*570, \*572) replaces the two trees with two dishes of rice that
  the women exchange. In the CE they embrace the wrong trees (3.115.23–24).
* **Renuka and the Narmada.** A rejected line (\*575) says she fell from the sky into the Narmada. Not in the
  CE.
