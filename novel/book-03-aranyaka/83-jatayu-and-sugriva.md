# Jatayu and Sugriva

"Jatayu, son of Aruna, king of the vultures, was a friend of Dasharatha," said
Markandeya. "He was of great valour, and Sampati was his brother. He saw Sita,
his friend's daughter-in-law, on Ravana's lap, and in his anger the bird rushed
at Ravana, lord of the rakshasas. Then the vulture said to him, 'Let go! Let
Maithili go! How can you carry her off while I am alive, night-rover? You will
not get away from me alive if you do not let the lady go.'

"When he had said this to the lord of the rakshasas, he tore him fiercely with
his talons. Ravana was torn to pieces by many blows of wing and beak, and poured
out blood like a mountain with its streams. As he was being wounded by the
vulture, who wanted what was dear and good for Rama, he took his sword and cut
off the bird's wings. When he had killed the king of the vultures, who was like
a mountain peak with its clouds torn away, the rakshasa rose upward, holding
Sita on his lap.

"Wherever Vaidehi saw a circle of hermitages, or a lake, or a river, she dropped
an ornament. On a mountain ridge she saw five bulls among the monkeys, and the
high-minded woman let fall a great divine garment there. Blown by the wind, it
fell among those five lords of the monkeys, bright yellow, like lightning among
the clouds.

---

"When Vaidehi had been carried off like this, wise Rama killed the great deer
and turned back, and saw his brother Lakshmana. 'How can you have come here,
leaving Vaidehi in a forest haunted by rakshasas?' he said, blaming his brother
when he saw him. Thinking of how the rakshasa in the form of a deer had drawn
him away, and of his brother's coming, he was in torment. Still blaming him,
Rama hurried up to him. 'Is Vaidehi alive, Lakshmana? I do not see her.'
Lakshmana told him all of it, all that Sita had said, the unworthy last words
that Vaidehi had spoken.

"With a burning heart Rama ran to the hermitage. There he saw the vulture,
struck down, as big as a mountain. Suspecting a rakshasa, Kakutstha drew his bow
with force and ran at him with Lakshmana. The vulture, full of splendour, said
to Rama and Lakshmana together, 'Bless you both. I am the king of the vultures,
a friend of Dasharatha.' When they heard his words, the two put up their fine
bows and said, 'Who is this who names our father?' Then they saw him with both
his wings cut off. The vulture told them that Ravana had struck him down for
Sita's sake. Raghava asked the vulture, 'Which way did Ravana go?' The vulture
told him by nodding his head, and died. Understanding his sign to mean the
south, Kakutstha gave him the funeral rites, honouring his father's friend.

"Then they saw the hermitage with its seat-mats and water-pots thrown about, its
pitchers smashed, empty, overrun by crowds of jackals. Deep in grief and sorrow,
pained by Vaidehi's loss, the scorchers of enemies went south into the Dandaka
forest.

"In that great forest Rama and Saumitri saw herds of deer running in every
direction, and heard a terrible noise of creatures, like a growing forest fire.
In a moment they saw Kabandha, terrible to look at, like a mountain of cloud,
with shoulders like a sal tree and great arms. His wide eyes were in his chest,
and he had a great belly and a great mouth. As it happened, that rakshasa seized
Lakshmana in his hand, and at once Saumitri fell into despair, Bharata. He
looked at Rama as he was being dragged towards that mouth, and said to Rama in
despair, 'See this state of mine. Vaidehi carried off, this disaster for me,
your fall from the kingdom, and our father's death! I shall not see you come
back to Kosala with Vaidehi, established in the kingship of the earth that was
our fathers' and grandfathers'. Blessed are those who will see my noble
brother's face, consecrated with kusha grass, parched grain and shami leaves,
like the moon with a wisp of cloud.'

"Wise Lakshmana lamented in many ways like this. Then Kakutstha, calm even in
the midst of turmoil, said to him, 'Do not despair, tiger among men. He is
nothing while I am here. Cut off his right arm. I have cut off his left.' As he
said this, Rama struck off the arm with a very sharp sword, cutting it like a
sesame stalk. Then mighty Saumitri, seeing his brother Raghava standing there,
struck off the rakshasa's right arm with his sword, and struck the rakshasa
again, hard, in the side. The huge Kabandha fell lifeless to the ground.

"Out of his body came a man, divine to look at, and he was seen standing in the
sky, blazing in heaven like the sun. Rama, who spoke well, asked him, 'Who are
you? Tell me, since I ask. What is this wonder, by what wish? It seems very
strange to me.'

"He told him, 'I am the gandharva Vishvavasu, king. By a brahmin's curse I came
into a birth fit for rakshasas. Sita has been carried off by Ravana, the king
who lives in Lanka. Go to Sugriva. He will help you. Here is Pampa, with its
lovely waters, full of geese and ducks, a lake near Mount Rishyamuka. Sugriva
lives here with four counsellors. He is the brother of Valin, the king of the
monkeys, who wears a golden garland. This much we can tell you. You will see
Janaki. Surely the king of the monkeys knows where Ravana lives.'

"When he had said this, that divine man of great radiance vanished, and the two
heroes, Rama and Lakshmana, were amazed.

---

"Then Rama, stricken with grief at Sita's loss, came to Pampa nearby, a lake
full of lotuses and water lilies. Fanned in that forest by a cool, pleasant wind
scented like amrita, he went in his mind to his beloved. Remembering his
beloved, the king of kings lamented there, burning with the arrows of love. Then
Saumitri said to him, 'Such a state should not touch you, giver of honour, any
more than sickness touches a self-possessed man who lives by the ways of the
old. You have news of Vaidehi and of Ravana. Win her back by manhood and by
understanding. Let us go to Sugriva, the bull among monkeys, on the mountain.
Take heart in me, your pupil, your servant and your ally.'

"Spoken to by Lakshmana with many such words, Raghava came back to himself and
set his mind on the task. The two heroic brothers, Rama and Lakshmana, used the
waters of Pampa and offered water to the ancestors, and then set out. They came
to Rishyamuka, a mountain rich in roots and fruits, and the heroes saw five
monkeys on its peak.

"Sugriva sent to them his counsellor Hanuman, a wise monkey, who stood like the
Himalaya. They spoke with him first, and then went to Sugriva, and Rama made
friends with the king of the monkeys, king. When Rama had told him his purpose,
they showed him the garment that Sita had let fall among the monkeys as she was
carried off. Having gained that proof, Rama himself consecrated Sugriva, lord of
the leaping monkeys, as king of the monkeys on earth. Kakutstha promised to kill
Valin in battle, and Sugriva promised to bring Vaidehi back, king. When they had
said this and made their pact, and had come to trust each other, they all went
to Kishkindha and waited there, eager for battle.

"When Sugriva reached Kishkindha, he roared with a voice like a flood. Valin
could not bear it, but Tara held him back. 'The way this mighty monkey Sugriva
is roaring, I think he has come with a protector. You should not go out.'

"Then golden-garlanded Valin, lord of the monkeys, who spoke well, said to Tara,
whose face was like the lord of the stars, 'You understand the cries of all
creatures, and you are wise. Think. Who has become the protector of this one who
only smells of a brother?'

"Tara, bright as the lord of the stars, thought for a moment, and then the wise
woman said to her husband, 'Hear it all, lord of the monkeys. Rama son of
Dasharatha, of great spirit, whose wife has been carried off, has made a pact
with Sugriva to have the same enemies and friends. He is an archer. His brother,
strong-armed Saumitri, named Lakshmana, never defeated and wise, stands ready to
see the task done. Mainda and Dvivida, Hanuman the Wind's son, and Jambavan king
of the bears are Sugriva's counsellors. All of them are great, wise and very
strong, and with Rama's courage to lean on, they are enough to destroy you.'

"The lord of the monkeys brushed aside her words, though they were spoken for
his good. He was jealous, and suspected her, thinking her heart had gone to
Sugriva. He spoke harshly to Tara and went out from the mouth of the cave, and
said to Sugriva, who stood near Malyavat, 'I have beaten you many times, fool,
and you love your life. I let you go, knowing you for a kinsman. Why are you in
a hurry to die again?'

"At this the slayer of enemies, Sugriva, said to his brother reasoned words that
fitted the moment, as if to rouse Rama. 'You have taken my wife and taken my
kingdom, king. What power have I to live? Know that this is why I have come.'

"When they had said many things like this, Valin and Sugriva fell on each other
in battle, fighting with sal and palm trees and rocks. Both struck each other,
and both fell to the ground. Both leapt about strangely and hit each other with
their fists. Both were drenched with blood and torn by nails and teeth, and the
heroes shone like two flowering kimshuka trees. No difference could be seen
between them in the fight. Then Hanuman hung a garland on Sugriva's neck. With
the garland on his neck the hero shone like the great, splendid mountain Malaya
with a garland of clouds.

"When the great archer Rama saw Sugriva marked like this, he drew the best of
bows, aiming at Valin as at a target. The twang of his bow sounded like a
machine, and Valin shook with fear, struck in the heart by the arrow. Pierced in
his vitals, vomiting blood from his mouth, he saw Rama standing nearby with
Saumitri. He reproached Kakutstha and fell fainting to the ground. Tara saw him
on the ground, like the lord of the stars fallen.

"When Valin had been killed, Sugriva took Kishkindha, and with it Tara, whose
face was like the lord of the stars, whose lord had fallen. Wise Rama made his
dwelling for four months on the lovely slope of Malyavat, attended by Sugriva.

---

"Ravana, meanwhile, driven by the force of desire, went to his city of Lanka and
settled Sita in a house like Nandana, near the Ashoka grove, like an ascetic's
hermitage. Wasted by thinking of her husband, wearing the dress of an ascetic,
given to fasting and austerity, the wide-eyed woman lived there through days of
sorrow, eating fruits and roots.

"The lord of the rakshasas set rakshasis there to guard her, carrying spears,
swords, lances, axes, clubs and firebrands. There was one with two eyes, one
with three, one with an eye in her forehead, one with a long tongue and one with
no tongue, one with three breasts, one with one foot, one with three braids of
hair, and one with a single eye. These and others, with blazing eyes and hair as
coarse as a camel's, sat round Sita day and night, never tiring. Those fierce
she-demons, with their terrible voices, always threatened the long-eyed woman,
their words harsh in every syllable. 'Let us eat her! Let us tear her up, cut
her into pieces as small as sesame seeds, this woman who scorns our master and
lives on here!'

"Terrified again and again as they threatened her, overcome with grief for her
husband, she sighed and said to them, 'Noble ladies, eat me quickly. I have no
desire to live without him, the lotus-eyed one with dark curling hair. Even
without food, parted from the one I love more than life, I shall let my body
waste away, like a snake at the foot of a palm tree. But I would never go to any
man but Raghava. Know that this is the truth, and do whatever comes next.'

"When they heard her words, the harsh-voiced rakshasis went to tell the lord of
the rakshasas all of it from the beginning. When they had all gone, a rakshasi
named Trijata, who knew dharma and spoke kindly, comforted Vaidehi. 'Sita, I
shall tell you something. Trust me, friend. Let your fear go, lovely-thighed
one, and hear what I say.

"'There is a wise old bull among the rakshasas named Avindhya. He looks for
Rama's good, and he spoke to me for your sake. "Tell Sita from me, and comfort
and cheer her: Your husband Rama is well, and mighty, with Lakshmana following
him. Glorious Raghava has made friends with the king of the monkeys, who has the
splendour of Shakra, and is making ready for your sake. And do not be afraid,
timid one, of Ravana, whom the world condemns. You are protected by Nalakubara's
curse, faultless one. For the wicked one was cursed long ago for laying hands on
his daughter-in-law Rambha. He cannot go near a woman against her will, though
he has not mastered his senses. Your husband will come soon, guarded by Sugriva.
The wise one will come with Saumitri and free you from here."

"'And I have seen very terrible dreams, of evil omen, foretelling the ruin of
this fool who is destroying the family of Pulastya. This night-rover is cruel,
wicked-hearted and mean in his deeds. By his nature and his bad conduct he adds
to everyone's fear. He rivals all the gods, his mind struck by Time. I have seen
the signs of his ruin in a dream. Ten-faced Ravana, smeared with oil, his hair
loose, sinking in mud, stood again and again in a chariot yoked to donkeys, as
if dancing. Kumbhakarna and the others, naked, their hair fallen, wearing red
garlands and red ointments, were dragged towards the south. Vibhishana alone,
with a white parasol and a turban, adorned with white garlands, climbed a white
mountain. His four counsellors, with white garlands and ointments, climbed the
white mountain too. They will be freed from this great fear. The earth with its
oceans was covered by Rama's weapon. Your husband will fill the whole earth with
his fame. I saw Lakshmana too, sitting on a heap of bones, eating honey and rice
boiled in milk, looking in every direction. And again and again I saw you,
weeping, your body wet with blood, guarded by a tiger, going towards the north.
You will soon have joy, Vaidehi, with your husband. Before long, Sita, you will
be with Raghava and his brother.'

"When she heard these words of Trijata, the fawn-eyed young woman had hope again
of being with her husband. When those fierce and cruel she-demons came back,
they saw her sitting with Trijata as before."

**Divergences.**

* **Shabari.** The popular episode of the old woman Shabari, who waits for Rama and offers him berries, is not in the Mahabharata. Kabandha's gandharva sends Rama straight to Sugriva (3.263.39–41). See L-156.
* **Valin's death.** Rama shoots Valin while he fights Sugriva, after Hanuman marks Sugriva with a garland (3.264.33–36). Valin reproaches Rama and falls (3.264.38). The long exchange between Valin and Rama known from other tellings is not here. See L-156.
* **Ravana reaches Lanka.** Rejected passages (\*1259–\*1261) describe Ravana crossing the sea and entering Lanka with Sita at this point. The CE tells it after Valin's death (3.264.41).
* **Jatayu's reward.** A rejected line (\*1262) has a heavenly voice praise Jatayu as he wins perfection. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1258, \*1263–\*1269) add small details. Not narrated.

* 3.263.21: Jatayu "told him by nodding his head", and Rama reads the sign as "the south".
* 3.263.25 *urogataviśālākṣam*: "His wide eyes were in his chest".
