# The Hunter's Past

"When the whole dharma of release had been told like this, Yudhishthira," said
Markandeya, "the brahmin, deeply pleased, said to the righteous hunter, 'All
that you have told me is reasonable. It is plain that there is nothing in the
dharmas here that you do not know.'

"'See the dharma that is before my eyes, best of the twice-born,' said the
hunter, 'by which I have won this success, bull among brahmins. Get up quickly,
blessed one, and come into the inner house. You should see my mother and father,
you who know dharma.'

"At these words he went in, and saw a fine house, most carefully kept,
delightful, with four halls, very lovely, like a temple, well honoured with
offerings to the gods, crowded with beds and seats and full of the finest
scents. There sat the hunter's parents, honoured, dressed in white, having
eaten, well content, on a fine seat. When the righteous hunter saw them, he fell
at their feet with his head.

"'Get up, get up, you who know dharma,' said the old pair. 'May dharma guard
you. We are pleased with your purity. May you live long. You are a good son, and
you always honour us well, son. You have no other god among the gods. Through
your devotion to the twice-born, you are full of self-control. Your father's
grandfathers, and his great-grandfathers, are always pleased with you, son,
because of your self-control and the honour you pay us. Your service never fails
in mind, in deed or in word, and we see no other, false thought in you now. As
Rama Jamadagnya honoured his old parents, so you have done everything, and more,
little son.'

"Then the righteous hunter presented the brahmin to them, and they welcomed the
brahmin and honoured him. The brahmin accepted their honour and asked them both,
'Are you well at home, with your son and your servants? Are your bodies always
free of sickness here?'

"'We are well at home, brahmin,' said the old pair, 'and among the servants in
every way. And you, blessed one, have you come here without hindrance?'

"'Yes,' the brahmin answered them gladly. Then the righteous hunter spoke to the
brahmin words full of meaning. 'Blessed one, my father and mother are my highest
gods. What is to be done for the gods I do for them. As all the thirty-three
gods, led by Shakra, are to be honoured by the whole world, so are these two old
ones to me. As the twice-born bring offerings to the gods, so I do for them,
without tiring. These two, my father and mother, are my highest gods, brahmin. I
always please them with flowers, fruits and jewels, brahmin. They are my fires,
the ones the wise speak of. They are the sacrifices and the four Vedas. They are
everything to me, brahmin. For them are my life, my wife, my sons and my
friends. With my sons and my wife I always serve them. I bathe them myself and
wash their feet, and I give them their food myself, best of the twice-born. I
speak pleasant words to them and avoid what is unpleasant. I do what pleases
them, even if it is joined to adharma. Knowing dharma alone to be the teacher, I
do this, best of the twice-born, and I always serve them, brahmin, without
tiring.

"'A man who wants to prosper has five teachers only, brahmin: father, mother,
the fire, the self and the teacher, best of the twice-born. The man who behaves
rightly towards them, best of the twice-born, always keeps his fires tended.
This is the eternal dharma of one who lives as a householder.'

---

"When he had presented his two elders, his mother and father, to the brahmin,
the righteous hunter spoke to the brahmin again. 'My eyes have been opened. See
the strength of austerity. That is why she said to you, "Go to Mithila": that
woman devoted to serving her husband, self-controlled and truthful: "A hunter
who lives in Mithila will tell you of dharma."'

"'I remember the words of that faithful woman,' said the brahmin, 'truthful and
rich in good conduct, you of restrained vows, and I think you are a man of
virtue, you who know dharma.'

"'What she said to you about me then, best of the twice-born, lord,' said the
hunter, 'that woman faithful to one husband saw it truly. There is no doubt of
it. I have shown you this out of a wish to help you, brahmin. Now listen to what
I will tell you for your good, my son, brahmin. You have wronged your mother and
father, best of the twice-born. You left your home without their leave,
blameless one, to go reciting the Vedas. That was not right. With grief for you
the two old ascetics have gone blind. Go and win their favour, so that great
dharma does not pass you by. You are an ascetic, great-spirited and always
devoted to dharma, but all this is worth nothing to you. Win their favour
quickly. Believe me, brahmin. You must not do otherwise. Go today, brahmin seer.
I tell you what is best for you.'

"'All you have said is true, no doubt of it,' said the brahmin. 'I am pleased
with you, you who know dharma, of good conduct and full of virtue.'

"'You are like a god,' said the hunter, 'since you follow dharma, the ancient,
eternal, divine dharma, hard for those whose selves are unformed to win. Without
tiring, honour your mother and father quickly. I see no dharma beyond this.'

"'How fortunate that I came here,' said the brahmin, 'and how fortunate that I
met you. Men like you, who show dharma, are hard to find in the world. There may
be one who knows dharma among a thousand men, or there may not. I am pleased
with your truth. Bless you, best of men. I was falling into hell, and you have
lifted me out. It had to be like this, sinless one, since I have seen you. As
King Yayati, fallen, was saved by his good daughter's sons, tiger among men, so
I have been saved here by you. I will serve my mother and father at your word.
One whose self is unformed does not know how to decide between dharma and
adharma.

"'Eternal dharma is hard to know for one living in a shudra's birth. I do not
think you are a shudra. There must be a reason for it, some ripening of deeds by
which you have come to be a shudra. I want to know it truly, great-minded one.
Tell me the truth of it all, if you will, since your self is restrained.'

"'Brahmins must not be disobeyed, best of the twice-born,' said the hunter.
'Hear all that happened to me in a former body, sinless one. I was a brahmin
before, son of an excellent brahmin, a student of the Veda, very skilled, one
who had gone to the far shore of the limbs of the Veda. Through faults I made
myself, brahmin, I came to this state.

"'A certain king, given to the science of the bow, was my friend, and through
keeping company with him I became excellent at the bow, brahmin. At that time
the king went out hunting with his chief warriors and his counsellors around
him, and killed many deer near a hermitage. Then I too shot a terrible arrow,
best of the twice-born, and a sage was struck by that straight-jointed arrow.
Fallen on the ground, brahmin, he cried aloud, "I have done no wrong at all. Who
has done this evil?" Thinking it was a deer, I suddenly came upon the sage. I
saw the seer pierced by the straight-jointed arrow, that brahmin of fierce
austerity, moaning on the ground. My mind was deeply shaken at having done such
a wrong, and I said to him, "I did this without knowing." And I said to the
sage, "Please forgive me, brahmin." Then the seer, fainting with anger, answered
me, "You will be a hunter, cruel one, in a shudra's birth," brahmin.

"'Cursed like this by the seer then, best of the twice-born, I tried to win the
seer's favour with words, for he was skilled in speech. "I did this wrong today
without knowing, sage. Please forgive it all. Be gracious, blessed one."

"'"The curse will not be otherwise," said the seer. "It will be so, without
doubt. But out of gentleness I will do you some kindness today. Living in a
shudra's birth, you will know dharma, and you will serve your mother and father,
no doubt of it. By that service you will win great success. You will remember
your former births and go to heaven. When the curse has run its course, you will
be a brahmin again."

"'So I was cursed long ago by that seer of fierce splendour, and so he showed me
grace, best of the two-footed. And I drew out his arrow, best of the twice-born,
and brought him to his hermitage, and he did not lose his life. I have told you
all that happened to me long ago. And before long I must go to heaven, best of
the twice-born.'

"'So men come to sorrows and to joys, wise one,' said the brahmin. 'You should
not grieve. You have done a hard thing, my son, knowing your own birth. The
fault of deeds came with the birth you made for yourself, wise one. Bear it for
a while, and then you will be a twice-born. And even now I think you a brahmin,
no doubt of it. A brahmin who lives by degrading wrong deeds, a hypocrite,
mostly doing evil, is like a shudra. But a shudra who always rises to
self-control, truth and dharma I think a brahmin, for one becomes twice-born by
conduct. By the fault of his deeds a man comes to a hard and terrible state. But
I think your fault is nearly spent, best of men. You should not grieve. Men like
you, who know the ways of the world and are always devoted to dharma, do not
despair.'

"'One should kill the sorrow of the mind with wisdom,' said the hunter, 'and the
sorrow of the body with medicines. That is the power of knowledge. One should
not become like children. Men of little understanding are joined to sorrows of
the mind by meeting what they do not want and parting from what they love.
Creatures are joined to good things and parted from them, all of them. This is
not a cause of grief for one alone. Seeing a thing joined with what he does not
want, a man quickly turns away from it. And then men set it right, if they see a
way. The one who grieves gains nothing. He only suffers.

"'Men who give up both sorrow and joy, wise men content with knowledge, grow in
happiness. Fools are given to discontent, and the learned come to contentment.
There is no end to discontent, but contentment is the highest happiness. Those
who have gone the road do not grieve, seeing the highest goal. One should not
set one's mind on despair. Despair is the worst poison. It kills the child whose
wisdom is not formed, like an angry snake. When a hard time comes, the man whom
despair overcomes loses his splendour, and there is no manhood left in him. The
fruit of a deed one keeps doing is surely seen. No one who gives way to
weariness wins anything good. Rather he should look for a way to free himself
from sorrow. Without grieving he should begin at once, and, well yoked, be free
of vice. Those who have gone beyond understanding and thought on the passing of
creatures, whose wisdom is formed, do not grieve, seeing the highest goal. I do
not grieve, wise one. I stand waiting for the time. With these examples before
me, brahmin, I do not sink, good man.'

"'Your wisdom is formed, and you are wise,' said the brahmin, 'and your
understanding is wide. I do not grieve for you. You are content with knowledge
and you know dharma. I take my leave of you. May it go well with you. May dharma
guard you. You must be watchful in dharma, best of those who uphold dharma.'

"'Yes,' said the hunter with joined hands. The best of the twice-born walked
round him, keeping him on his right, and set out. The brahmin went and did all
the service for his old mother and father, as was right, strict in his vows.

"I have told you all of it, Yudhishthira, my son, best of those who uphold
dharma, the dharma you asked about: the greatness of the faithful wife and of
the brahmin, the service of mother and father, and the dharma of the hunter."

"This is a most wonderful tale of dharma, brahmin, best of all who uphold
dharma, best of the twice-born," said Yudhishthira. "It was so pleasant to hear,
wise one, that the time has passed like a moment. I am not satisfied, blessed
one, hearing the highest dharma."
