# Maitreya's Curse

When he heard that Vidura had come back and that the king had comforted him, the
evil-minded King Duryodhana, the son of Dhritarashtra, was tormented. He sent
for the son of Subala, and for Karna and Duhshasana, and, sunk in a darkness
born of folly, the king said this.

"Here is Dhritarashtra's trusted counsellor, Vidura, come back, the wise friend
of the sons of Pandu, who is devoted to their good. Before Vidura turns the
king's mind again towards bringing the Pandavas back, think of a plan that is
good for me. If ever I see the Parthas come back here, I will waste away again,
lifeless and without restraint. I will take poison, or hang myself, or use a
blade, or go into the fire. I cannot bear to see them prosper here again."

"What childish idea have you taken up, king, lord of the people?" said Shakuni.
"They left after making an agreement. This will not happen. All the Pandavas
keep to their word, bull of the Bharatas. They will never accept your father's
word, my son. Or if they do accept it, and come back to the city, breaking the
agreement, we will play for the stake again. Let us all stay neutral, following
the king's wishes, and watch closely for the Pandavas' weak points."

"It is just as you say, wise uncle," said Duhshasana. "Your advice always
pleases me when you speak."

"We all look to what you want, Duryodhana," said Karna. "We are all plainly of
one mind, king."

When Karna said this, King Duryodhana was not much pleased, and quickly turned
his face away. Karna saw this. He opened his fine eyes wide, and in a fury,
angry with Duhshasana and the son of Subala, he roused himself and said, "Well,
kings, listen to what I think. We all want to do the king what pleases him, with
our hands ready to serve. But we cannot all stay tirelessly at his pleasure. Let
us take up our weapons, mount our chariots in our armour, and go together to
kill the Pandavas in the forest. When they are all at peace, gone the way no one
knows, the sons of Dhritarashtra and we will have no more quarrels. As long as
they are in trouble, as long as they are sunk in grief and have no allies, they
can be beaten. That is what I think."

When they heard his words, they praised the son of the suta again and again, and
all answered, "Yes!" Having said this, they set out in fury, each in his own
chariot, in a body, their minds made up to kill the Pandavas.

When Krishna Dvaipayana, pure in soul, saw with his divine sight that they had
set out, he came. The blessed one, honoured by the worlds, stopped them all.
Then he went quickly to the king whose eyes were his wisdom, as he sat there,
and spoke to him.

---

"Dhritarashtra, wise king, listen to what I say," said Vyasa. "I will tell you
what is best for all the Kauravas. I am not pleased, strong-armed one, that the
Pandavas have gone to the forest, beaten by trickery by those who do
Duryodhana's bidding. When the thirteenth year is full, they will remember their
sufferings, and in their anger they will let loose their poison on the Kauravas,
Bharata. Why then does this wicked and foolish son of yours, always angry with
the Pandavas, want to kill them for the sake of the kingdom? Let this fool be
restrained. Let your son be at peace. If he tries to kill them in the forest, he
will lose his own life.

"Do what is right, as wise Vidura says, as Bhishma says, as we say, as Kripa and
Drona say. War with one's own kin is condemned, wise king. Do not undertake it.
It is against dharma, and it brings no fame. If the way he looks at the Pandavas
is left alone, Bharata, it will lead to great harm, king. Or else let this
foolish son of yours go to the forest with the Pandavas, king, alone and without
helpers. Then, if from living with them your son comes to love the Pandavas, you
will have done what you need to do, lord of men. But they say, great king, that
the character a man is born with does not leave him until he dies. What do
Bhishma, Drona and Vidura think, or you yourself? Let what is fitting be done
here, before the matter goes past us."

"Blessed one, I did not like this business of dice either," said Dhritarashtra.
"I think I was made to do it by fate, forcing its way, sage. Bhishma does not
like it, nor Drona, nor Vidura, and Gandhari does not want the dice. It was set
going by folly. But though I know it, I cannot give up the witless Duryodhana,
holy one, you of firm vows, because of my love for my son."

"You speak the truth, king, son of Vichitravirya," said Vyasa. "I know well that
a son is the highest thing. There is nothing higher than a son. Even Indra was
taught this by Surabhi's tears. No other riches, however great, are higher than
a son. I will tell you a great and excellent story about this, the talk between
Surabhi and Indra, lord of the people.

"Long ago, they say, Surabhi, the mother of cows, was weeping in heaven, my son,
and Indra felt pity for her. 'Why are you weeping, lovely one?' said Indra. 'I
hope all is well with the gods, and with men, and with the cows. This cannot be
for nothing.'

"'I see no disaster for you, lord of the thirty gods,' said Surabhi. 'But I
grieve for my son, and that is why I weep, Kaushika. Look at that cruel farmer,
and at my weak little son. He is beating him with a goad, and he is crushed by
the plough. When I see him so tired, and being beaten, lord of the gods, I am
full of pity, and my heart is troubled. One of them is strong and carries the
heavier load. The other has little strength or breath. He is thin, his veins
stand out, and he can hardly carry the load. It is for him I grieve, Vasava.
Look, Vasava, he is beaten with the goad and pricked again and again, and he
cannot carry the load. That is why I cry out in my pain and sorrow, shedding
tears from my eyes in pity.'

"'You have thousands of sons being worked hard, lovely one,' said Indra. 'Why
pity this one, when only one son is suffering?'

"'I have a thousand sons, and they are all the same to me,' said Surabhi. 'But I
feel more pity, Shakra, for the son who is in trouble.'

"When Indra heard Surabhi's words he was amazed, and from then on he thought a
son dearer even than life, Kauravya. The blessed chastiser of Paka poured down
heavy rain on that very place at once, and so stopped the farmer's work. So, as
Surabhi said, let all your sons be the same to you, king, and feel more pity for
those who are in trouble. As my son Pandu was to me, so are you, my son, and so
is wise Vidura. I say this out of love. For a long time you have had a hundred
and one sons, king. Pandu has only five, and they are unhappy and in great
sorrow. How will they live? How will they prosper? That is how my mind burns for
the Parthas in their trouble. If you want the Kauravyas to live, king, let your
son Duryodhana make peace with the Pandavas."

"It is just as you say, wise sage," said Dhritarashtra. "I know it, and so do
all these kings. You think what brings the Kurus happiness is good, and Vidura
says the same to me, sage, and Bhishma and Drona. If I deserve your kindness, if
you have pity for the Kauravas, instruct my wicked son Duryodhana."

"Here comes the blessed seer Maitreya, king," said Vyasa. "He has been with the
Pandava brothers, and has come here wanting to see us. This great seer will
instruct your son Duryodhana, king, as is proper, for the peace of your family.
Do what he says, king of kings, without hesitating. For if it is not done, he
will curse your son in his anger."

---

With these words Vyasa left, and Maitreya appeared. The king and his son
received him with honour. When the king, Dhritarashtra, the son of Ambika, had
given him water for his feet and all the other courtesies, and the great sage
had rested, the king spoke. He said to him humbly, "I hope your journey to
Kurujangala was a pleasant one, my lord. I hope the five heroes, the Pandava
brothers, are well. I hope those bulls among men mean to keep the agreement. I
hope the brotherly love of the Kurus will not be broken."

"I was going on a pilgrimage to the holy places," said Maitreya, "and I came to
Kurujangala. By chance I saw the Dharma King in the Kamyaka forest. Crowds of
sages had come to see the great man, dressed in matted hair and deerskin, living
in the forest of ascetics, my lord. There I heard, great king, of your sons'
folly, and of the great disaster that has come in the shape of dice. So I have
come to you, looking to the good of the Kauravas, for I have always had great
love and fondness for you, my lord. It is not fitting, king, while you and
Bhishma are alive, that your sons should quarrel with one another, lord of men.
You yourself are the post round which all turns, king, in holding back and
holding on. Why do you ignore this terrible wrong as it rises up? What was done
in the hall, joy of the Kurus, was the work of robbers. Because of it, king, you
do not shine among the ascetics."

Then the blessed seer Maitreya turned to King Duryodhana, who could never bear
anything, and said gently, "Duryodhana, strong-armed, best of speakers, wise
one, listen to what I tell you for your own good. Do not betray the Pandavas,
king. Do what is good for yourself, for the Pandavas, for the Kurus and for the
world, bull among men. For all of them are tigers among men, heroes, bold
fighters, all with the strength of ten thousand elephants, with bodies as hard
as the thunderbolt, firm. They are all devoted to truth and to their vows, all
men of pride. They killed the enemies of the gods, the rakshasas who take any
shape they please, Hidimba and Baka first among them, and the rakshasa Kirmira.
When the great men left here by night, that savage one stood blocking their way,
unmoving as a mountain. Bhima, who glories in battle, the best of the strong,
killed him by strength, as a tiger kills a small deer, with a beast's death.
Look how Bhima struck down the great archer Jarasandha, with the strength of ten
thousand elephants in battle, at the time of the conquest of the quarters. What
man, who is subject to old age and death, could stand against them in battle,
when Vasudeva is their kinsman and Parshata their brother-in-law? So let there
be peace between you and the Pandavas, bull of the Bharatas. Do as I say, king.
Do not fall into the power of death."

As Maitreya was saying this, lord of the people, Duryodhana struck his thigh,
which was like an elephant's trunk, with his hand. With a smile, the fool
scratched the ground with his foot. He said nothing, and stood with his face a
little turned down.

When Maitreya saw Duryodhana scratching the earth and not listening, king, anger
took hold of him. The best of sages was overcome with anger, and, as fate would
have it, he set his mind on cursing him. He touched water, his eyes red with
anger, and Maitreya cursed the evil-minded son of Dhritarashtra.

"Because you slight me, and will not do as I say, reap the fruit of this pride
of yours at once. A great war will arise from your treachery, in which mighty
Bhima will break your thigh with the blows of his mace."

When he said this, King Dhritarashtra tried to appease the sage. "Let it not be
so."

"If your son makes peace, king, in whatever way," said Maitreya, "the curse will
not come about, my son. If he does the opposite, it will."

Then Duryodhana's father, put out of countenance, said to Maitreya, "How was
Kirmira struck down by Bhima?"

"I will not tell you," said Maitreya, "for your son will not listen, and I am
displeased. Vidura here will tell you everything when I have gone."

With these words, Maitreya left the way he had come. Duryodhana, shaken by the
killing of Kirmira, went out.
