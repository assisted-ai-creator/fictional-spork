# Markandeya

As they lived there, the rains came, the season that ends the heat and brings
happiness to all creatures. Great, loud, dark clouds covered the sky and the
quarters and rained day and night without pause. They had their homes in the
ending of the heat, in hundreds and thousands. The net of the sun's rays was
gone from them, and they shone clear with lightning. The earth, sprinkled with
water, grew with grass, full of drunken gadflies and creeping things, its smoke
and dust laid, no longer red. With everything covered by water, nothing could be
made out, level or rough, rivers or trees. Swift rivers, their waters stirred,
roaring loud, as if breathing hard, made the forests beautiful when the heat was
over. Sounds of many kinds were heard at the edges of the forests, of boars,
beasts and birds crying as the rain beat on them. Chatakas and peacocks with
flocks of cuckoos flew about drunk with joy, and so did the proud frogs. So the
rainy season, of many shapes, loud with clouds, passed happily for them as they
wandered over the desert places.

Autumn came, full of flocks of curlews and swans, with its forests and meadows
grown high and its rivers of clear water. The autumn, with its clear sky and
stars, full of beasts and birds, was happy for the great-spirited Pandavas. They
saw the nights free of dust, cool from the clouds, bright with hosts of planets
and stars and with the moon. They saw the rivers and lotus pools, cool and
auspicious, adorned with white lilies and white lotuses. Wandering by the holy
fords of the Sarasvati, whose banks were like the sky, thick with nipa trees and
wild rice, they were glad. The heroes, firm bowmen, rejoiced to see the
auspicious Sarasvati full to the brim, with her clear water. As they lived
there, the holiest night came to them, the autumn night of Karttika, at the
joint of the moon, Janamejaya. With the ascetics of holy deeds and great spirit,
the Pandavas, best of the Bharatas, performed the highest yoga all together. At
the first rising of the dark fortnight, the Pandavas set out for the Kamyaka
forest with Dhaumya, the charioteers and the stewards.

---

When the sons of Kunti, led by Yudhishthira, reached Kamyaka, the hosts of sages
welcomed them, and they settled there with Krishna. Then, as the sons of Pandu
lived there in trust, many brahmins gathered round them on every side. Then a
certain brahmin, a dear friend of Arjuna's, said, "The strong-armed one of noble
mind, the self-controlled Shauri, will come here. For Hari knows you have come
here, upholders of the Kurus. Hari always longs to see you and seeks your good.
And Markandeya of great austerity, who has lived many years, given to study of
the Veda and to austerity, will soon join you."

As he was speaking, Keshava, the best of chariot fighters, appeared in his
chariot yoked with Sainya and Sugriva. Like Maghavan with Pauloma's daughter,
the son of Devaki came with Satyabhama, wanting to see the best of the Kurus.
Krishna got down from his chariot and, glad and wise, saluted the Dharma King as
the rules require, and Bhima, best of the strong. He honoured Dhaumya, and the
twins saluted him. He embraced Gudakesha and comforted Draupadi. When the
Dasharha saw his dear Phalguna, the hero, the tamer of enemies, come back after
so long, he embraced him again and again. So too Satyabhama, Krishna's dear
queen, embraced Draupadi, the beloved wife of the Pandavas. Then all the
Pandavas, with their wife and their priest, honoured the lotus-eyed one and
gathered round him on every side.

Wise Krishna, meeting Dhananjaya, the Partha, the terror of the asuras, shone
like the great lord of beings, the blessed one himself, meeting Guha. Then
Kiritin told Gada's elder brother all that had happened in the forests, as it
was, and asked in turn how Subhadra and Abhimanyu were. The slayer of Madhu
honoured the Parthas, Krishna and the priest as was proper, then sat down with
King Yudhishthira and spoke in his praise.

"Dharma is higher than winning a kingdom, Pandava," he said, "and they say that
its purpose is austerity itself, king. Keeping your own dharma with truth and
straightness, you have won this world and the next. First you studied, keeping
your vows. You won the whole science of the bow properly. You gained wealth by
the dharma of a kshatriya, and you performed all the ancient sacrifices. You
take no pleasure in the ways of the village. You do nothing for desire, lord of
men, and you do not give up dharma out of greed for wealth. That is why, by your
nature, you are the Dharma King. Giving and truth and austerity, king, faith and
peace, steadiness and patience: these have always been your highest delight,
Partha, even when you had kingdoms, wealth and pleasures.

"When the crowds of the Kurujangala saw Krishna helpless in the hall, in conduct
that had left behind all dharma and all law, who but you could have borne it,
Pandava? Without doubt, with all your wishes fulfilled, you will soon rule your
people well. Here we are to restrain the Kurus, when your promise is fulfilled."

The lion of the Dasharhas said to Dhaumya and Krishna, to Yudhishthira, the
twins and Bhima, "How fortunate that Kiritin has come back to you happy, well,
with his weapons won." With his friends, the lord of the Dasharhas said to
Krishna Yajnaseni too, "Krishna, your sons are devoted above all to the science
of the bow, true to their vows, children of good character. They keep the
discipline that good men always keep, Yajnaseni. Your father and your brothers
invite them with kingdom and lands, Krishna, but the boys find no pleasure in
the house of Yajnasena or of their uncles. They have gone happily to the
Anartas, devoted above all to the science of the bow. Since your sons entered
the city of the Vrishnis, Krishna, they do not long for the gods. As you
yourself would treat them, and as noble Kunti would, so Subhadra treats them,
always, without neglect, and more. As Rukmini's son is teacher and refuge to
Aniruddha, to Abhimanyu, to Sunitha and to Bhanu, so he is to your sons too,
Krishna. The tireless teacher trains them well, and young Abhimanyu always
trains them too, brave as they are, in holding the mace, sword and shield, in
weapons and their lore, and in driving chariots and riding horses. Rukmini's son
has given them their training well and handed them weapons like a teacher, and
he is pleased with the courage of your sons and of Abhimanyu. When your sons go
out looking for sport, Yajnaseni, chariots, carriages and elephants follow each
one of them."

Then Krishna said to the Dharma King, "The warriors of the Dasharhas, the
Kukuras and Andhakas, are ready to do your bidding. They stand wherever you wish
them, king. Let the army of the Madhus turn out, lord of men, with its swift
bows like the wind, led by the one whose weapon is the plough. Let it come with
its horsemen, foot soldiers, horses, chariots and elephants, ready for your
cause. Let Suyodhana, son of Dhritarashtra, the worst of evildoers, be sent,
Pandava, with his kin and his host of friends, on the road of Saubha and the
lord of Saubha. Or, if you wish, lord of men, keep to the agreement as it was
made in the hall, and let the City of the Elephant, its horsemen and warriors
held by the Dasharha fighters, wait for you. Free of anger, your sin put away,
take your pleasure wherever you like. Then, free of grief, you will come into
the City of the Elephant, prosperous, first among its kingdom."

The great-spirited Dharma King understood the thought of the best of men, spoken
as it should be. He praised him, looked at him, and said to Keshava with joined
hands, "Without doubt, Keshava, you are the Pandavas' refuge. The Parthas have
their shelter in you. When the time comes, you will do this, and more than this.
There is no doubt of it. The time has been spent as promised, all twelve years
in lonely places. When we have finished the time of living unknown as the rules
require, Keshava, the sons of Pandu will come to you."

---

As the Varshneya and the Dharma King were speaking, Bharata, the righteous
Markandeya appeared behind them, of great austerity, grown old in austerity, who
had lived many thousands of years. All the brahmins honoured that aged seer of
many thousand years when he came, and so did Krishna with the Pandavas. When the
best of seers had been honoured and sat at ease, trusting them, Keshava spoke
with the consent of the brahmins and the Pandavas. "The Pandavas are eager to
listen, Markandeya, and so are the brahmins gathered here, and Draupadi and
Satyabhama, and I. Tell us the highest words, the holy tales of long ago, the
eternal ways of the good, of kings and women and seers."

As they sat there, the divine seer Narada too, pure in spirit, came to see the
Pandavas. All the bulls among men honoured that great-spirited wise one too with
water for his feet and the guest offering, as is proper. Knowing that they were
ready to listen, the divine seer Narada welcomed the tale Markandeya was about
to tell. Knowing the time, Narada said to him, as if smiling, "Brahmin seer,
tell what you mean to say to the Pandavas."

At these words Markandeya of great austerity answered, "Wait a moment. There
will be a great deal to tell." At his words the Pandavas and those brahmins
waited, looking at the great sage like the sun at midday.

Seeing that the great sage wanted to speak, the Pandava, king of the Kurus,
urged him to begin the tales. "You know the deeds of the gods, the daityas and
the great seers, and of all the royal seers, ancient one. We have long thought
you worth serving and waiting on, and we have longed for you. And the son of
Devaki has come here to see us. When I see myself fallen from happiness, and the
wicked sons of Dhritarashtra thriving in every way, this thought comes to me. A
man is the doer of his deeds, good and bad, and he eats their fruit. How then is
God the doer? Or, best of those who know brahman, does what a man has done
follow him in his joys and sorrows here, or else in another body? And the
embodied one, when he leaves the body and is sought out by his good and bad
deeds, how is he joined to them after death, or here, best of the twice-born? Is
this of this world, or of the other world? And where do a dead creature's deeds
stay, Bhargava?"

"This question is fit for you, best of speakers," said Markandeya, "and it is
well put. You know what is to be known, and you ask to keep things in order. I
will tell you about it here. Listen with one mind, how a man eats joy and sorrow
here and in the other world.

"Prajapati, born first, created pure and spotless bodies for embodied beings,
bound to dharma. The men of old, joy of the Kurus, were holy. Their strength and
purpose were never in vain. They kept good vows, spoke the truth, and were one
with brahman. They all went up to the sky with the gods at will, and came back
again at will, all going where they pleased. Those men died when they chose and
lived as they chose. They had few troubles, no fears, their aims were achieved
and nothing troubled them. They saw the hosts of gods and the great-spirited
seers. All dharmas were plain before their eyes. They were self-controlled and
free of envy. They lived thousands of years and had thousands of sons.

"Then, at another time, men who walked the earth were overcome by desire and
anger and lived by trickery and deceit. Overcome by greed and delusion, those
men were abandoned by the gods. The wicked, by their evil deeds, go to animal
births and to hell. They are cooked again and again in the many turnings of the
world. Their wishes are empty, their purposes empty, their knowledge empty, and
they are without sense. They come to suspect everything and to share in
suffering, and mostly they are marked by their evil deeds. They are born in bad
families, full of sickness, evil-minded, without power. The wicked become
short-lived, and the fruit of their cruel deeds comes up in them. They beg for
everything they want. They deny the other world and break the bounds.

"The way of a dead creature, son of Kunti, comes from his own deeds here. You
ask where the store of deeds of the wise man and of the fool stays, and where he
stays when he eats the good or the other. That is your question. Hear the answer
to it. With his first body, made by the gods, a man gathers a great store of
good and bad deeds. At the end of his life he leaves this body, now nearly worn
out, and is born at once in a womb. There is no state in between. There the deed
he has done always follows him like a shadow, and it bears fruit, and he is born
to deserve happiness or to deserve sorrow. Joined to the rule of fate, that
creature is seen by those with the eye of knowledge to bear good marks or bad.

"That is the way of those without understanding, Yudhishthira. Now learn the
highest way of those who have knowledge. There are men who have practised
austerity, who hold to all the scriptures, firm in their vows, given to truth
and to serving their elders. They are of good character, of pure birth, patient,
self-controlled, full of splendour. They pass mostly through good births and
bear good marks. Having mastered their senses, they are masters of themselves.
Because they are pure, they have little sickness. Because they have few troubles
and fears, nothing afflicts them. With the eye of knowledge they know their own
selves and others', dying, being born and dwelling in the womb, in every way.
Having come to this land of deeds, they go again to the home of the gods. Men
get something by fate, king, something by chance, and something by their own
deeds. Let there be no other thought for you.

"Now learn this likeness too, best of speakers. I think it is the best thing in
the world of men, Yudhishthira. One man has this world and not the next. One has
the next and not this. One has both this world and the next. One has neither the
next nor this. Those who have great wealth and always take their pleasure with
their bodies well adorned have this world, destroyer of great enemies, not the
next, for they are always given to bodily pleasure. Some are joined in yoga,
devoted to austerity, given to study of the Veda. They wear out their bodies,
master their senses and are set on the good of all creatures. They have that
world and not this one, slayer of enemies. Those who practise dharma first, and
win wealth by dharma in its time, and take wives and sacrifice, have this world
and the next. The unlucky fools who strive neither for learning, nor austerity,
nor giving, nor even for offspring, and find no pleasures, have neither this
world nor the next.

"But all of you are of great courage and spirit, with divine power and strong
bodies. You have come from that world to the earth for the work of the gods, and
you have learned your knowledge well. You will do great deeds, heroes, living by
austerity, self-control and good conduct. You will satisfy the gods, the seers
and all the hosts of the dead with the highest rites. Then you will come in due
course, by your own deeds, to the highest heaven, the home of those who have
done holy deeds. Do not doubt it, lord of the Kauravas, when you see this
hardship of yours, you who deserve happiness."
