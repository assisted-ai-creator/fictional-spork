# Savitri

"I do not grieve for myself, great sage," said Yudhishthira, "nor for these
brothers of mine, nor for the loss of the kingdom, as I grieve for this daughter
of Drupada. When we were tormented by wicked men at the dice, Krishna saved us.
And again she was carried off by force from the forest by Jayadratha. Is there
any woman, seen before or heard of, as faithful to her husband and as greatly
blessed as this daughter of Drupada?"

"Hear, King Yudhishthira," said Markandeya, "of the great fortune of women of
good family, all of it, as it came to the king's daughter Savitri.

"There was a king among the Madras whose self was dharma, most devoted to
dharma, kind to brahmins, a refuge, true to his promises, master of his senses.
He sacrificed and gave generously, he was able, and the people of the city and
the country loved him. The king was named Ashvapati, and he delighted in the
good of all creatures. He was patient, truthful, master of his senses, and
without children. As his years went by he fell into grief.

"To have children he took up a strict discipline, eating little at fixed times,
chaste, master of his senses. He made a hundred thousand offerings with the
Savitri verse, best of kings, and ate only a little at every sixth mealtime. He
kept this discipline for eighteen years. When the eighteenth year was complete,
Savitri was pleased, and showed herself to the king in bodily form, king. Rising
out of the fire of the agnihotra with great joy, the giver of boons spoke to the
king.

"'I am pleased with you, king, for your pure chastity, your self-control and
discipline, and your devotion to me with your whole self. Choose a boon as you
wish, Ashvapati, king of Madra. But never be careless in the ways of dharma.'

"'I undertook this for the sake of children, wanting dharma,' said Ashvapati.
'May I have many sons, goddess, to raise up my family. If you are pleased with
me, goddess, this is the wish I choose. For the twice-born tell me that
offspring is the highest dharma.'

"'I knew this wish of yours before, king,' said Savitri, 'and I spoke to the
Grandfather about sons for you. By the grace of the self-born one, granted on
earth, a radiant daughter will soon be born to you, gentle one. You must not
answer me in any way. I tell you this pleased, by the Grandfather's gift.'

"The king accepted Savitri's words, saying, 'So be it,' and again begged her
favour: 'Let it be soon.' When Savitri had vanished, the king went to his own
house and lived happily in his kingdom, ruling his people by dharma.

"After some time the king, keeping his vow, got his eldest queen, who walked in
dharma, with child. That child grew in the princess of Malava, bull of the
Bharatas, like the lord of the stars in the sky in the bright half of the month.
When the time came, she gave birth to a daughter with lotus eyes, and the king
joyfully performed her rites. Because she had been given by Savitri, who was
pleased, and after offerings with the Savitri verse, the brahmins and her father
named her Savitri.

"The king's daughter grew up like Shri in bodily form, and in time the girl
reached her youth. Seeing her, slender-waisted and wide-hipped, like a golden
image, people thought, 'A daughter of the gods has come among us.' But no one
asked for that lotus-eyed girl, who seemed to blaze with splendour. Her
splendour held them back.

"Then she fasted, washed her head, went to the gods, made offerings in the fire
as was proper, and had the brahmins recite blessings at the festival. Then she
took the flowers left from the offering and went to her great-hearted father,
like the goddess Shri in bodily form. She greeted her father's feet, first
offered him the flowers, and stood at the king's side with joined palms, the
lovely one. When the king saw his daughter grown to youth, like a goddess, and
not asked for by any suitors, he was grieved.

"'Daughter,' said the king, 'it is time to give you in marriage, and no one asks
me for you. Look for a husband yourself, one equal to you in virtue. Tell me of
the man you want, and I shall think it over and give you to him. Choose as you
please. For I have heard the twice-born recite this from the treatise on dharma,
and you too hear it from me, fair one. The father who does not give his daughter
away is to be blamed, and the husband who does not go to his wife, and the son
who does not protect his mother when her husband is dead. Hear this from me and
hurry to look for a husband. Act so that I shall not be blamed by the gods.'

"When he had said this to his daughter, and to his old counsellors, he gave them
orders to go with her, and urged her, 'Go.' The high-minded girl greeted her
father's feet, as if shy, and understanding her father's words, set out without
hesitating. Mounted on a golden chariot, surrounded by old counsellors, she went
to the lovely ascetic forests of the royal seers. There she greeted the feet of
the old and honoured, and went through all the forests one by one, my son. So
the king's daughter went from place to place, giving gifts of wealth to the
chief brahmins at all the holy places.

---

"Then the king of Madra was sitting in the middle of his hall with Narada,
talking, Bharata. When she had gone to all the holy places and the hermitages,
Savitri came back to her father's house with the counsellors. When the lovely
girl saw her father sitting with Narada, she bowed her head to the feet of both.

"'Where has this daughter of yours been,' said Narada, 'and where has she come
from, king? Why do you not give her, a young woman, to a husband?'

"'I sent her out for that very purpose,' said Ashvapati, 'and she has come back
today. Hear from her, divine seer, what husband she has chosen.'

"Urged by her father, 'Tell it in full,' the lovely girl took his words as if
they were a god's, and said, 'Among the Shalvas there was a king whose self was
dharma, a kshatriya named Dyumatsena, who later went blind. When he had lost his
sight and his son was a child, a former enemy who lived nearby took his kingdom,
seeing his weakness. He went to the forest with his wife and their little child,
and in the great forest, keeping great vows, he practised austerity. His son,
born in the city and grown up in the ascetics' forest, is Satyavat. He is fit to
be my husband. I have chosen him in my heart.'

"'Alas,' said Narada, 'Savitri has done a very bad thing, king, in choosing,
without knowing it, the virtuous Satyavat. His father speaks the truth, and his
mother speaks the truth, so the brahmins named him Satyavat, "truthful". As a
boy he loved horses, and made horses of clay, and drew horses in pictures, so he
is also called Chitrashva.'

"'Is the prince, Satyavat, who delights his father, now full of energy?' said
the king. 'Is he wise, patient and brave?'

"'He is as full of energy as the sun,' said Narada, 'as wise as Brihaspati, as
brave as great Indra, as patient as the earth.'

"'Is Satyavat, the king's son, generous and kind to brahmins?' said Ashvapati.
'Is he handsome and noble and pleasant to look at?'

"'In giving, as far as he is able, he is equal to Rantideva, son of Sankriti,'
said Narada. 'He is kind to brahmins and truthful, like Shibi Aushinara. He is
noble like Yayati, as pleasant to look at as the moon, and in beauty like one of
the Ashvins, the mighty son of Dyumatsena. He is self-controlled, gentle, brave
and truthful, master of his senses, friendly, free of envy, modest and
steadfast. Uprightness is always in him, and his constancy is firm. In short, so
say those grown old in austerity and in good conduct.'

"'You tell me he has every virtue, lord,' said Ashvapati. 'Tell me his faults
too, if he has any.'

"'He has one fault, and no other,' said Narada. 'In a year from today Satyavat,
his life spent, will lay down his body.'

"'Come, Savitri,' said the king. 'Go and choose another, lovely one. He has one
great fault that outweighs his virtues. As the lord Narada, honoured by the
gods, tells me, in a year he will lay down his body, for his life is short.'

"'A share is allotted once,' said Savitri. 'A daughter is given once. Once a man
says, "I give." These three things happen once and once only. Long-lived or
short-lived, virtuous or without virtue, I have chosen my husband once, and I
will not choose a second. First one decides in the mind, then one says it in
words, then one does it in deeds. My mind is my authority in this.'

"'Your daughter Savitri's mind is firm, best of men,' said Narada. 'She cannot
be moved from this dharma in any way. The virtues that are in Satyavat are not
in any other man. So I am in favour of giving your daughter to him.'

"'What the lord has said is true and not to be questioned,' said the king. 'I
shall do it, for the lord is my teacher.'

"'May the giving of your daughter Savitri be free of obstacles,' said Narada.
'Now we shall go. Bless you all.' When he had said this, Narada flew up into the
sky and went to heaven, and the king had everything made ready for his
daughter's wedding.

---

"Thinking only of the giving of his daughter, the king gathered all the things
for the wedding. Then he called all the old brahmins and the priests with his
household priest, and on a holy day set out with his daughter. He came to the
sacred forest, to Dyumatsena's hermitage, and went on foot with the brahmins to
the royal seer. There they saw the greatly blessed king, blind, sitting on a mat
of kusha grass at the foot of a sal tree. The king honoured the royal seer as
was fitting, and with restrained speech made himself known. The one who knew
dharma offered him the guest-water, a seat and a cow, and the king said to the
king, 'Why have you come?' He told him all his purpose and what was to be done,
speaking of Satyavat.

"'This is my lovely daughter named Savitri, royal seer,' said Ashvapati. 'Take
her, you who know dharma, as your daughter-in-law, by your own dharma.'

"'We have fallen from the kingdom and live in the forest,' said Dyumatsena. 'We
practise dharma as disciplined ascetics. How will your daughter, who does not
deserve it, bear this hardship of forest life in the hermitage?'

"'My daughter knows, as I do, that happiness and sorrow come and go,' said
Ashvapati. 'Such words do not suit one like me. I have come to you with my mind
made up, king. You should not kill my hope, out of friendship and affection. You
should not refuse me, who have come to you in love. You are a fit match for me,
and I for you. Take my daughter as your daughter-in-law, the wife of Satyavat.'

"'I wished for this alliance with you long ago,' said Dyumatsena, 'but I
thought, "I have lost my kingdom," and so I hesitated. Let the wish I had long
ago be fulfilled today, for you are a guest I have longed for.'

"Then the two kings called all the brahmins who lived in the hermitage and had
the wedding performed as the rites prescribe. When Ashvapati had given his
daughter and a fitting dowry, he went back to his own house, full of the
greatest joy. Satyavat rejoiced at winning a wife with every virtue, and she
rejoiced at winning the husband her heart had wanted. When her father had gone,
she laid aside all her ornaments and took up bark and ochre cloth. With her
service and her virtues, her courtesy and self-control, and by doing all they
wished, she pleased them all. She pleased her mother-in-law by caring for her
body and with clothing and all such things, and her father-in-law by her worship
of the gods and by her restrained speech. In the same way she pleased her
husband with kind words, with skill and calm, and with her care in private.

"So while those good people lived there in the hermitage practising austerity,
some time went by, Bharata. But whether Savitri lay down or stood, by day or by
night, the words Narada had spoken stayed in her mind.

---

"Then, when a long time had passed, the time came when Satyavat was to die,
king. As Savitri counted each day that went by, the words Narada had spoken were
always in her heart. Thinking, 'He must die on the fourth day,' the lovely woman
took a vow of three nights and stood day and night.

"When he heard of his daughter-in-law's painful discipline, the king was
grieved. He rose and said to Savitri, comforting her, 'This is a very harsh
thing you have undertaken, princess. To stand for three nights is very hard.'

"'Do not be troubled, father,' said Savitri. 'I shall complete my vow. It is
done by resolve, and resolve is what achieves it.'

"'I cannot tell you in any way to break your vow,' said Dyumatsena. 'One like me
should rightly say, "Complete it."'

"When he had said this, great-minded Dyumatsena fell silent, and Savitri,
standing, looked like a thing of wood. The night before her husband's death
passed for Savitri, bull of the Bharatas, as she stood there in sorrow. 'Today
is the day,' she thought, and she made offerings into the blazing fire when the
sun had risen a yoke's length, and did the morning rites. Then she greeted all
the old brahmins, and her mother-in-law and father-in-law, in turn, and stood
with joined palms, restrained. All the ascetics who lived in the ascetics'
forest spoke blessings for Savitri's good, auspicious blessings that she would
never be widowed. 'So be it,' said Savitri, intent on the yoga of meditation,
and accepted all the ascetics' words in her mind. Waiting for that time and that
hour, the king's daughter, deeply grieved, thought of Narada's words as he had
spoken them.

"Then her mother-in-law and father-in-law said to the king's daughter, who stood
apart, with affection, best of the Bharatas, 'You have completed your vow as it
was taught, and as it should be. The time for eating has come. Do what comes
next.'

"'I shall eat when the sun has set and my wish is fulfilled,' said Savitri.
'That is the resolve in my heart, and the promise I have made.'

"As Savitri was speaking about food, Satyavat put an axe on his shoulder and set
out for the forest. Savitri said to her husband, 'You should not go alone. I
shall come with you. I cannot bear to leave you.'

"'You have never been to the forest before,' said Satyavat, 'and the path is
hard, lovely one. You are worn thin with your vow and fasting. How will you go
on foot?'

"'I feel no weakness from fasting,' said Savitri, 'and I am not tired. I have
set my heart on going. You should not stop me.'

"'If you have set your heart on going,' said Satyavat, 'I shall do what pleases
you. But ask my elders, so that no blame touches me.'

"She who kept great vows went to her mother-in-law and father-in-law and said,
'My husband is going to the great forest for fruits. I would like leave from
you, my lady, and from my father-in-law, to go out with him. I cannot bear to be
parted from him. Your son has set out for the sake of his elders and the
agnihotra. He must not be stopped. He could be stopped only if he had set out to
the forest for some other reason. For almost a year I have not left the
hermitage, and I long to see the forest in flower.'

"'Since Savitri was given by her father to be my daughter-in-law,' said
Dyumatsena, 'I do not remember her ever asking for anything. So let my
daughter-in-law have her wish as she desires. But be careful, daughter, on
Satyavat's way.'

"With leave from both, the glorious woman went with her husband, as if laughing,
but with a heart in pain. The wide-eyed woman saw lovely, many-coloured forests
all around, loud with the cries of peacocks. 'Look,' said Satyavat to Savitri in
sweet words, at the holy rivers and the great trees in flower. The faultless
woman watched her husband in every moment, and remembering the sage's words, she
thought of him as already dead. She followed her husband, walking gently, her
heart split in two, waiting for that time."
