# Mudgala

Eleven years went by in hardship for the great Pandavas as they lived in the
forest, bull among the Bharatas. Those best of men, who deserved happiness, ate
fruits and roots and bore the greatest hardship, thinking of the time that would
come.

But the royal seer Yudhishthira, strong-armed, brooded on his brothers' great
hardship, which had come from the wrong he himself had done, and he did not
sleep in peace. It was as if arrows had been driven into his heart. He saw the
wickedness that had come from the dice game in its time, and he remembered the
suta's son's harsh words. The Pandava was wretched, sighing again and again,
carrying a great poison of anger.

Arjuna, the twins, glorious Draupadi and mighty Bhima, the strongest of all,
looked to Yudhishthira and bore their very great hardship. Thinking that only a
little time was left, the bulls among men seemed to take on a new body, full of
energy and anger.

After some time Vyasa, the son of Satyavati, the great yogin, came to see the
Pandavas. When Kunti's son Yudhishthira saw him coming, he went out to meet the
great one and received him with due rites. When he was seated, the joy of the
Pandavas sat close by him, eager to serve him, his senses held in check, and
pleased Vyasa by bowing to him.

The great seer looked at his grandsons, thin, living in the forest on what the
forest gave. In pity he spoke in a voice choked with tears.

"Yudhishthira, strong-armed one, best of those who keep dharma, listen. Those
who have not practised austerity do not win great happiness, my son. A man meets
happiness and sorrow in turn. No one meets endless unhappiness, bull among men.
The wise man, joined to the highest understanding, who knows rising and setting,
does not grieve and does not rejoice. Let him enjoy happiness when it comes and
bear sorrow when it comes. Let him wait on what the time brings, as a farmer
waits on his crops.

"There is nothing higher than austerity. By austerity one wins greatness. Know,
Bharata, that nothing is beyond the reach of austerity. Truth, straightness,
freedom from anger, sharing, self-control and calm, freedom from envy,
harmlessness, purity and the restraint of the senses: these are the means, great
king, of men who do good deeds. Foolish people who delight in what is against
dharma, bound for the way of beasts, fall into a hard birth and find no
happiness. The deed done here is enjoyed in the next world. So a man should yoke
his body to austerity and discipline.

"Let him give as much as he can, with honour and a bow, gladly, at the right
time and to the right person, free of envy, king. The man who speaks the truth
wins long life, and the straight man a life without toil. The man without anger
and envy wins the highest contentment. The self-controlled man, always intent on
calm, meets no hardship, and does not burn when he sees fortune in another's
hands. The man who shares and gives becomes rich in pleasures and happiness, and
the harmless man enjoys the best of health. The man who honours those who
deserve honour is born in a great family, and the man who has conquered his
senses does not meet with disasters. Joined to the law of time, he whose mind
leans towards the good is born again with that bent, and so he has a good mind."

"Lord, great sage," said Yudhishthira, "of the dharma of giving and of
austerity, which brings more good after death? Which is said to be harder?"

"There is nothing on earth harder than giving," said Vyasa. "Men have a great
thirst for wealth, and it is won with pain. For the sake of wealth brave men
give up their dear lives and go into great battles, into the sea and into the
wilderness. Some men take to farming and keeping cows. Some go out as servants,
wanting wealth. To give up what has been earned with such pain is very hard.
Nothing is harder than giving, and so I hold giving highest.

"But there is a distinction to be understood here. Let a man give wealth that
has been rightly earned, to good people, at the right place and time and to the
right person. The dharma of giving done with wealth wrongly gained does not save
the doer from great danger. A gift given to the right person at the right time,
even a very small one, with a wholly pure mind, is said to bear endless fruit
after death. On this they tell an old story, of the fruit that Mudgala won by
giving away a drona of rice."

---

"How did that great man give away a drona of rice?" said Yudhishthira. "To whom
was it given, lord, and in what way? Tell me. I think the life of that man who
walked in true dharma bore fruit, since the lord who sees dharma before his eyes
was pleased with his deeds."

"There was a man named Mudgala at Kurukshetra, king," said Vyasa. "His self was
dharma, and he lived by gleaning. He was strict in his vows, truthful and free
of envy. He kept the vow of the guest and did the rites. He lived the pigeon's
way, and the great ascetic kept the sacrifice called ishtikrita. With his son
and wife the sage ate once a fortnight. Living the pigeon's way, in a fortnight
he gathered a drona of rice. He performed the rites of the new moon and the full
moon without envy, and he kept his body alive on what was left over from the
gods and his guests. At every festival Indra himself, lord of the three worlds,
with the gods, took his share, great king.

"When he had kept the festival, living as a sage, he gave food to his guests
with a glad heart. On that day, as the great man gave food from his drona of
rice, what was left grew at the sight of a guest, since he had no envy. Hundreds
of wise brahmins would eat from it, and yet by the purity of the sage's giving
that food went on increasing.

"Then Durvasas heard of Mudgala, most devoted to dharma and strict in his vows,
king. Durvasas, clothed in the sky, came to him. He wore no settled dress, like
a madman, Pandava, and his hair was loose, and the sage said harsh words of many
kinds. He came up to the brahmin, and the best of sages said, 'Know, best of
sages, that I have come here wanting food.'

"'Welcome to you,' Mudgala said to the sage. He offered water for his feet and
for sipping, and then gave the hungry man excellent food, won by austerity. The
man who kept the vow of the guest, firm in his vows, gave it with the greatest
faith to the madman. Then the madman, full of hunger, ate all that tasty food,
and Mudgala gave it to him. When he had eaten it all, he smeared his limbs with
the leavings and went back the way he had come.

"In the same way, when the next festival came round, he came and ate all the
food of the wise man who lived by gleaning. The sage, without food, gathered
grain again. Hunger could not make Mudgala change. No anger, no envy, no
disrespect and no agitation came into the best of brahmins as he gleaned with
his son and wife. In the same way, having made up his mind, Durvasas, best of
sages, came to Mudgala, whose dharma was gleaning, at the proper time six times
over. But the sage saw no change at all in his mind. He saw that the mind of
that pure-souled man was pure and without stain.

"Then the sage was pleased and said to Mudgala, 'There is no giver like you in
this world, free of envy. Hunger drives out the sense of dharma and takes away
firmness. The tongue, following the senses, drags a man towards tastes. Life
comes from food, and the mind is restless and hard to hold. The one-pointedness
of mind and senses is, beyond doubt, austerity. To give up with a pure heart
what has been won with toil is hard. You have done all of it, good man, as it
should be done. We are pleased and favoured to have met you. Conquest of the
senses, firmness, sharing, self-control and calm, compassion, truth and dharma:
all of it is set firm in you. You have won the worlds by your deeds, and you
have reached the highest state. Ah! Your gift is proclaimed far and wide by the
dwellers in heaven. You will go to heaven in your body, you of good vows.'

"As the sage Durvasas was saying this, a messenger of the gods came to Mudgala
in a flying chariot. It was yoked with geese and cranes, hung with nets of
little bells, going where it was wished, wonderful to see and filled with a
heavenly scent. He said to the brahmin seer, 'Mount this chariot, won by your
deeds. You have reached the highest perfection, sage.'

"The seer said to the messenger of the gods as he spoke, 'I want to hear from
you about the qualities of those who live in heaven. What are the qualities of
those who live there? What is their austerity, and what is their resolve? What
is the happiness of heaven in heaven, and what is its fault, messenger of the
gods? The good, of good family, say that friendship between good men is made in
seven steps. Putting that friendship first, I ask you, lord. Tell me without
hesitation what is true and good here. When I have heard it, I shall make my
decision by your words.'"

---

"Great seer," said the messenger of the gods, "you think like a child, weighing
the highest happiness of heaven when it has come to you, when you should prize
it greatly, as if you did not know better. That world which is called heaven is
above. It goes upward, a good road, always travelled by the gods' path, sage.
Those who have not practised austerity, who have not offered great sacrifices,
liars and unbelievers do not go there, Mudgala. Men whose self is dharma, who
have conquered themselves, calm, self-controlled, free of envy, devoted to the
dharma of giving, and heroes who bear the marks of battle go there, brahmin.
They have done the foremost deeds, made up of calm and self-control, and they go
to the worlds of those who have done good, where good men dwell.

"There, Maudgalya, are the gods, the Sadhyas and the Vishve Devas, the Maruts
with the great seers, the Yamas and the Dhamas, and the gandharvas and
apsarases. For each of these orders of gods there are many separate worlds,
shining, full of every wish, made of light and lovely. There is golden Meru,
king of mountains, thirty-three thousand yojanas high, where the gardens of the
gods are, Mudgala, holy Nandana and the rest, the pleasure grounds of those who
have done good. There is no hunger or thirst, no weariness, no fear of cold or
heat. There is nothing loathsome or foul, and no sickness at all. Everywhere
there are pleasing scents and touches that please, and sounds that please the
ear and the mind, sage. There is no grief and no old age there, no toil and no
lament.

"Such is that world, sage. It comes from the fruit of a man's own deeds. Men are
born there by their own good deeds. Those who arrive there have bodies of light,
Maudgalya, born of their deeds, not of mother and father. There is no sweat
there, no bad smell, no dung or urine. Dust does not soil their clothes, sage.
Their garlands, with heavenly scents, lovely, do not fade, and such as they are
carried about in flying chariots, brahmin. Free of envy, grief and weariness,
without delusion or spite, those who have won heaven live happily there, great
sage.

"Above those worlds, best of sages, are the worlds of Shakra, one above another,
full of heavenly qualities. Beyond them, brahmin, are the lovely worlds of
Brahma, made of light, where seers go who have been purified by their own good
deeds. There are others there named Ribhus, gods even of the gods. Their worlds
are further still, and the gods sacrifice to them here. Those worlds shine with
their own light, bright, beyond, granting every wish. They suffer no torment
caused by women, and no envy of the lordship of the worlds. They do not live on
offerings, nor do they eat amrita. They have divine bodies, yet not bodies with
form. They are the eternal gods of the gods, who do not long for happiness in
happiness. They do not turn with the turning of the aeons. How could there be
old age or death for them? They have no joy, no delight, no pleasure. They have
no sorrow and no happiness. How could they have passion or hatred, sage? That
highest state is longed for even by the gods, Maudgalya. It is the supreme
perfection, hard to win, beyond the reach of those who move among desires.

"These are the thirty-three worlds. The other worlds are reached by the wise
through the best disciplines, or through gifts given with due rites. You have
won this happy dawn, brought about by your gift. Enjoy it, won by your good
deeds, shining with the light of your austerity. This is the happiness of
heaven, brahmin, and these are its many kinds of worlds. I have told you the
qualities of heaven. Now hear its faults from me.

"In heaven one enjoys the fruit of deeds already done, and no other deed is
done. It is enjoyed until it is cut off at the root. That I think is its fault,
and the fall at its end. And there is the fall of those whose minds are filled
with happiness, Mudgala. There is the discontent and the burning pain that come
from seeing brighter fortunes, which is hard for those who stand in a lower
place. There is the dimming of the senses of those who fall, and their being
overcome by passion. And when their garlands fade, there is the fear of those
about to fall. These cruel faults, Maudgalya, reach as far as the house of
Brahma. But the good qualities of those who have done good in the world of
heaven are beyond counting.

"And this is another, the best quality of those who fall from heaven, sage:
because of the good that clings to them they are born among men. There too a man
is born very fortunate and happy. But if he does not wake up there, he goes down
lower. The deed done here is enjoyed in the next world. This is held to be the
field of deeds, brahmin, and that the field of fruits. I have told you all you
asked me, Mudgala. Now, out of kindness to you, good man, let us go, and
quickly."

---

"When he heard this," said Vyasa, "Maudgalya weighed the words in his mind. When
he had weighed them, the best of sages said to the messenger of the gods,
'Messenger of the gods, I bow to you. Go, my friend, as you please. I have no
use for heaven or its happiness, with its great fault. That fall is a great
sorrow, and the pain of it is very cruel. Those who enjoy heaven fall back here,
and so I do not want heaven. I will seek only that place where, once they have
gone there, they do not grieve, or suffer, or move.'

"When he had said this, the sage sent the messenger of the gods away. He gave up
living by gleaning and took up the highest calm. Blame and praise became the
same to him, and a clod, a stone and gold were equal. With pure knowledge and
yoga he was always in meditation. By the yoga of meditation he won strength and
the highest power, and went to the eternal, highest perfection, whose mark is
nirvana.

"So you too, son of Kunti, should not grieve. You have fallen from a rich
kingdom, but you will win it back by austerity. After happiness comes sorrow,
and after sorrow happiness. They come round to a man in turn, like the spokes of
a wheel to the rim. You will win the kingdom of your fathers and grandfathers,
you of boundless courage, after the thirteenth year. Let the fever of your mind
pass."

When he had said this to the joy of the Pandavas, the wise lord Vyasa went back
to his hermitage for austerity.
