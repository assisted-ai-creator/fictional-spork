# Hiranyapura

"As I was coming back," said Arjuna, "I saw another great thing: a divine city
that went where it wished, as bright as fire and the sun. It had trees made of
jewels and bright, shining birds, and the Paulomas and Kalakeyas lived in it,
always joyful. It had gate towers and turrets and four gates, and it was hard to
approach. It was divine, made of every jewel, wonderful to see, surrounded by
trees of divine jewels bearing flowers and fruit. It had lovely divine birds,
and all round it were asuras, always glad, garlanded, with spears, lances,
clubs, bows and hammers in their hands. When I saw that wonderful city of the
daityas, king, I asked Matali, 'What is this I see?'

"'There was a daitya woman named Puloma, and a great asura woman named Kalaka,'
said Matali. 'They practised the highest austerity for a thousand years of the
gods. At the end of their austerity the Self-born gave them a boon. They chose
this boon: that their sons should have little sorrow, and that gods, rakshasas
and snakes should not be able to kill them, king of kings. And Brahma made this
lovely city for the Kalakeyas, best of the Bharatas. It flies through the sky,
bright with good deeds, full of every jewel. Not even the immortals can assail
it, with the hosts of yakshas and gandharvas, the snakes, asuras and rakshasas.
It is rich in every good thing one could wish for, free of sorrow and sickness.
This divine city flies through the sky, where the immortals do not come, and the
Paulomas and the danava Kalakeyas live in it, hero. This great city is called
Hiranyapura, and it is guarded by the Kalakeyas and the great asura Paulomas.
They live here always glad, king of kings, free of fear and free of care, and
none of the gods can kill them. But Brahma long ago named a human as their
death.'

"When I knew that gods and asuras could not kill them, lord, I said gladly to
Matali, 'Drive quickly to that city. I will destroy with my weapons these haters
of the lord of the thirty gods. For there are no evil ones who hate the gods
whom I cannot kill.' So Matali quickly brought me near Hiranyapura in that
divine chariot yoked with bays.

"When they saw me, the sons of Diti, in bright ornaments and clothes, rose up at
great speed, mounted their chariots in their armour. Then the lords of the
danavas, in anger, fierce in courage, struck at me with nalika arrows and iron
shafts, broad-headed arrows, lances, swords and javelins. I held back that great
rain of weapons with a great rain of weapons, trusting in the strength of my
learning. Ranging the battle along the chariot paths I bewildered them all, and
the danavas, bewildered, struck each other down. As they ran at one another in
their bewilderment I cut off their heads with blazing arrows by hundreds.

"As I struck them down, the sons of Diti took refuge in their city again and
flew up into the sky with it, using the magic of the danavas. Then I held them
back with a great rain of arrows, blocking the daityas' road and stopping their
going. That divine city that flies through the sky, going where it will, with
divine splendour, was held up by the daityas at their ease through the boon. Now
it fell under the earth, now it rose up again. Now it went swiftly sideways, now
it sank in the water. I held that city, like Amaravati, going where it wished,
with weapons of many kinds, lord of men. Then, bull of the Bharatas, with a net
of arrows driven by divine weapons, I held the city fast with the daityas. Torn
by the straight iron arrows I loosed, the asura city fell to the earth, king,
broken. Struck by my iron arrows with the force of thunderbolts, the asuras,
driven by Time, wandered about, king.

"Then Matali quickly came down to the earth ahead of us, as if falling, in the
chariot as bright as the sun. Then sixty thousand chariots of those unforgiving
ones, eager to fight me, came round me, Bharata. I struck them down with sharp
arrows winged with vulture feathers, and they came back in the battle like the
waves of the ocean. Thinking, 'These cannot be beaten in battle by a human,' I
set all my weapons to work one after another. Then those thousands of chariots,
with their wonderful fighters, turned back my divine weapons, as if gently. The
great chariot fighters could be seen in the battle by hundreds and thousands,
ranging along many wonderful chariot paths. With their bright diadems and
crests, their bright armour and banners and their bright ornaments, they almost
delighted my heart. But I could not hurt them in the battle with rains of arrows
driven by weapons, and they hurt me. Pressed by many of them, skilled in battle
and masters of weapons, I was shaken in that great battle, and a great fear came
over me.

"Then, in the battle, I bowed to Rudra, the god of gods. Saying, 'Good fortune
to all creatures,' I set to the bow the great weapon known as the Raudra, the
destroyer of all enemies. Then I saw a man with three heads and nine eyes, three
faces and six arms. He was blazing, his hair flaming like the sun, his heads
crowned with great snakes flicking their tongues, slayer of enemies. Then, free
of fear, I brought that terrible, eternal Raudra weapon to Gandiva, bull of the
Bharatas. Bowing to the three-eyed Sharva of boundless splendour, I loosed it,
Bharata, to bring down the lords of the danavas.

"As soon as it was loosed there were forms in thousands, lord of the people.
There were deer, lions and tigers, bears, buffalo, snakes and cattle. There were
elephants, yaks and sharabhas everywhere, bulls, boars and cats, jackals, ghosts
and bhurundas everywhere. There were vultures, garudas and makaras, pishachas
with yakshas, and enemies of the gods. There were guhyakas in the battle and
nairritas, jhashas, elephant-faced ones and owls. There were shoals of fish and
tortoises bearing weapons and swords of many kinds, and yatudhanas bearing maces
and hammers. When that weapon was loosed, the whole world was filled with these
and many others of many forms. The danavas who had come together were struck
down again and again by beings with three heads, four tusks, four faces and four
arms, of many forms, eating flesh, fat and marrow. In a moment, Bharata, I
killed all the danavas with other arrows too, which split enemies apart, bright
as the sun and fire, gleaming like the thunderbolt, made of the hardest stone.

"When I saw them fallen from the sky, lifeless, driven down by the weapon from
Gandiva, I bowed again to the Creator, the slayer of Tripura. When the
charioteer of the gods saw them crushed by the Raudra weapon, adorned with
divine ornaments, he felt the greatest joy. Seeing this deed done, unbearable,
which even the gods could hardly attempt, Matali, Shakra's charioteer, honoured
me. Pleased, with joined hands, he said, 'You have done a deed that gods and
asuras could not bear to do. Not even the lord of the gods could do it in
battle. This great city that flies in the sky, which gods and asuras could not
destroy, you have crushed, hero, by your own courage, your weapons and the
strength of your austerity.'

"When the city was destroyed and the danavas killed, all the women came out of
the city wailing. With their hair loose, in pain, grieving like ospreys, they
fell on the ground, mourning their sons, fathers and brothers. They wept and
wailed, their voices broken, their lords killed, beating their breasts with
their hands, their garlands and ornaments slipping. The city of the danavas,
full of grief, without glory, struck with pain and misery, without splendour and
without lords, no longer shone. Like a city of the gandharvas, like a lake whose
elephants have been killed, like a forest of dry trees, the city vanished from
sight.

"Then Matali quickly brought me, glad at heart, my work done, from the battle to
the house of the king of the gods. When I had broken Hiranyapura and killed the
great asuras and the Nivatakavachas, I came to Shakra. Matali told the lord of
the gods all my deed at length, just as it happened, you of great splendour. He
told of the destruction of Hiranyapura, the turning back of the magic, and the
killing in battle of the mighty Nivatakavachas. When he heard it, the blessed
thousand-eyed Purandara, glorious, was pleased, and with the Maruts he said,
'Well done! Well done!' Then the king of the gods comforted me again and again,
and said these very sweet words together with the gods: 'You have done in battle
a deed beyond gods and asuras, Partha. By killing my enemies you have paid a
great teacher's fee. So you must always be, Dhananjaya, steady in battle, and
use the weapons without confusion. For in battle you cannot be withstood by
gods, danavas and rakshasas, with the yakshas, asuras and gandharvas, the hosts
of birds and the snakes. And the righteous Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, will rule
the earth won by the strength of your arms, son of Kunti.'

---

"Then I trusted him, and my arrow wounds had healed. The king of the gods,
showing me favour, said to me at the right time, 'All the divine weapons are in
you, Bharata. No human on earth can overcome you. When you stand in battle, my
son, Bhishma, Drona, Kripa, Karna and Shakuni, with their kings, are not worth a
sixteenth part of you.' And the lord Maghavan gave me this armour for my body,
the divine armour that cannot be pierced, and a golden garland. The god gave me
the conch Devadatta, of great sound, and Indra himself set this divine diadem on
me. Then Shakra gave me divine clothes and divine ornaments, lovely and large.
So, honoured there, I lived happily in Indra's holy house with the young
gandharvas, king.

"Then Shakra, pleased, said to me with the immortals, 'It is time for you to go,
Arjuna, for your brothers are thinking of you.' So I lived five years in Indra's
house, Bharata, king, remembering the strife born of the dice. Then I came to
Gandhamadana and saw you, surrounded by your brothers, on the top of this
mountain."

"How fortunate, Dhananjaya, that you have won the weapons, Bharata," said
Yudhishthira, "and how fortunate that you have pleased the king, the lord of the
gods. How fortunate, scorcher of enemies, that you have seen the blessed Sthanu
himself, with the goddess, and pleased him with a good fight, sinless one. How
fortunate that you have met the guardians of the world, bull of the Bharatas.
How fortunate we all are, and how fortunate that you have come back. Today I
think this whole earth, garlanded with cities, is won, and the sons of
Dhritarashtra brought under our power. But I want to see those divine weapons of
yours, Bharata, with which you killed the mighty Nivatakavachas."

"Tomorrow at dawn you will see all the divine weapons," said Arjuna, "with which
I struck down the terrible Nivatakavachas."

When Dhananjaya had told them of his coming there, he spent that night with all
his brothers.

---

When that night was over, the Dharma King Yudhishthira rose. He did the
necessary rites with his brothers. Then he urged Arjuna, the joy of his
brothers, "Show us the weapons with which you conquered the danavas, son of
Kunti."

Then Dhananjaya the Pandava showed those divine weapons the gods had given him,
Bharata. The one of great splendour had made himself pure in the highest degree,
as is proper. He stood in a chariot that was the earth, with a mountain for its
pole, trees for its limbs, fine bamboos for its three bamboo posts, and
Dhananjaya shone in it. Then, well clad in that shining armour, taking the bow
Gandiva and Devadatta, born of the water, the strong-armed son of Kunti,
shining, began to show those divine weapons one after another.

As he was about to use the divine weapons, the earth, trodden by his feet, shook
with its trees. The rivers and the great ocean were stirred, the mountains
split, and the wind did not blow. The thousand-rayed sun did not shine, fire did
not burn, and the Vedas did not come to the minds of the twice-born at all. The
creatures that live under the earth, Janamejaya, rose up in pain and surrounded
the Pandava. Trembling, their hands joined, their faces covered, burning under
those weapons, they all begged Dhananjaya. Then the brahmin seers and the
siddhas, the divine seers and all moving creatures stood there. The finest royal
seers, the dwellers in heaven, the yakshas, rakshasas and gandharvas and the
birds all stood there. Then the Grandfather came, and all the guardians of the
world, and the blessed great god with his hosts.

Then the Wind, great king, scattered bright, fragrant divine garlands all round
the Pandavas. Gandharvas, urged by the gods, sang songs of many kinds, and hosts
of apsarases danced in troops, king. At that time of turmoil Narada, urged by
the gods, came and said to the Partha these words, worth hearing, king. "Arjuna,
Arjuna, do not use the divine weapons, Bharata. They must never be used where
there is no target. Nor should a man use them on a target unless he is in
distress. There is very great harm in using the weapons, joy of the Kurus. If
they are guarded as the teaching says, Dhananjaya, they will be strong and bring
happiness, no doubt of it. If they are not guarded, Pandava, they bring
destruction even to the three worlds. Never do this again anywhere. And you,
Ajatashatru, will see them in battle, used by the Partha to crush your enemies."

When they had stopped the Partha, all the gods, and all the others who had come
there, went back as they had come, bull among men. When they had all gone back,
joy of the Kurus, the Pandavas lived gladly in that same forest with Krishna.
