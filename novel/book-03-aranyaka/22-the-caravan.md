# The Caravan

"When the woman flawless in every limb heard the caravan leader's words," said
Brihadashva, "she went with them, longing to see her husband. After many days,
in the great, cruel forest, the merchants saw a large, lovely lake, good in
every way, fragrant with lotuses. There was plenty of grass and firewood, with
many roots and fruits, and it was crowded with birds of every kind. When they
saw it, with its sweet water, charming and pleasant, their animals were worn
out, and they decided to camp. With the caravan leader's consent they went into
that fine wood, and the great caravan camped there, on its western shore.

"Then at midnight, when all was silent and still and the tired caravan slept, a
herd of elephants came to drink at the mountain stream, muddy with the flow of
their rut. The fine caravan lay asleep across their path to the lotus pool, and
the elephants suddenly trampled it as it slept and struggled on the ground.
Blind with sleep and in great fear, the men of the caravan cried out, 'Help!
Help!', looking for refuge, and ran for the thickets of the forest. Some men
were killed by tusks, some by trunks, some by feet. That caravan, full of oxen,
donkeys, camels and horses, crowded with people on foot, ran in terror and
struck at itself. Crying out in terror, men fell to the ground. Some were caught
in the trees and broken, some fell into rough places. So that whole rich caravan
was destroyed.

"When the next day came, the people left alive came out of the thickets,
grieving for the slaughter, for a brother, a father, a son or a friend, lord of
the people. And there the princess of Vidarbha grieved. 'What evil have I done?
This sea of people that I found in the empty forest has been killed by a herd of
elephants, surely because of my bad luck alone. I must have a long sorrow still
to bear, beyond doubt. No one dies before his time; that is the teaching of the
elders I have heard. That is why I was not crushed today by the herd of
elephants, in all my misery. For nothing happens to men here that is not made by
fate. And even as a child I did no wrong, in deed, thought or word, that this
sorrow should come to me. I think it was the guardians of the world, who came to
my bridegroom choice. I rejected those gods there for Nala's sake. Surely it is
by their power that I have been parted from him.'

"When she had lamented these and other sorrows, the fine woman went on, lost in
sorrow and grief, tiger among kings, with some of the brahmins left alive,
masters of the Veda. Going on for a long time, she came at evening to the great
city of Subahu, the truthful king of the Chedis. Wrapped in her cut half cloth,
she entered that fine city.

"The people of the city saw her walking like a madwoman, pale, thin and
wretched, her hair loose and unwashed. As she came into the city of the king of
the Chedis, the village boys followed her, curious. Surrounded by them, she came
near the king's palace. The king's mother, up on the terrace, saw her surrounded
by the crowd. She had the crowd sent away and had Damayanti brought up to the
fine terrace, king, and asked her in wonder, 'Though you are so unhappy, your
body is very beautiful. You shine like lightning in the clouds. Tell me who you
are and whose you are. Your beauty is not human, even without ornaments. You are
alone, and you are not afraid of men, you who shine like an immortal.'

"When she heard her words, Bhima's daughter said, 'Know that I am a woman
devoted to her husband. I am a sairandhri, of good birth, a serving woman who
lives as she chooses, alone, eating roots and fruit, sleeping wherever evening
finds me. My husband has countless virtues, and he was always devoted to me. And
I followed that hero, my husband, always, like his shadow.

"'By fate he became much too attached to dice. He was beaten at the game and
went alone into the forest. I followed my husband into the forest, the hero, in
his one garment, bewildered, like a madman, comforting him. Once in the forest,
for some reason, the hero, starving and in despair, lost even that one garment.
I followed him, naked and mad and out of his mind, and for many nights I never
slept. Then after a long time he left me somewhere as I slept. He cut my cloth
in half and abandoned me, though I had done no wrong. I search for my husband,
burning day and night, and I cannot find him, my love, who shines like an
immortal, the lord of my life and wealth.'

"As Bhima's daughter lamented like this, her eyes full of tears, the king's
mother, herself in greater pain, said to her in her pain, 'Stay with me, good
lady. I have come to love you. My men will search for your husband, good woman.
Or he may come here himself, as he wanders here and there. Living here, good
lady, you will find your husband.'

"When she heard the king's mother's words, Damayanti said, 'I can live with you,
mother of heroes, on certain terms. I will not eat what others leave, nor wash
anyone's feet, and I will not speak to other men at all. If any man courts me,
he must be punished by you. And I must see brahmins, to search for my husband.
If it can be so here, I will stay, beyond doubt. If not, I have no heart to stay
anywhere.'

"The king's mother said to her gladly, 'I will do all this. It is good that you
keep such a vow.' When she had said this to Bhima's daughter, lord of the
people, the king's mother said to her own daughter, named Sunanda, Bharata,
'Sunanda, take this sairandhri, who looks like a goddess, for your own. Enjoy
her company, with your mind at ease.'

---

"When he had left Damayanti, lord of the people, King Nala saw a great fire
burning in the deep forest. In the middle of the fire he heard the voice of some
creature, calling loudly again and again, 'Run to me, Nala! King of good name!'

"'Do not be afraid,' said Nala, and he went into the middle of the fire. There
he saw a king of snakes lying coiled. The snake joined his hands and, trembling,
said to Nala, 'Know me, king, as the snake Karkotaka, lord of men. I deceived a
brahmin seer, a great ascetic who had done no wrong, and he cursed me in his
anger, lord of men. Because of his curse I cannot move a single step. I will
tell you what is good for you. You should save me. I will be your friend. There
is no snake like me. I will make myself light for you. Pick me up and go
quickly.'

"When he had said this, the lord of snakes became the size of a thumb. Nala took
him and went to a place free of the fire. When he came to an open space, clear
of the fire's black track, he wanted to put him down. But the snake Karkotaka
spoke again. 'Walk on, Naishadha, counting some of your steps. There I will do
you the greatest good, great king.'

"Nala began to count, and at the tenth step the snake bit him. As soon as he was
bitten, his form vanished. Nala stood amazed when he saw himself changed, and
the king saw the snake in his own shape. Then the snake Karkotaka comforted Nala
and said, 'I have hidden your form, so that people will not know you. The one
because of whom you have been changed, Nala, and brought to great sorrow, will
live in sorrow in you because of my poison. As long as he does not let you go,
great king, he will live in sorrow in you, his limbs wrapped in poison. Out of
anger at him, who wronged you, a sinless man, lord of men, though you did not
deserve it, I have given you protection. You will have no fear of fanged beasts
or of enemies, tiger among men, nor of those who know the Veda, lord of men, by
my favour. And you will feel no pain from poison, king. And in battles, king of
kings, you will always win.

"'Go from here, king, calling yourself the charioteer Bahuka, to Rituparna, for
he is skilled at dice. Go today, lord of the Nishadhas, to the lovely city of
Ayodhya. The king will give you the secret of the dice in return for the secret
of horses. The splendid king, born of the Ikshvaku line, will be your friend
too. When you know the dice, you will come to good fortune. You will be joined
again with your wife. Do not set your mind on grief. You will have your kingdom
again and your two children. I tell you the truth.

"'When you wish to see your own form, lord of men, you must remember me, and put
on this garment. Covered with this garment, you will take your own form again.'
With these words he gave him a pair of divine garments.

"When he had instructed Nala and given him the garments, Kaurava, the king of
snakes vanished there and then, king.

---

"When the snake had vanished, Nala the Naishadha set out, and on the tenth day
he entered Rituparna's city. He went to the king, saying, 'I am Bahuka. In
driving horses there is no one on earth to equal me. I should be asked in hard
matters and in delicate ones. I know how to prepare food better than others.
Whatever crafts there are in this world, and whatever else is very hard to do, I
will try to do it all. Take me into your service, Rituparna.'

"'Stay, Bahuka, bless you,' said Rituparna. 'You will do all this. My mind has
always been set above all on driving fast. See that my horses become fast. You
are master of the horses, and your pay is ten thousand. These two, Varshneya and
Jivala, will always wait on you. You will be happy with them. Stay with me,
Bahuka.'

"When he said this, Nala lived there, honoured, in Rituparna's city, with
Varshneya and Jivala. The king lived there, always thinking of the princess of
Vidarbha, and every evening he sang this one verse:

"'Where does she sleep, the poor woman, hungry and thirsty and tired, thinking
of that fool? Whom does she wait on now?'

"As the king said this one night, Jivala said to him, 'Who is this woman you
grieve for all the time? I want to hear, Bahuka.'

"King Nala said to him, 'There was a certain fool whose wife was greatly
honoured, and he was closer still to her. For some reason the fool was parted
from her. Parted from her, the fool wanders, tormented by unhappiness. Burning
with grief day and night, never resting, he remembers her at nightfall and sings
one verse. He has wandered the whole earth, found something somewhere, and lives
there, not deserving it, remembering that sorrow more and more. That woman
followed that man into the forest, even in his trouble. If she lives, abandoned
by that man of little merit, it is a hard thing. She is alone and young, she
does not know the roads and was not used to such things, and she is beset by
hunger and thirst. If she lives, it is a hard thing, in the great, cruel forest
always roamed by beasts of prey, abandoned by that man of little merit, that
fool, my friend.'

"So, remembering Damayanti, the Naishadha king lived in that king's house,
unknown."

**Divergences.**

* **The merit of hearing.** The line that telling the story of Karkotaka, Damayanti, Nala and
  Rituparna destroys Kali, often quoted, is a rejected passage here (\*301, after 3.63.13), and again after 3.78.9 (\*358). The CE's own word comes at the end of the tale: the story "is called the destroyer of Kali" (3.78.10).
* **Karkotaka's curse.** A rejected passage (\*300) gives the words of the seer's curse, that the
  snake would stay fixed like a tree until Nala carried him. In the CE Karkotaka says only that
  he cannot move a step (3.63.6).
* **The elephants.** Rejected passages (\*284, @11, @12) have wild elephants charge the caravan's
  tame elephants and add a fire and scattered jewels. The CE has only the herd coming to drink
  (3.62.6–10).

**Choices.**

* 3.62.7 *sahasā*: "suddenly".
* 3.62.13 *nāprāptakālo mriyate*: "No one dies before his time".
* 3.62.26 *sairandhrīm*: "a sairandhri", a serving woman who lives as she chooses. Draupadi will use
  the same word of herself in Virata's city (Book 4).
* 3.63.9 *kṛṣṇavartmanā*: "the fire's black track"; *kṛṣṇavartman*, "black-pathed", is a name
  of fire.
* 3.63.19 *bāhukaḥ*: Bahuka, the name Nala takes.
* 3.63.20 *akṣahṛdayam … aśvahṛdayena*: "the secret of the dice in return for the secret of horses".
* 3.64.6 *śataṃ śatāḥ*: "ten thousand", a hundred hundreds; the coin is not named.
* 3.64.7 *vārṣṇeyajīvalau*: Varshneya and Jivala.
* 3.64.10: the evening verse, kept as one verse.
