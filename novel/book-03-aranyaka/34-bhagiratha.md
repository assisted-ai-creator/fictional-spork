# Bhagiratha

"When the gods had gathered," said Lomasha, "Brahma, the Grandsire of the
worlds, said to them, 'Go, all you gods, as you please, where you wish. In a
long course of time the ocean will return to its nature, through the great king
Bhagiratha, for the sake of his kinsmen.'"

"How were his kinsmen the cause, brahmin?" said Yudhishthira. "And what was the
reason, sage? How was the ocean filled through Bhagiratha's labour? I want to
hear this in full, you who are rich in austerity, the finest story of kings,
told by you, brahmin."

When the great Dharma King said this, the best of brahmins told the greatness of
great Sagara.

"There was a king named Sagara," said Lomasha, "born in the line of the
Ikshvakus, with beauty, courage and strength, a mighty king, and he had no son.
He destroyed the Haihayas and the Talajanghas, Bharata, brought the other kings
under his power, and ruled his own kingdom. He had two wives, proud of their
beauty and youth, best of the Bharatas: the princess of Vidarbha, and Shaibya,
bull of the Bharatas. Wanting a son, the king practised very great austerity
with his two wives, king of kings, on Mount Kailasa. Practising great austerity
joined with yoga, he came to the great three-eyed destroyer of Tripura. This was
Shankara, Bhava, Ishana, with the trident in his hand, bearer of Pinaka, the
three-eyed Shiva, the fierce lord of many forms, the husband of Uma. As soon as
he saw the giver of boons, the strong-armed king and his two wives bowed down
and asked him for a son.

"Hara, pleased, said to the best of kings and his wives, 'At the moment you
chose your boon from me here, lord of men, it was settled. Sixty thousand sons,
brave and proud in battle, will be born to one of your wives, best of men. But
they will all perish together, king. One brave son, who will carry on the line,
will be born to the other.' When he had said this, Rudra vanished there and
then.

"King Sagara went home with his wives, overjoyed, my son. Then, best of men, his
two lotus-eyed wives, the princess of Vidarbha and Shaibya, became pregnant. In
time the princess of Vidarbha gave birth to a gourd, and Shaibya bore a son, a
boy like a god. The king made up his mind to throw away the gourd. Then he heard
a deep voice from the sky. 'King, do not be rash. You should not abandon your
sons. Take the seeds out of the gourd and keep them carefully in steaming pots
filled with ghee, one part in each. Then you will have sixty thousand sons,
king. Mahadeva has decreed that your sons will be born in this way, lord of men.
Do not think otherwise.'

---

"When the king heard this from the sky, best of kings, he believed it, bull of
the Bharatas, and did as he was told. So sixty thousand sons were born to that
royal seer of matchless splendour, by Rudra's grace, lord of the earth. They
were terrible and cruel in their deeds, and they could move through the sky.
Because they were so many, they despised all the worlds with the immortals. They
harassed the thirty gods, the gandharvas and rakshasas and all creatures, brave
and fond of battle.

"Tormented by the foolish sons of Sagara, the worlds went with all the gods to
Brahma for refuge. The blessed Grandsire of all the worlds said to them, 'Go
back as you came, all you gods, with the worlds. Before very long there will be
a great and terrible destruction of the sons of Sagara, gods, by their own
deeds.' When he said this, the gods and the worlds took leave of the Grandsire
and went away as they had come, lord of men.

"After a long time had passed, bull of the Bharatas, mighty King Sagara was
consecrated for the horse sacrifice. His horse wandered the earth, well guarded
by his sons. When it came to the ocean, dry and fearful to see, it vanished
there, carefully guarded as it was. The sons of Sagara thought the fine horse
had been stolen, my son, and went and told their father that the horse had been
stolen and could not be seen. He told them, 'Search for the horse, all of you,
in every direction.'

"At their father's command they searched for that horse in every direction and
over the whole face of the earth, great king. The sons of Sagara all met
together, and they had not found the horse, nor the one who took it. They came
and stood before their father with joined hands. 'King, lord of the earth, at
your command we have searched the whole earth, with its oceans, forests and
islands, its rivers, streams and caves, its mountains and woodlands. We have not
found the horse, nor the one who took it.'

"When he heard their words, the king was beside himself with anger, and, driven
by fate, said to them all, 'Go, and do not come back. Search for the horse
again. Do not come back without that sacrificial horse, my sons.' Taking their
father's command, the sons of Sagara began to search the whole earth again. Then
the heroes saw a place where the earth was split open. Coming to that hole, the
sons of Sagara dug, and with spades and mattocks they dug the ocean. As the sons
of Sagara dug together, the home of Varuna was torn apart on every side and came
to great distress. Asuras, snakes, rakshasas and creatures of every kind cried
out in pain as the sons of Sagara killed them. Creatures were seen by hundreds
and thousands with their heads cut off, their bodies broken, their knees, bones
and skulls split. As they dug the ocean, the home of sea monsters, a very long
time passed, and the horse was not seen.

"Then in the north-east of the ocean, lord of the earth, the furious sons of
Sagara broke through to Patala. There they saw the horse wandering on the
ground, and the great Kapila, the matchless mass of splendour, blazing with
austerity like fire with its flames.

---

"When they saw the horse, king, their hair stood on end with joy. Driven by
Time, they paid no heed to great Kapila, and ran in fury to seize the horse.
Then Kapila, the best of sages, grew angry, great king, the best of sages whom
they call Vasudeva. Opening his eye and sending out his fiery power at them, the
splendid one burned up those foolish sons of Sagara. When the great ascetic
Narada saw them turned to ashes, he went to Sagara and told him.

"When the king heard those terrible words from the sage's mouth, he was stricken
for a moment, and then remembered the words of Sthanu. Comforting himself, he
thought only of the horse. He called Amshumant, the son of Asamanjas, his
grandson, tiger of the Bharatas, and spoke to him. 'Those sixty thousand sons of
boundless power met their death through me, coming up against the power of
Kapila. And your father, my son, sinless one, I cast out, guarding dharma and
wanting the good of the people of the city.'"

"Why did Sagara, tiger among kings, cast out his own son, a hero, hard to cast
out?" said Yudhishthira. "Tell me, you who are rich in austerity."

"Sagara had a son known as Asamanjas, whom Shaibya bore," said Lomasha. "He
seized the weak children of the people of the city by their feet as they cried,
and threw them into the river. Then the people of the city came, full of fear
and grief, and stood with joined hands and begged Sagara, 'Great king, you
protect us from the fears of enemy armies and the rest. Now protect us from the
terrible fear of Asamanjas.' When he heard the terrible words of the people of
the city, the best of kings was stricken for a moment. Then he said to his
ministers, 'Let my son Asamanjas be banished from the city today. If you want to
please me, do this quickly.' When the king said this, lord of men, his ministers
did as the king had commanded, quickly. I have told you all of how great Sagara
banished his son, wanting the good of the people of the city.

"Now I will tell you all that Sagara said to the great archer Amshumant. Hear it
from me as I tell it.

"'My son,' said Sagara, 'I suffer for the casting out of your father, for the
death of my sons and for the loss of the horse. So, grandson, burning with grief
and bewildered at the hindrance to my sacrifice, bring back the horse and raise
me up from hell.'

"When great Sagara said this, Amshumant went in sorrow to the place where the
earth was split. He entered the ocean by that same road and saw great Kapila and
the horse. When he saw that ancient best of seers, the mass of splendour, he
bowed his head to the ground and told him his business. Then splendid Kapila was
pleased with Amshumant, and the righteous one said to him, 'I will give you a
boon,' Bharata. He chose first the horse, for the sacrifice. Second he chose
water, wishing to purify his fathers.

"Splendid Kapila, the bull among sages, said to him, 'I give you whatever you
ask, bless you, sinless one. Patience, dharma and truth are fixed in you.
Through you Sagara has his wish, and through you your father has a son. By your
power the sons of Sagara will go to heaven. And your grandson will please
Maheshvara and bring the river of the three paths from heaven to purify the sons
of Sagara. Take the sacrificial horse, bless you, bull among men. Let great
Sagara's sacrifice be finished, my son.'

"When great Kapila said this, Amshumant took the horse and came to great
Sagara's place of sacrifice. He greeted great Sagara's feet, and Sagara kissed
his head. He told him everything, the destruction of the sons of Sagara as he
had seen and heard it, and that the horse had come to the place of sacrifice.
When King Sagara heard it, he put away his grief for his sons, honoured
Amshumant and finished the sacrifice. When his sacrifice was finished, Sagara,
honoured by all the gods, made the ocean, the home of Varuna, his son.

"When he had ruled his kingdom for a very long time, the lotus-eyed king laid
the burden on his grandson and went to heaven. Righteous Amshumant ruled the
earth girdled by the sea, great king, as his grandfather had done. He had a son
named Dilipa, who knew dharma. Amshumant gave him the kingdom and died. When
Dilipa heard of the great death of his fathers, he was tormented with grief and
thought of their fate. The king made great efforts to bring down the Ganga, and
though he strove with all his strength, he could not bring her down. He had a
splendid son, devoted to dharma, famed as Bhagiratha, truthful and free from
envy. Dilipa consecrated him as king and went to the forest. By the power of his
austerity the king went from the forest to heaven when his time came, bull of
the Bharatas.

---

"That king, a great archer, an emperor, a great chariot fighter, was the delight
of the whole world's heart and eyes. The strong-armed one heard from great
Kapila of the terrible death of his fathers and that they had not reached
heaven. With an aching heart he gave his kingdom to his minister, and the lord
of men went to the slope of the Himalaya to practise austerity. Wanting to win
the favour of the Ganga, his sins burned away by austerity, he saw the Himalaya,
best of mountains, best of men.

"It was adorned with peaks of every shape, full of ores, embraced on every side
by clouds leaning on the wind. It was bright with rivers, thickets and slopes
full of water, haunted by lions and tigers hidden in its caves and hollows.
Birds of many colours sang there with many voices, bhringarajas, geese, datyuhas
and water hens, peacocks and woodpeckers, cuckoos and jivajivakas, dark-eyed
chakoras and putrapriyas. It was crowded with lotus pools in lovely waters, and
adorned with the sweet calls of cranes. Kinnaras and apsarases sat on its flat
rocks, and its trees were rubbed on every side by the tusks of the elephants of
the quarters. Vidyadharas wandered there, it was full of jewels of every kind,
and snakes of fierce poison with blazing tongues lived there. In places it was
like gold, in places like silver, in places like heaps of black eye-paint. To
that Himalaya he came.

"There the best of men took up terrible austerity. He lived on fruit, roots and
water for a thousand years. When a thousand divine years had passed, the great
river Ganga showed herself to him in bodily form.

"'What do you want from me, great king?' said Ganga. 'What shall I give you?
Tell me, best of men. I will do as you say.'

"When she said this, the king answered the daughter of Himavat. 'Giver of boons,
great river, my grandfathers were sent by Kapila to the house of Yama as they
searched for the horse. Those sixty thousand great sons of Sagara met their
death in a moment, coming up against the power of Kapila. Dying like that, they
have no place in heaven until you sprinkle those bodies with your waters. Take
my fathers, the sons of Sagara, to heaven, blessed one. It is for them that I
ask you, great river.'

"When the Ganga, whom the world bows to, heard the king's words, she was well
pleased and said to Bhagiratha, 'I will do as you say, great king, no doubt of
it. But the force of my fall from the sky is hard to bear. No one in the three
worlds can bear it, king, except the best of the gods, the blue-throated
Maheshvara. Please Hara, the giver of boons, by austerity, strong-armed one.
That god will hold me on his head as I fall, and he will do what you wish, for
the good of your fathers.'

"When the great king Bhagiratha heard this, king, he went to Mount Kailasa and
pleased Shankara. Meeting him when the time came, the best of men took from him
the boon of holding the Ganga, king, for his fathers to dwell in heaven.

---

"When the blessed one heard Bhagiratha's words, he said to the king, to please
the dwellers in heaven, 'So be it. I will hold the auspicious, divine, holy
river of the gods as she falls from the sky, for your sake, best of kings.' When
he had said this, the strong-armed god went to the Himalaya, surrounded by his
terrible attendants with weapons of every kind raised. Standing there, he said
to Bhagiratha, the best of men, 'Pray to the river, the daughter of the king of
mountains, strong-armed one. I will hold the best of rivers as she falls from
heaven.'

"When the king heard these words spoken by Sharva, he bowed with a controlled
mind and thought of the Ganga. Then, thought of by the king, the lovely river of
holy water, seeing the Lord standing there, suddenly fell from the sky. When
they saw her falling, the gods came with the great seers, and the gandharvas,
snakes and rakshasas, wanting to see it. Then the Ganga, daughter of Himavat,
fell from the sky, with great whirlpools churning, full of fish and crocodiles.
Hara held the Ganga, the girdle of the sky, king, as she fell on his forehead,
like a string of pearls.

"She flowed out in three streams, king, running to the sea, her water heaped
with foam like rows of geese. In places she curved in wide bends, in places she
stumbled, covered with the cloth of her own foam, and went like a drunken woman.
In places she roared with the sound of her waters. Doing these many things,
fallen from the sky, she reached the surface of the earth and said to
Bhagiratha, 'Show me the road, great king, by which I am to go. I have come down
to earth for your sake, lord of the earth.'

"When he heard these words, King Bhagiratha set out for where the bodies of the
great sons of Sagara lay, to purify them with the holy water, best of men. When
Hara, whom the world bows to, had held the Ganga, he went with the thirty gods
to Kailasa, the best of mountains. The king came to the ocean with the Ganga and
filled the ocean, the home of Varuna, with her force. The king took the Ganga as
his daughter, and there, his wish fulfilled, he gave water to his fathers.

"I have told you all of how the Ganga of the three paths was brought down to
earth to fill the ocean. I have told you how the ocean was drunk by the great
one for a purpose, and how Vatapi, the killer of brahmins, was destroyed by
Agastya, great king, as you asked me, lord."

**Divergences.**

* **Jahnu.** The familiar story that the sage Jahnu drank the Ganga and let her go again, so that she
  is called Jahnavi, is a rejected line here (\*538, after 3.108.16). The CE does call her "daughter of
  Jahnu" elsewhere (1.92.49) but does not tell the story. See L-128.
* **Asamanjas's cruelty.** A rejected line (\*526) says he caught the children "as they played". The CE
  says only that he seized them crying and threw them into the river (3.106.10).
