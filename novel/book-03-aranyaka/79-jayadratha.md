# Jayadratha

The great chariot fighters, best of the Bharatas, took their pleasure in that
Kamyaka forest, full of deer, and roamed there like immortals. They looked at
the many kinds of forest glades all around, and the lines of trees in full
flower, lovely in each season. Given to hunting, the Pandavas wandered through
that great forest like Indra, and took their pleasure for some time, the tamers
of enemies.

Then at the same time the tigers among men, scorchers of enemies, all went off
in the four directions to hunt for the brahmins. They left Draupadi at the
hermitage with the leave of Trinabindu, the great seer blazing with austerity,
and of Dhaumya, their household priest.

---

Then the king of the Sindhus, the glorious son of Vriddhakshatra, was on his way
to the Shalveyas, wanting to marry. Surrounded by a great retinue fit for a
king, with many kings, he came to Kamyaka. There he saw the Pandavas' glorious,
beloved wife, Draupadi, standing at the door of the hermitage in the empty
forest. She shone with the highest beauty of body, lighting up the forest glade
like lightning against a dark cloud. "Is she an apsaras, or a daughter of the
gods, or an illusion made by the gods?" So they all thought, joining their
palms, as they looked at her, the faultless one.

Then the king of the Sindhus, Jayadratha son of Vriddhakshatra, was amazed when
he saw her, faultless in every limb, and his heart was stirred. Bewildered by
desire, he said to King Kotikashya, "Whose is this woman, faultless in every
limb, if she is human at all? I have no need to marry, now that I have seen this
great beauty. I shall take her and go back to my own home. Go, friend, and find
out whose she is, who she is and where she comes from. Why has the fair-browed
one come to this thorny forest? Would this beauty of the world, with her fine
hips, long eyes, lovely teeth and slender waist, accept me today? Might I have
my wish, if I won this finest of women? Go, Kotika, and find out who her
protector is."

When he heard this, Kotikashya, wearing earrings, jumped down from his chariot,
went up to her, and asked her, as a jackal asks a tigress.

---

"Who are you," said Kotikashya, "bending down the branch of a kadamba, standing
lovely and alone at the hermitage, like the flame of a fire blazing in the
night, fanned by the wind, fair-browed one? You are very beautiful. Are you not
afraid in the forest? Are you a goddess, a yakshi or a danavi, a fine apsaras or
the wife of a great daitya? Are you a lovely daughter of the serpent king, or a
woman of the forest, or one of the night-rovers? Are you the wife of King
Varuna, or of Yama, of Soma or the lord of wealth? Have you come from the house
of Dhatri or Vidhatri, of lordly Savitri, or of Shakra? You do not ask us who we
are, and we do not know who your protector is here.

"We ask, lady, to add to your honour, where you come from and who your lord is.
Tell us truly your kin, your husband and your family, and what you are doing
here. I am the son of King Suratha, whom men know as Kotikashya. The one
standing there on the golden chariot, like fire offered into the altar, is the
king of the Trigartas, lotus-eyed one, the hero named Kshemankara. Beyond him is
the great archer, the eldest son of the lord of the Kunindas, broad-shouldered,
who always lives in the mountains. He is gazing at you in wonder.

"The dark, handsome young man standing by the lotus pool is the son of Subala,
king of the Ikshvakus, the slayer of enemies, lovely one. Twelve princes of the
Sauviras follow him with banners, all on chariots yoked to red horses, like
fires blazing at sacrifices. They are Angaraka, Kunjara and Guptaka,
Shatrunjaya, Sanjaya and Supravriddha, Prabhankara, Bhramara and Ravi, Shura,
Pratapa, and one named Kuhara. The one whom six thousand chariot fighters
follow, with elephants, horses and foot soldiers, is Jayadratha, king of the
Sauviras, if you have heard of him, fortunate one. His other brothers, of
undaunted spirit, the chief heroes of the Sauviras, strong young men able to
break through armies like clouds, follow the king. With these companions the
king travels, guarded like Indra by the hosts of Maruts. We do not know. Tell
us, lovely-haired one, whose wife you are and whose daughter."

---

Then Draupadi, the king's daughter, answered that best of the Shibis. She looked
at him slowly, let go of the branch, and gathered her silk upper cloth about
her.

"I know in my mind, son of a king, that a woman like me should not speak to you.
But there is no man or woman here to speak to you on my behalf. I am alone at
present, and so I answer you, good man. Listen. How could I, alone in the
forest, keeping to my own dharma, talk with a man alone? But I know you are the
son of Suratha, whom men know as Kotikashya. So I will tell you of my kin,
Shaibya. Listen.

"I am the child of King Drupada, and men know me as Krishna, Shaibya. I chose
five men as my husbands, and you will have heard of them at Khandavaprastha:
Yudhishthira, Bhimasena and Arjuna, and the two sons of Madri, heroes among men.
The Parthas left me here, divided the four directions between them and went
hunting. The king went east, Bhimasena south, Jaya west and the twins north. But
I think it is time now for those best of chariot fighters to be coming back
here. You will be honoured by them, and then go as you wish. Unyoke your horses
and bathe. The great son of Dharma loves his guests, and he will be glad to see
you."

When she had said this much to the son of Shibi, the moon-faced daughter of
Drupada, trusting him, went into that fine leaf hut, thinking of their duty to
guests.

---

While all the kings were sitting there, Bharata, the Sauvira heard Kotikashya's
report and said to the Shaibya, "When she was speaking, my heart delighted in
her. How could you come back from that first among women? Now that I have seen
her, other women seem to me like the females of monkeys, strong-armed one. I
tell you the truth. She has stolen my heart by the mere sight of her. Tell me of
that lovely woman, Shaibya, if she is human."

"This is Draupadi, Krishna," said Kotikashya, "the glorious king's daughter, the
queen of the five sons of Pandu, greatly honoured. All the Parthas love and
prize her, and she is faithful. Go on to the Suviras, Sauvira, and be happy,
with her left alone."

At this, Jayadratha, lord of the Sauviras and the Sindhus, of wicked heart,
answered, "Let us see Draupadi." He went into the empty hermitage like a wolf
into a lions' den, one of seven, and said this to Krishna. "Are you well,
beautiful one? Are your husbands in good health, and are those whose welfare you
wish in good health too?"

"The Kauravya King Yudhishthira, son of Kunti, is well," said Draupadi, "and so
am I, and his brothers, and the others you ask about. Take this water for your
feet and this seat, king's son. I will give you fifty deer for your breakfast.
Yudhishthira son of Kunti himself will give you black antelopes, spotted deer,
nyanku deer, antelopes, sharabhas and hares, ruru deer, shambaras and gayals,
and many other deer, boars and buffaloes, and other kinds of beasts."

"Breakfast is well," said Jayadratha. "You have done me every honour. Come, get
into my chariot and simply be happy. You should not stay loyal to the Parthas.
They have lost their fortune and fallen from the kingdom. They are wretched and
dazed, and they live in the forest. Wise women do not keep to a husband whose
fortune is gone. Let a woman follow the one who prospers, and not stay with one
whose fortune is ruined. They have lost their fortune and their kingdom, and
they are ruined for all time. Enough of suffering hardship out of loyalty to the
sons of Pandu. Be my wife, lovely-hipped one. Leave them and be happy. Win all
of Sindhu and Sauvira with me."

When the king of the Sindhus said these words, which made her heart shake,
Krishna moved away from that place, frowning. Scorning his words and brushing
them aside, the slender-waisted Krishna said to the Saindhava, "Not like that.
Be ashamed." The faultless one, waiting for her husbands to come, drew him on,
answering words with words.

---

Her lovely face was flushed with anger, her eyes were red, and her brows were
arched and knit. She flashed her face at the protector of the Suvira kingdom,
and Drupada's daughter spoke to him again.

"Fool, are you not ashamed to insult these glorious great chariot fighters,
fierce as venomous snakes? They are like great Indra. They keep to their own
work, and they stand firm even among hosts of yakshas and rakshasas. Good people
say nothing wicked of anyone worth praising, whether he lives in the forest or
keeps a house, an ascetic full of learning. It is dog-men who bark like this,
Suvira. I think there is no one in this gathering of kshatriyas who could take
your hand today and pull you back as you fall into the mouth of hell.

"You who hope to beat the Dharma King are like a man driving off with a stick a
rutting elephant as big as a mountain peak, roaming a Himalayan valley, from its
herd. You are plucking in your childishness the whiskers from the face of a
mighty lion as it sleeps, and you will run when you see Bhimasena angry. You are
kicking a mighty, most terrible, full-grown lion, born in the mountain caves, as
it sleeps fierce, you who would provoke the fierce Jishnu to anger. You are
treading like a madman on the tails of two black snakes with forked tongues and
sharp venom, you who want to fight the two best of men, the youngest Pandavas.

"As the bamboo, the plantain and the reed bear fruit for their own destruction
and not their growth, so you will take me, guarded by them, as the crab takes
its young."

"I know, Krishna," said Jayadratha. "I know what kind of men those sons of kings
are. But you cannot frighten us today with this terror. We were all born in the
seventeen highest families, Krishna, and we are greater in the six qualities. We
think the sons of Pandu are lacking, Draupadi. So get up quickly onto an
elephant or a chariot. We cannot be stopped by words alone. Or else speak
humbly, and hope for the favour of the king of the Sauviras again."

"I am strong," said Draupadi, "but the king of the Sauviras thinks me weak here.
Am I one who would speak humbly to the king of the Sauviras, out of fear of
force? The one whose track the two Krishnas would follow, standing together in a
single chariot, Indra himself could never carry off, let alone a wretched mere
man.

"Kiritin, the slayer of enemy heroes, will stand in his chariot, killing the
hearts of his foes. He will enter your army for my sake like fire burning dry
brush in the hot season. Then the Vrishni heroes who follow Janardana, and all
the great archers of the Kekayas, all these princes will follow my track in joy.
The terrible arrows loosed from Gandiva's bowstring, sounding like thunder, most
swift, strike against Dhananjaya's hand and make a most terrible sound. The
bearer of Gandiva, with the blare of his conch and the slap of his arm-guard,
will pour out great floods of arrows from Gandiva again and again, swift as
flights of locusts. When he plants his arrows in your chest, what will your mind
be like then?

"You will see Bhima running at you, mace in hand, and the sons of Madri rushing
in every direction, pouring out the venom of an anger born of resentment. Then
you will suffer for a long time, low man. As I have never been false to my noble
husbands in any way, even in my mind, by that truth I shall see you today
overpowered and dragged along by the Parthas. I shall not be able to panic,
though you drag me off, cruel man. I shall meet the heroes of the Kurus and come
back again to the Kamyaka forest."

---

The large-eyed woman looked at them as they tried to seize her, and threatened
them, and in fear she said, "Do not touch me! Do not!" and cried out to Dhaumya,
the household priest. Jayadratha seized her by the end of her upper garment, and
she pushed him hard. Thrown back by her, the wicked man fell like a tree cut off
at the root. But she was seized again with great force, and the king's daughter
sighed again and again. As she was dragged, Krishna greeted Dhaumya's feet and
mounted the chariot.

"You cannot take her without defeating the great chariot fighters," said
Dhaumya. "Think of the ancient dharma of the kshatriya, Jayadratha. You have
done a mean thing, and you will reap its evil fruit, no doubt of it, when you
meet the heroic Pandavas, led by the Dharma King."

When he had said this, Dhaumya followed the glorious king's daughter as she was
carried off, walking in the midst of the foot soldiers.

**Divergences.**

* **Draupadi reminds him of Duhshala.** A rejected passage (\*1202) has Draupadi tell Jayadratha that he is the husband of Duhshala, the Pandavas' cousin, and so a brother to her. Not narrated. That Jayadratha is Duhshala's husband is in the CE (1.108.18), but Draupadi does not say it here. See L-152.
* **Kotikashya reports back.** Rejected lines (\*1197–\*1199) describe Kotikashya returning to Jayadratha and passing on her words. The CE moves straight to Jayadratha's reply (3.251.1).
* Other rejected lines (\*1195–\*1196, \*1200–\*1201, \*1203) add small details. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.248.4 *brāhmaṇārthe*: "to hunt for the brahmins", to feed the brahmins who live with them.
* 3.248.6 *vārddhakṣatriḥ*: "the son of Vriddhakshatra". *śālveyān*: "to the Shalveyas", the people of Shalva.

* 3.249.6–11: Kotikashya names the kings and the twelve Sauvira princes as the text names them. The Subala here is an Ikshvaku king, not Shakuni's father.

* 3.252.14 *kṛṣṇau*: "the two Krishnas", Krishna and Arjuna.
