# Kubera's Lake

He went on, and on a lovely peak of Kailasa, in a fine forest, he saw a lovely
lotus pool guarded by rakshasas. It lay near Kubera's palace, fed by a mountain
stream, very lovely, with wide shade, covered with trees and creepers of every
kind. It was thick with green lotuses, divine, with golden lotus flowers, a
thing that purifies the world, fine and wonderful to see. There the son of Kunti
saw clear water, cool, light, fine, tasting of the drink of immortality,
wholesome and plentiful.

That lovely lotus pool was full of saugandhika lotuses, covered with golden
lotuses of the finest scent, on lovely stalks of fine beryl, bright with many
colours. Swans and ducks shook them, and they shed clean pollen. It was the
pleasure ground of Kubera, the great-spirited king of the yakshas, deeply
honoured by gandharvas, apsarases and gods. Seers visited it, and yakshas,
kimpurushas, rakshasas and kinnaras, and Vaishravana guarded it. As soon as
mighty Bhimasena, son of Kunti, saw it, he was overjoyed, looking at that divine
lake.

At the king's command rakshasas called the Krodhavashas guarded it, hundreds of
thousands of them, with bright weapons and gear. They saw Bhima, son of Kunti,
the hero of terrible courage, dressed in deerskins and wearing golden armlets.
He was armed, his sword bound on, fearless, the tamer of enemies, coming for the
lotuses. They called out to one another, "This tiger among men, armed and
dressed in deerskins: you should ask him what he has come here to do."

Then they all came up to strong-armed Vrikodara, full of splendour, and asked
him, "Tell us who you are. You wear the dress of a hermit and you are seen in
bark clothes. Tell us what you have come for, you of great splendour."

"I am Bhimasena, the Pandava, next after the son of Dharma, rakshasas," said
Bhima. "I have come with my brothers to wide Badari. The Panchali saw a
saugandhika there, the finest of all, surely carried from here by the wind, and
she wants many. Know, night-rangers, that I have come here to gather flowers to
please my lawful wife, whose body is without fault."

"This is Kubera's beloved pleasure ground, bull among men," said the rakshasas.
"A human being, bound by the law of death, cannot take his pleasure here. Divine
seers, yakshas and gods take leave of the lord of the yakshas before they drink
and take their pleasure here, Vrikodara. The gandharvas and apsarases take their
pleasure here too, Pandava. Any wicked man who scorns the Lord of Wealth and
wants to take his pleasure here wrongfully will perish, no doubt of it. You
scorn him and want to take the lotuses from here by force. How then do you call
yourself the brother of the Dharma King?"

"Rakshasas," said Bhima, "I do not see the Lord of Wealth here near me. And even
if I saw the great king, I would not stoop to beg. Kings do not beg. That is the
eternal law, and I will never give up the dharma of a kshatriya. And this lovely
lotus pool was born of a mountain stream. It is not in the house of the great
Kubera. It belongs equally to all creatures and to Vaishravana. When things are
so, who should beg from whom?"

---

When he had said this to all the rakshasas, Bhimasena plunged in. The mighty one
was forbidden by the rakshasas' words, as they shouted at him in anger on every
side, "No! Not like this!" But Bhima of terrible courage, of great splendour,
made nothing of those rakshasas and plunged in, and they all tried to hold him
back.

"Seize him! Bind him! Cut him up! We will cook Bhimasena and eat him!" So they
cried in anger, and they ran after him quickly with their weapons raised and
their eyes rolling. Then the mighty one took his huge, heavy mace, like the rod
of Yama, bound with bands of gold, and rushed at them, and cried, "Stand!
Stand!" Then, wielding javelins, axes and other weapons, the terrible
Krodhavashas suddenly fell on him, wanting to kill him. Fierce, they surrounded
Bhima on every side.

He was born of Kunti by the Wind, mighty, brave and swift, a killer of enemies.
He always loved truth and dharma, and in courage no enemy could overcome him.
The great hero broke their many moves and the weapons of his enemies, and killed
them by the hundreds beside the lotus pool, their bravest first. They saw his
courage and strength, the strength of his skill and the strength of his arms.
Together, from every side, they could not stand against him, and with their
bravest killed they suddenly turned back. Torn apart, their senses gone, the
Krodhavashas took to the sky and fled quickly to the peaks of Kailasa, broken
and tormented by Bhima.

Like Shakra when he has conquered the hosts of danavas and daityas, he had
overcome the hosts of enemies in battle. The victor plunged into the lotus pool
and took the lotuses he wanted. He drank the water, like the drink of
immortality, and his courage and splendour grew greater still. Then he pulled up
the saugandhika lotuses of the finest scent and took them.

Then the Krodhavashas, driven off by Bhima's strength, went together to the Lord
of Wealth and told him, in great distress, of Bhima's courage and his strength
in battle, just as it had been. When the god heard their words he laughed and
said to the rakshasas, "Let Bhima take all the lotuses he wants. It is for
Krishna's sake. I know this." So with leave from the Lord of Wealth they went,
their anger gone, to the best of the Kurus, and they saw Bhima alone in that
lotus pool, taking his pleasure as he liked.

---

Then he gathered many of those costly, divine, spotless flowers of many kinds,
bull of the Bharatas. Then a great, swift wind arose, blowing low, dragging
gravel, harsh to the touch, stirring up battle. A great meteor fell, with a
thunderclap and a great glare. The sun lost its light, its rays hidden, covered
in darkness. A terrible thunderclap came as Bhima set about his work of courage.
The earth shook, and a rain of dust fell. The quarters turned red, and beasts
and birds cried harshly. Everything was covered in darkness, and nothing could
be seen.

When he saw that wonder, Yudhishthira, son of Dharma, best of speakers, said,
"Who is going to attack us? Get ready, Pandavas, mad for battle, and bless you.
From what I see, the time for our courage is very near." When he had said this,
the Dharma King looked all round. Not seeing Bhima, Yudhishthira, the tamer of
enemies, asked Krishna and the twins, who were near him, about his brother
Bhima, of terrible deeds in battle. "Is Bhima set on some deed, Panchali? Or has
the hero, who loves a rash act, done something rash already? For these omens
have appeared without cause on every side, foretelling a great battle and
showing a sharp danger."

Proud Krishna, his loving queen with the lovely smile, wanting to please him,
answered him as he spoke. "King, the saugandhika the wind brought: I gave it to
Bhimasena today out of love. And I told the hero too, 'If you see many, gather
them all and come back quickly.' Surely, king, the strong-armed Pandava has gone
from here to the north-east to fetch them, to please me."

At her words the king said to the twins, "Let us go together quickly by the way
Vrikodara went. Let the rakshasas carry the brahmins, the weariest and the
weakest. And you, Ghatotkacha, who look like an immortal, carry Krishna. It is
clear to me that Bhima has gone far from here, for he has been gone a long time,
and he is as fast as the wind. He is swift, like Vinata's son in leaping over
the earth. He could fly up into the sky and come down where he pleases. Let us
follow him by your power, night-rangers, before he offends the siddhas who speak
of brahman."

"So be it," said all of them, with Hidimba's son at their head, bull of the
Bharatas. They knew where Kubera's lotus pool lay. Taking up the Pandavas and
all those brahmins, together with Lomasha, they set out with glad hearts. When
they had gone, they all saw in that forest a most lovely lotus pool, full of
lotuses in full flower. And they saw great Bhima standing on its shore, and the
yakshas he had killed, with their great wide eyes. He stood on the river bank
with his mace raised in both hands, like the Ender with his rod in his hand at
the time when creatures are destroyed.

When the Dharma King saw him he embraced him again and again, and said gently,
"Son of Kunti, what have you done? This was a rash act, bless you, and the gods
do not like it either. Do not do such a thing again, if you want to please me."
When he had counselled the son of Kunti and received the lotuses, they took
their pleasure in that same lotus pool, like immortals.

At that very time the huge-bodied guards of that garden appeared, holding rocks
as weapons. When they saw the Dharma King, and the divine seer Lomasha, and
Nakula and Sahadeva, and the other bulls among brahmins, they all bowed low with
humility, Bharata. The Dharma King comforted them, and the night-rangers were
appeased. Then, known to Kubera, the bulls among men, the upholders of the
Kurus, stayed there a short while, enjoying themselves.

---

After that, while the Pandavas were living there in trust, when those rakshasas
had gone, and Bhimasena's son too, a rakshasa, as it happened, carried off the
Dharma King, the twins and Krishna while Bhimasena was away.

He had waited on the Pandavas day after day, saying, "I am a brahmin skilled in
mantras, the finest knower of all the weapons." He was looking for his chance,
and inspecting the Parthas' quivers and bows. He was known by the name of
Jatasura. When Bhimasena, the tamer of enemies, had gone out to hunt, he took on
another form, deformed, terrible and huge. Taking all their weapons and seizing
Draupadi, the wicked one set off, carrying three of the Pandavas.

But Sahadeva, the Pandava, got away with an effort and shouted for Bhimasena in
the direction the mighty one had gone. Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, as he was
carried off, said to him, "Your dharma is failing, fool, and you do not look to
it. All others, among men and those born as animals too, gandharvas, yakshas,
rakshasas, birds and beasts, live on men. So you live on them too. When this
world prospers, your world prospers. When this world grieves, the gods grieve
with it, and they thrive when they are worshipped by the rules with offerings to
the gods and the Fathers. We are the keepers and protectors of the kingdom,
rakshasa. When the kingdom is not protected, how can there be prosperity, how
can there be happiness?

"A rakshasa should never scorn a king who has done no wrong, and we have done
not the smallest wrong, eater of men. One should never betray friends, or those
who trust one, or those whose food one has eaten, or where one has found
shelter. You lived in our shelter, honoured and at ease. You have eaten our
food. How can you want to carry us off, evil-minded one? Your conduct is empty,
your growing old is empty, your mind is empty. You deserve an empty death, and
today you will die for nothing. But if your mind is evil and you have cast off
every dharma, give us our weapons and take Draupadi by fighting. If you do this
deed without thinking, you will win only adharma and ill fame in the world. By
laying hands today on this woman, a human woman, rakshasa, you have stirred
poison and drunk it by the pot."

Then Yudhishthira made himself heavy for him, and overcome by the weight, the
rakshasa could not go as fast. Then Yudhishthira said to Draupadi and Nakula,
"Do not be afraid of this fool of a rakshasa. I have taken away his speed. The
strong-armed son of the Wind cannot be far away. When the moment comes, the
rakshasa will be no more."

Sahadeva, looking at the foolish rakshasa, said to Yudhishthira, son of Kunti,
"King, what task is there for a kshatriya higher than this, to give up his life
facing the enemy in battle, or to conquer? Let us fight. He will kill us, or we
will kill him, strong-armed scorcher of enemies. This is the place and the time,
king. The time for the dharma of a kshatriya has come, you whose courage is
true. Winning or falling, we should reach the good road. If the sun sets today
while the rakshasa is still alive, Bharata, I will never again say, 'I am a
kshatriya.' Hey, rakshasa! Stand! I am Sahadeva the Pandava. Kill me and then
carry them off, or be killed and sleep here today."

As he was speaking, strong-armed Bhimasena appeared by chance, like Vasava with
his thunderbolt. He saw his two brothers there and glorious Draupadi, and
Sahadeva on the ground, taunting the rakshasa. He saw the foolish rakshasa, his
mind struck by Time, going round and round, kept off the road by fate. When the
mighty one saw his brothers and Draupadi being carried off, Bhima grew angry and
said to the rakshasa, "I knew you before, as you went about testing our weapons.
But I thought nothing of you, and so you were not killed then. You were hidden
in a brahmin's form, and you said nothing unkind to us. You did what pleased us
and nothing to displease us. How could I kill a guest in a brahmin's form who
had done no wrong? A man who kills such a one, even thinking him a rakshasa,
goes to hell.

"No one can be killed before Time has ripened him. Surely today you are ripe,
since you have thought of this, a thought given you by Time, the doer of
wonders, to carry off Krishna. You have swallowed this hook, hung on the line of
Time, like a fish in the water with its mouth sewn. How will you get away from
me today? You will not reach the place you set out for, where your mind went
ahead of you. You will go the road of Baka and Hidimba."

At Bhima's words the rakshasa, driven by Time, let them all go in fear and stood
up to fight. His lip quivering with rage, he said to Bhima again, "My wits are
not confused, evil one. I delayed for your sake. I have heard of all the
rakshasas you have killed in battle. Today I will offer them water with your
blood."

At these words Bhima licked the corners of his mouth and seemed to smile in his
anger, like Time the Ender himself. Wanting only to fight with his arms, he
rushed at the rakshasa. The rakshasa too rushed in fury at Bhima, who stood
there eager to fight, as Bala rushed at the wielder of the thunderbolt. As the
most terrible fight with arms began, the two sons of Madri both ran up in anger.
Vrikodara, son of Kunti, held them back, laughing, and said, "I am a match for
the rakshasa. Watch. I swear by myself and my brothers, by dharma, by good deeds
and by sacrifice, king, I will kill the rakshasa."

When he had said this, the two heroes, the rakshasa and Vrikodara, challenging
each other, grappled with their arms. The blows between Bhima and the rakshasa,
both angry, neither bearing the other in battle, were like those between a god
and a danava. Tearing up trees again and again, they struck each other, roaring
like thunderclouds at the end of the hot season. The best of the strong broke
great trees with their thighs, furious with each other, each wanting to win.
That battle of trees, which destroyed the trees, was like the one long ago
between the brothers Valin and Sugriva, lions among monkeys.

Swinging trees again and again, for a while they beat each other, roaring over
and over. At last all the trees in that place were thrown down and piled up in
hundreds, as each wanted to kill the other. Then, Bharata, the mighty ones took
up rocks for a while and fought like two great mountains with great clouds.
Unforgiving, they struck each other with fierce, fierce-looking, huge rocks,
like thunderbolts of great speed. Proud of their strength, they struck each
other again, and then gripped each other with their arms and dragged each other
about like elephants. They fell on each other with most terrible fists, and
there was a great cracking noise between the two mighty ones.

Then Bhima clenched his fist, like a five-headed snake, and struck the rakshasa
hard on the neck. Seeing the rakshasa worn out, struck by Bhimasena's arm, very
weary, Bhimasena closed in on him. Then strong-armed Bhima, like an immortal,
lifted him in his arms by force and dashed him on the ground. The Pandava
crushed all his limbs, struck him with his forearm and took the head from the
body. The head of Jatasura, torn off by Bhimasena's strength, its lips bitten,
its eyes rolling, fell smeared with blood, like a fruit fallen from its stalk,
its teeth biting its lip.

When he had killed him, the great bowman went to Yudhishthira, praised by the
best of the brahmins as Vasava is by the Maruts.

**Divergences.**

* **Bhima picks the flowers first.** Rejected lines (\*764, \*765) have Bhima take the lotuses by force before the fight. In the CE he plunges in, fights,
  and takes the lotuses after the guards have fled (3.152.13–22).
* **"Take leave of the king".** A rejected line (\*761) has the rakshasas tell Bhima to ask Kubera's leave and then drink and take. Not narrated.
* **Jatasura's claim.** A rejected line (\*773) has Jatasura claim to be a pupil of Rama Jamadagnya. In the CE he says only that he is a brahmin skilled in
  mantras and weapons (3.154.3).
* **Where the others were.** A rejected passage (\*776) says that Ghatotkacha had gone and that Lomasha and the seers had gone to bathe and gather flowers.
  The CE says only that the rakshasas and Ghatotkacha had gone (3.154.1).
* **Sahadeva's sword.** A rejected line (\*777) has Sahadeva draw a sword and break free. In the CE he gets away "with an effort" (3.154.7).
* **Yudhishthira's talk.** An appendix passage (@17, after 3.153.31) gives Yudhishthira a speech about the fords they have seen. Not narrated.
