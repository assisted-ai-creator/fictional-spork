# Savitri and Yama

"Then the mighty one, with his wife to help him, gathered fruits and filled his
basket, and then split wood," said Markandeya. "As he was splitting the wood, he
began to sweat, and from the effort a pain came into his head. Worn out, he went
to his dear wife and said, 'From this effort of mine a pain has come into my
head, Savitri. My limbs, and my heart, seem to burn, and I feel as if I were
unwell, you of few words. My head seems pierced with spikes. I want to sleep,
lovely one. I have no strength to stand.'

"Savitri came to her husband, held him, took his head in her lap and sat down on
the ground. Then the ascetic woman, thinking over Narada's words, reckoned that
moment, that instant, that hour and that day. After a moment she saw a man in
yellow clothes, his hair bound up, handsome, with the splendour of the sun, dark
and bright, red-eyed, a noose in his hand, terrifying, standing at Satyavat's
side and looking at him. When she saw him, she suddenly got up, gently laid her
husband's head down, joined her palms, and spoke in distress with a trembling
heart. 'I know you are a god, for this form is not human. If you please, god,
tell me who you are, and what you mean to do.'

"'You are faithful to your husband, Savitri,' said Yama, 'and rich in austerity.
So I speak to you. Know that I am Yama, lovely one. This husband of yours,
Satyavat, the king's son, has come to the end of his life. I shall bind him and
take him. Know that this is what I mean to do.'

"When he had said this, the lord of the ancestors, the blessed one, began to
tell her all he meant to do, as it was, to please her. 'This man is joined to
dharma, handsome, an ocean of virtues. He does not deserve to be taken by my
men, and so I have come myself.' Then Yama, by force, drew out of Satyavat's
body a person the size of a thumb, bound by the noose and helpless in his power.
Then that body, its life drawn out, without breath, its light gone, motionless,
was unlovely to see.

"Yama bound him like this and set off facing south. And Savitri, deep in grief,
followed Yama, the greatly blessed woman, faithful to her husband, perfected by
her discipline and her vows.

"'Turn back, Savitri,' said Yama. 'Go and perform his funeral rites. You have
paid your debt to your husband. You have come as far as anyone can come.'

"'Where my husband is taken, or where he goes himself, I must go too,' said
Savitri. 'This is the eternal dharma. By my austerity, my service to my elders,
my love for my husband and my vow, and by your grace, my way is not barred. The
wise, who see the truth of things, say that friendship is made in seven steps.
Putting that friendship first, I shall say something. Hear it. Those who have
not mastered themselves do not live in the forest keeping dharma, the dwelling
and the toil. They say that dharma comes from understanding. That is why the
good call dharma the chief thing. By the dharma of one, as the good think, all
of us have come upon this road. I do not wish for a second or a third. That is
why the good call dharma the chief thing.'

"'Turn back,' said Yama. 'I am pleased with these words of yours, well joined in
sound, syllable, letter and reason. Choose a boon here, all but his life. I
shall give you any boon, faultless one.'

"'My father-in-law, fallen from his kingdom, has come to live in the forest, and
has lost his sight in the hermitage,' said Savitri. 'By your grace may the king
have his sight again and be strong, like fire and the sun.'

"'I give you every boon, faultless one,' said Yama. 'It will be just as you have
said. I see you are tired from the road. Turn back and go, so that you do not
grow weary.'

"'How can I be weary near my husband?' said Savitri. 'For where my husband is,
there is my sure way. Where you take my husband, there is my way. Lord of the
gods, hear my words again. To meet the good even once is the highest thing to
wish for. Beyond that, they call it friendship. Meeting a good man is never
fruitless. So one should live among the good.'

"'You have spoken words that please the heart and increase the understanding of
the wise,' said Yama, 'words that rest on what is good. Choose a second boon,
lovely one, all but this Satyavat's life.'

"'Long ago my wise father-in-law's own kingdom was taken from him,' said
Savitri. 'May the king win it back. And may my elder never give up his own
dharma. This is the second boon I choose from you.'

"'He will soon get back his own kingdom,' said Yama, 'and the king will not fall
from his own dharma. I have done your wish, king's daughter. Turn back and go,
so that you do not grow weary.'

"'You restrain these creatures by your law,' said Savitri, 'and having
restrained them, you lead them away, not as you please. That is why you are
famed as Yama, the restrainer, god. Hear these words I speak. Harming no
creature in deed, mind or word, kindness and giving: this is the eternal dharma
of the good. This world is mostly like this: men are gentle only as far as their
power reaches. But the good show pity even to enemies who come to them.'

"'Like water to a man who is thirsty are these words you have spoken,' said
Yama. 'Choose here whatever boon you wish, lovely one, all but this Satyavat's
life.'

"'My father, the king, has no son,' said Savitri. 'May my father have a hundred
sons of his own body, to carry on his family. This is the third boon I choose
from you.'

"'Your father shall have a hundred glorious sons to carry on his family, lovely
one,' said Yama. 'I have done your wish, king's daughter. Turn back. You have
come far along the road.'

"'This is not far for me, near my husband,' said Savitri. 'My mind runs on much
further. As you go, hear again the words I have made ready to say. You are the
mighty son of Vivasvat, and so the wise call you Vaivasvata. Creatures are
pleased by your calm and your dharma, and so you are the King of Dharma here,
lord. A man does not trust even himself as much as he trusts the good. So
everyone wants above all to be loved by the good. Trust is born from goodwill
towards all creatures. So people above all put their trust in the good.'

"'The words you have spoken, lovely woman, I have heard from no one but you,'
said Yama. 'I am pleased with them. Choose a fourth boon, all but his life, and
go.'

"'May a hundred sons, of my own body, be born to Satyavat and me,' said Savitri,
'to carry on the family, strong and brave. This is the fourth boon I choose from
you.'

"'You shall have a hundred sons, strong and brave, to give you joy, lady,' said
Yama. 'Do not weary yourself, king's daughter. Turn back. You have come far
along the road.'

"'The conduct of the good is always eternal dharma,' said Savitri. 'The good do
not sink and do not suffer. The meeting of the good with the good is never
fruitless. The good do not follow fear from the good. The good lead the sun by
truth. The good hold up the earth by austerity. The good are the refuge of what
has been and what is to be, king. Among the good, the good do not sink. Knowing
this to be the eternal conduct that the noble follow, the good act for others
and do not look for a return. Grace among good men is never in vain, nor does a
purpose or honour come to nothing. Since this is always settled among the good,
the good are protectors.'

"'The more you speak words joined to dharma, pleasing to the heart, well phrased
and full of meaning,' said Yama, 'the higher my devotion to you. Choose a boon
beyond compare, you of firm vows.'

"'You who give honour,' said Savitri, 'this gift is not, as with the other
boons, without an exception for the good deed. I choose this boon: let Satyavat
live. For without my husband I am as good as dead. I do not want happiness
without my husband. I do not want heaven without my husband. I do not want
fortune without my husband. I will not choose to live without my husband. You
yourself gave me the boon of a hundred sons, and yet my husband is being taken
from me. I choose this boon: let Satyavat live. Then your word will be true.'

---

"'So be it,' said Yama, Vaivasvata, and loosed the nooses. The King of Dharma,
glad at heart, said to Savitri, 'Here, good woman, joy of your family, I have
set your husband free. He is healthy, and you may take him, and he will achieve
his aims. He will live four hundred years with you. When he has sacrificed with
sacrifices and with dharma, he will win fame in the world. Satyavat will beget a
hundred sons in you, and they will all be kings, kshatriyas with sons and
grandsons, famous, named after you, and they will last for ever here. And your
father will have a hundred sons by your mother, Malavi. They will be called the
Malavas, eternal, with sons and grandsons, your brothers, kshatriyas like the
thirty gods.'

"When he had given her this boon, the mighty King of Dharma turned Savitri back
and went to his own house. When Yama had gone, Savitri, having won back her
husband, went to where her husband's body lay. When she saw her husband on the
ground, she went up to him, held him, took his head in her lap and sat down on
the ground.

"Satyavat came to himself and spoke to Savitri, looking at her again and again
with love, like a man come home from abroad. 'I have slept a long time. Why did
you not wake me? And where is that dark man who dragged me away?'

"'You have slept a long time in my lap, bull among men,' said Savitri. 'The
blessed god Yama, who restrains creatures, has gone. You are rested, greatly
blessed one, and awake, king's son. If you can, get up. See how deep the night
is.'

"Satyavat, coming to himself, rose like one who has slept well, looked in every
direction and at the edges of the forest, and said, 'I came out with you for
fruits, slender one. Then, as I was splitting wood, my head ached. Burning with
the pain in my head, I could not stand long, and I slept in your lap. I remember
all this, lovely one. As you held me, sleep took my mind. Then I saw a terrible
darkness, and a man of great power. If you know what it was, tell me, slender
one. Was it a dream I saw, or was it true?'

"Savitri said to him, 'The night is deepening. Tomorrow I shall tell you all
that happened, king's son. Get up, get up, bless you. Think of your parents, you
of good vows. The night is deep and the sun has gone down. These night-rovers
are roaming, glad, with cruel cries. We can hear the rustle of leaves as the
beasts roam in the forest. These jackals, howling terribly, cry out fiercely
from the south-west, and they make my heart shake.'

"'The forest looks frightening, covered in thick darkness,' said Satyavat. 'You
will not know the path, and you will not be able to walk.'

"'Today in this forest, where there has been a fire, a dry tree stands burning,'
said Savitri. 'The fire can be seen here and there, fanned by the wind. I shall
bring fire here and light it all round. There is wood here. Let go of your
distress. If you cannot bring yourself to go, since I see you are unwell, and
you will not know the path in the forest covered in darkness, we shall go
tomorrow morning when the forest can be seen, if you agree. Let us spend this
night here, if you like, sinless one.'

"'The pain in my head has gone,' said Satyavat. 'My limbs feel well. I want, by
your kindness, to see my mother and father again. I have never before gone to
the hermitage late. My mother keeps me in before twilight comes. Even when I go
out by day, my parents are anxious. My father searches for me with the people of
the hermitage. My mother and father, in great grief, have reproached me many
times before, saying, "You come back so late." What state they must be in today
because of me, I keep thinking. If I am not seen, they will be in great sorrow.

"'Once, in the night, weeping, deep in grief, the old couple said to me many
times with love, "Without you we cannot live a moment, my son. As long as you
live, my son, our life is sure. You are the staff of two old blind people. In
you our line is set, in you our funeral cake, our fame and our descendants." My
mother is old, and my father is old, and I am their staff. What state will they
fall into, not seeing me tonight? I resent the sleep because of which my father
and my blameless mother have fallen into danger on my account. And I too am in
danger, in hard trouble. For I cannot bear to live without my mother and father.

"'Surely by now my father, whose eye is wisdom, is asking each person in the
hermitage, his mind in turmoil. I do not grieve for myself as I grieve for my
father, lovely one, and for my mother, very frail, who followed her husband.
Because of me they will suffer great pain today. I live while they live. I live
thinking, "They must be supported by me. I must do what pleases them."'

"When he had said this, the one whose self was dharma, who served his elders and
loved his elders, raised his arms in grief and wept aloud. Seeing her husband
wasted with sorrow like this, Savitri, who walked in dharma, wiped the tears
from his eyes and said, 'If I have practised austerity, if I have given, if I
have made offerings, may this night be well for my mother-in-law, my
father-in-law and my husband. I do not remember ever having spoken a false word,
even in play. By that truth, may my husband's parents live today.'

"'I want to see my parents,' said Satyavat. 'Go, Savitri, quickly. If I find
anything bad has happened to my mother or father, I will not live, lovely one. I
swear it by truth. If your mind is set on dharma, if you want me alive, or if
you would do what pleases me, go to the hermitage now.'

"Then lovely Savitri rose, tied up her hair, and lifted her husband up in her
arms. Satyavat stood, rubbed his limbs with his hand, looked in every direction
and fixed his eyes on the basket. Savitri said to him, 'Tomorrow you will bring
the fruits from here. I shall carry the axe for you, for our safety.' She hung
the loaded basket on the branch of a tree, took her husband's axe and came back
to him. The lovely-thighed woman put her husband's arm on her left shoulder,
held him with her right arm, and walked on gently.

"'From going often, timid one, I know the paths,' said Satyavat. 'And I can see
by the moonlight through the trees. We came by the road where we picked the
fruits. Go the way we came, lovely one. Do not doubt the road. At this clump of
palasha trees the path divides in two. Go by the path to the north of it, and
hurry. I am well, I am strong, and I want to see both my parents.'

"Talking like this, he hurried towards the hermitage."

**Divergences.**

* **The boon of sons.** In the popular retelling Savitri tricks Yama into granting sons "by Satyavat" and so forces him to return her husband. In the CE she does ask for a hundred sons by Satyavat (3.281.44). But it is Yama who then offers "a boon beyond compare" without the usual exception (3.281.50), and she names Satyavat's life openly, pointing out that his gift of sons requires it (3.281.51–53). See L-159.
* **Why Yama came himself.** A rejected passage (\*1320) has Savitri ask why Yama came in person instead of sending his messengers. In the CE Yama explains it unasked (3.281.15).
* **Savitri guards the body.** A rejected line (\*1321) has Savitri arrange a guard for her husband's body before following Yama. Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1318–\*1319, \*1322–\*1329) add small phrases, including another wording of the fourth boon. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.281.16 *aṅguṣṭhamātraṃ puruṣam*: "a person the size of a thumb", the soul.
* 3.281.23–24: Savitri's first speech is compressed in the text and hard to construe. The novel keeps close to the words.
