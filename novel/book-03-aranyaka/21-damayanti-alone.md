# Damayanti Alone

"When she had killed the hunter," said Brihadashva, "the lotus-eyed woman went
on into the empty forest, fearful, loud with the shrill of crickets. Lions,
tigers, boars, bears, ruru deer and leopards lived there. It was crowded with
birds of every kind, and mlecchas and thieves haunted it. It was thick with
shala trees and bamboo, dhava, fig trees, tinduka, ingudi and kimshuka, with
arjuna and arishta trees, with sandalwood and silk-cotton. It was full of
rose-apple, mango, lodhra, khadira, teak and cane, of kashmari, amalaka,
plaksha, kadamba and cluster figs. It was thick with jujube and bilva and
crowded with banyans, with priyala, palmyra and date palms, haritaka and
bibhitaka.

"She saw mountains of every kind, veined with hundreds of ores, and thickets
loud with birds, and wonderful caves. She saw rivers, lakes and pools, and
beasts and birds of every kind. She saw many terrible pishachas, snakes and
rakshasas, ponds and tanks and mountain peaks on every side, rivers and seas,
wonderful to see. The daughter of the lord of Vidarbha saw herds of buffalo
there, boars, jackals, bears, monkeys and snakes. Full of splendour, of glory,
of steadiness and of the highest grace, the princess of Vidarbha wandered alone,
searching for Nala. Bhima's daughter, the king's child, feared no one there,
though she had come into that cruel wilderness, wasted by her husband's
misfortune.

"The daughter of Vidarbha sat down on a flat rock, king, her whole body wrapped
in grief for her husband, and lamented in great sorrow.

"'Strong-armed lord of the Nishadhas, with the chest of a lion, where have you
gone, king, leaving me here in the empty forest? Hero, you sacrificed with the
horse sacrifice and other rites with rich fees. How can you act falsely towards
me, tiger among men? What you said in my presence, tiger among men, splendid
one, you should make true, good bull among kings. Remember what the birds, the
geese, said to you, lord of the earth, and what they said to me. The four Vedas,
with their limbs and lesser limbs, studied in full, best of men, are on one side
of the scale, they say, and truth alone on the other. So you should make true,
killer of enemies, lord of men, the word you spoke to me before, hero.

"'Ah, hero, am I not dear to you, sinless one, as they say? Why do you not
answer me in this terrible wilderness? This fierce king of the forest, with his
mouth wide open, his look cruel, tormented by hunger, threatens me. Why do you
not save me? "No woman is dear to me but you, lovely one," you said then. Make
that old word true, good king. I am your dear wife, lord of men, weeping and out
of my mind. You are the one I long for, and I long for you, my lord. Why do you
not answer me? I am thin, wretched, pale and dirty, lord of the earth, covered
by half a cloth. I am alone, weeping like a woman with no protector, like a doe
strayed from the herd, you with the wide eyes. You do not honour me, you who
deserve honour, as I weep, grinder of enemies. Great king, I am here in the
great forest, alone, your good wife, calling to you. Why do you not answer me?

"'You are of good family and good character, lovely in every limb. I do not see
you today on this mountain, best of men, nor in this terrible forest haunted by
lions and tigers. I do not see you lying, sitting, standing or walking, lord of
the Nishadhas, you who make my sorrow grow. Whom shall I ask in my misery,
wasted by grief for you? Have you met King Nala here in the forest and seen him?
Who will tell me today that Nala is here in this forest, the handsome,
great-hearted destroyer of enemy ranks? From whom shall I hear today the sweet
words, "This is he, King Nala, the lotus-eyed, whom you are looking for"?

"'Here comes the splendid king of the forest, the tiger, with his four fangs and
his great jaws, straight towards me. I will ask him without fear. You are the
lord of the beasts, the master of this forest. Know that I am Damayanti, the
daughter of the king of Vidarbha, wife of Nala, lord of the Nishadhas, killer of
enemies. I am alone, wretched, wasted by grief, searching for my husband.
Comfort me, king of beasts, if you have seen Nala. Or, king of the forest, if
you do not tell me of Nala, eat me, best of beasts, and free me from my sorrow.

"'This king of beasts has heard my lament in the forest and goes his way to this
river of sweet water that runs to the sea. I will ask this holy mountain about
the king, this king of mountains, towering with its many high peaks, shining,
touching the sky, many-coloured and lovely. It is strewn with ores of every kind
and adorned with stones of every sort. It stands high like the banner of this
great forest. It is full of lions, tigers, elephants, boars, bears and deer, and
loud on every side with birds of every kind. It is adorned with kimshuka,
ashoka, bakula and punnaga trees, with rivers full of birds, and with peaks.

"'Blessed one, best of mountains, famous one, divine to see, refuge of many,
giver of many good things, I bow to you, upholder of the earth. I have come to
you and I bow to you. Know that I am a king's daughter, a king's daughter-in-law
and a king's wife, famed as Damayanti. My father is the king, the lord of
Vidarbha, a great chariot fighter, a lord of the earth named Bhima, guardian of
the four classes. He has performed the royal consecration and the horse
sacrifice, rites with rich fees, the best of kings, with his wide, lovely,
curving eyes. He honours brahmins, his conduct is good, he speaks the truth and
is free from envy. He is of good character and good behaviour, of great fortune,
knowing dharma, and pure. The lord guards the Vidarbhas well and has beaten his
enemies. Know that I am his daughter, blessed one, come to you.

"'Among the Nishadhas, great mountain, my father-in-law was the best of kings, a
name well spoken, famed as Virasena. His son is a hero, splendid, true in his
courage, who rules the kingdom that came to him in turn from his father. His
name is Nala, tamer of enemies, known as the king of good name. He honours
brahmins, knows the Veda, speaks well, does holy deeds, drinks the soma and
keeps the sacred fires. He sacrifices, he gives, he fights, and he rules well.
Know that I am his wife, best of mountains, come here, robbed of fortune,
without my husband, without a protector, in misfortune, searching for my
husband, that best of men.

"'With these hundreds of peaks of yours that scratch the sky, best of mountains,
have you seen King Nala in this cruel forest? He walks like a lordly elephant.
He is wise, long-armed, impatient of wrong, bold, true to his word, steady, my
glorious husband, the lord of the Nishadhas. Have you seen Nala? Why do you not
comfort me with a word today, best of mountains, alone and weeping in my misery,
as you would your own daughter in her misery? Hero, bold one, you who know
dharma, true to your word, lord of the earth, if you are in this forest, king,
show yourself. When shall I hear the Naishadha's voice, gentle and deep, like
the sound of a rain cloud, like nectar? When will the great king call me
"princess of Vidarbha" in that lovely voice, full and in keeping with the Veda,
that drives away my grief?'

---

"When she had said this to that best of mountains, Damayanti, the king's
daughter, went on again towards the north. After three days and nights the fine
woman saw a matchless grove of ascetics, like a divine garden. It was graced by
ascetics like Vasishtha, Bhrigu and Atri, restrained, sparing in their food,
full of self-control and purity. Some lived on water, some on air, some on
leaves. They had conquered their senses, great and blessed ones who longed to
see the road to heaven. She saw the lovely circle of hermitages where the
ascetics lived, sages dressed in bark and deerskins, masters of their senses.

"When she saw the hermitage, with its beasts of every kind, its troops of
monkeys and its ascetics, she went in. She had fine brows, fine hair, fine hips
and breasts, and a face with fine teeth. She was full of splendour, well poised,
with a lovely curving, stately walk, the darling of Virasena's son, the jewel of
women, blessed and proud. She greeted the elders in austerity and stood bowing
humbly, and all the ascetics said to her, 'Welcome.' When they had honoured her
as was proper, those rich in austerity said, 'Sit down. Tell us what we can do
for you.'

"The lovely woman said to them, 'Is all well with you, blessed ones, sinless
ones, in your austerity, your fires, your duties, and with the beasts and birds
here? Is all well, blessed ones, as you keep your own dharma?'

"'All is well, good lady, everywhere,' they told the glorious woman. 'Tell us,
you who are flawless in every limb, who you are and what you wish to do. As soon
as we saw your perfect beauty and your splendour here, we were filled with
wonder. Take heart. Do not grieve. Are you the great goddess of this forest, or
of the mountain, or of this river, lovely one? Tell the truth, blameless one.'

"She said to the seers, 'I am not the goddess of this forest, brahmins, nor of
the mountain, nor of the river. Know, all of you rich in austerity, that I am a
woman. I will tell you everything at length. Hear it all from me. Among the
Vidarbhas there is a lord of the earth named Bhima, of great splendour. Know,
all you best of the twice-born, that I am his daughter. The wise lord of the
Nishadhas, famed as Nala, is my husband, lord of the people, a hero, a winner of
battles, learned. He is devoted to the worship of the gods and kind to the
twice-born, the guardian of the Nishadha line, blessed and splendid. He speaks
the truth, knows dharma, is wise, keeps his promises and crushes his enemies. He
honours brahmins, is devoted to the gods, splendid, conqueror of enemy cities.
Nala, the best of kings, with the splendour of the king of the gods, is my
husband, with his wide eyes and his face like the full moon, killer of enemies.
He has performed the chief rites, has mastered the Veda and its limbs, kills his
rivals in battle, and shines like the sun and the moon.

"'Some base men, masters of deceit, low and evil, challenged that lord of the
earth, devoted to truth and dharma. Crooked men, skilled at dice, beat him at
the game and won his kingdom and wealth. Know that I am the wife of that bull
among kings, famed as Damayanti, longing to see my husband. I wander here in
misery through forests and mountains, lakes and rivers, lovely ponds and
wildernesses of every kind, searching for my husband Nala, skilled in battle,
great-hearted, master of weapons. Has the king, Nala, lord of the people of the
Nishadhas, come to this holy grove of yours, blessed ones? It was because of
him, brahmins, that I came into this cruel forest, fearful and terrible, haunted
by tigers and deer. If I do not see King Nala in some days and nights, I will do
myself good by freeing myself from this body. What use is life to me without
that bull among men? How can I live now, tormented by grief for my husband?'

"When Bhima's daughter Damayanti lamented alone like this in the forest, the
ascetics, who speak the truth, said to her, 'Your future will be good, lovely
one, good lady. We see by our austerity that you will soon see the Naishadha.
Daughter of Bhima, you will see Nala, lord of the Nishadhas, killer of enemies,
the best of those who uphold dharma, free from his fever. You will see your
husband, good lady, freed from all his sins, with every jewel, ruling that same
fine city, tamer of enemies. He will be a terror to his foes and an end to his
friends' sorrows, a king of good family.'

"When they had said this to Nala's beloved queen, the king's daughter, the
ascetics vanished, with their sacred fires and their hermitages. When she saw
this great wonder, Damayanti, flawless in every limb, daughter-in-law of King
Virasena, was amazed. 'Was it a dream I saw? What was this that happened here?
Where are all those ascetics? Where is that circle of hermitages? Where is that
lovely river of holy water, full of birds of every kind, and those charming
trees, bright with fruit and flowers?'

"Bhima's daughter Damayanti, of the bright smile, thought for a long time. Then,
lost in grief for her husband, wretched, her face grew pale. She went on to
another place, and there she saw an ashoka tree. Her eyes full of tears, she
lamented in a voice choked with weeping. She went up to that best of trees, the
ashoka, in flower, lovely, crowned with its young shoots, loud with birds.

"'Ah, this splendid tree in the heart of the forest shines with its many crowns
of flowers, like a splendid king of the Dravidas. Make me free of sorrow
quickly, ashoka, lovely to see. Have you seen the king, free of sorrow, fear and
trouble? Have you seen Nala, tamer of enemies, Damayanti's dear husband, the
lord of the Nishadhas, my love? He wears half of a single cloth, his skin is
tender and fine, a hero tormented by misfortune, who came into this forest.
Ashoka tree, do what will let me go free of sorrow. Be true to your name,
ashoka, "sorrowless", by destroying my sorrow.'

"So, in her misery, the fine woman, Bhima's daughter, walked three times round
the ashoka tree and went on into a still harsher country. She saw many trees and
many rivers, many lovely mountains and many beasts and birds. Bhima's daughter
saw caves and slopes and wonderful rivers as she searched for her husband.

---

"When she had gone a long way, Damayanti of the bright smile saw a great
caravan, crowded with elephants, horses and chariots. It was crossing a lovely
river of clear, bright water, wide and cool, a river of deep pools fringed with
reeds. Curlews and ospreys called loudly over it, chakravaka birds cried along
it, it teemed with turtles, crocodiles and fish, and it was bright with
sandbanks and islands.

"As soon as she saw the great caravan, Nala's glorious wife, the fine woman,
went up to it and came among the people. She looked like a madwoman, stricken
with grief, covered by half a cloth, thin, pale, dirty, her hair thick with
dust. When the people there saw her, some fled in fear, some stood lost in
thought, some cried out. Some laughed at her, others spoke against her. Some
took pity on her, Bharata, and asked her, 'Who are you? Whose are you, good
lady? What are you looking for in the forest? We were frightened when we saw
you. Are you a woman? Tell the truth. Are you the goddess of this forest, or of
the mountain, or of the quarters? We have come to you for refuge, good lady. Are
you a yakshi, or a rakshasi, or a mortal woman? Whatever you are, bring us good
fortune and protect us, blameless one. Make it so that this caravan goes safely
and quickly on its way, good lady. We have come to you for refuge.'

"When the caravan said this, Damayanti, the king's daughter, the good woman,
grieving at her husband's misfortune, answered them. She spoke to the caravan
and its leader, to all the people there, young, old and children, and to the men
at its head. 'Know that I am a woman, the daughter of a lord of men, a king's
daughter-in-law and a king's wife, longing to see my husband. The king of
Vidarbha is my father, and my husband is the king of the Nishadhas, the blessed
Nala. I am searching for him, the unconquered. If you know of the king, tell me
quickly of my love, of Nala, the tiger among kings, the destroyer of enemy
hosts.'

"The master of that great caravan, the caravan leader named Shuchi, said to her,
flawless in every limb, 'Listen to my words, good lady. I am the leader of the
caravan, you of the bright smile, its master. I see no man named Nala, glorious
one. I see elephants, leopards, buffalo, tigers, bears and deer in this hard
forest where no men live. So may the king of the yakshas, Manibhadra, be kind to
us today.'

"She said to all the merchants and to the caravan leader, 'Where is this caravan
going? Tell me.'

"'This caravan belongs to Subahu, the truthful king of the Chedis,' said the
caravan leader. 'It is going quickly to his country, for trade, daughter of
men.'"

**Divergences.**

* **Truth against the horse sacrifice.** The familiar saying that truth outweighs a thousand horse
  sacrifices is a rejected line here (\*259, after 3.61.16). In the CE the scale holds the four Vedas
  on one side and truth on the other (3.61.16).
* **A wife who outlives her husband.** A rejected couplet (\*275, after 3.61.84) calls a wife who lives
  on after her husband's death the lowest of women. Not in the CE.
* **Damayanti's complaint against the "gambler".** A rejected passage (\*281, after 3.61.119) has her
  call Nala a gambler who cut her cloth and left her. Not in the CE, where she speaks of him to the
  caravan only with praise.
