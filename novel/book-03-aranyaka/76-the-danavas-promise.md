# The Danavas' Promise

As the unforgiving King Duryodhana sat fasting to death, Shakuni son of Subala
spoke to comfort him, king. "You have heard what Karna said, Kaurava, and he
said it well. Why would you throw away in your folly the rich fortune I won for
you, best of kings, and give up your life for want of sense? Today I see that
you have never sat at the feet of the old. The man who does not check the joy or
the gloom that rises in him loses the fortune he has won, like an unfired pot in
water. Fortune does not stay with a king who is too fearful, too unmanly, too
slow, careless, or overcome by the senses through his vices.

"You have been treated well. How can you grieve at it as though it were the
opposite? Do not grieve and so destroy the good the Parthas have done. Where you
should rejoice and honour the Pandavas, you grieve instead, king of kings. This
is backwards. Be calm, do not give up your life, be content and remember the
good that was done. Give the Parthas their kingdom, and win fame and dharma.
When you have seen what they did, you will not be ungrateful. Make brotherhood
with the Pandavas, settle them, give them their father's kingdom, and then you
will find happiness."

When he heard Shakuni's words, Duryodhana looked at the heroic Duhshasana, who
had fallen at his feet, overcome with love for his brother. He raised the tamer
of enemies with his well-made arms, embraced him, and smelt the crown of his
head with love. Remembering the words of Karna and of the son of Subala, King
Duryodhana fell into the deepest despair. His whole self filled with shame, he
lost all hope. When he heard his friends, he said angrily, "I have no use for
dharma, wealth or pleasure, for lordship, for command or for enjoyments. Do not
hinder me. Go. My mind is made up. It is fixed on fasting to death. Go to the
city, all of you, and honour my elders."

They answered the crusher of enemies, "Your way is our way too, king of kings,
Bharata. How could we go into the city without you?"

His friends, his counsellors, his brothers and his people spoke to him in many
ways, but he would not be moved from his resolve. With his mind made up, the son
of Dhritarashtra spread a bed of darbha grass, touched water, made himself pure,
and lay on the ground. Wearing clothes of kusha grass and bark, he kept the
strictest restraint. The tiger among kings held his tongue, longing to go to
heaven. He made his offerings in his mind, and put away all outward rites.

---

Then the daityas and danavas who live in Patala, the fierce ones whom the gods
had beaten long ago, learned of his resolve. Knowing that Duryodhana's death
would be the ruin of their own side, they held a rite of the sacred fires to
call him to them. Skilled in spells, they carried out the rites taught in the
Upanishad, with the spells spoken by Brihaspati and Ushanas and those taught in
the Atharva Veda, with prayers and spells. Brahmins who had mastered the Veda
and its limbs, firm in their vows, poured milk as the offering into the fire
with spells, with great care.

When the rite was complete, a very wonderful spirit rose up there, yawning,
king, and said, "What shall I do?" The daityas, deeply pleased, said to her,
"Bring here King Duryodhana, son of Dhritarashtra, who is fasting to death." She
promised, "So be it," and went off, and in the blink of an eye she came to where
King Suyodhana was. She took the king and went into Rasatala, and in a moment
she told the danavas that he had been brought.

When they saw the king brought there by night, the danavas gathered, all
delighted, their eyes a little wide, and spoke to Duryodhana with pride.

"Ho, Suyodhana, king of kings, upholder of the Bharatas' line, always surrounded
by heroes and great men! Why have you done this rash thing, this fast to death?
The man who takes his own life goes down, and gets a name that brings him
disgrace. Wise men like you do not set themselves on acts that go against their
aims, that bring much harm and strike at the root. Check this thought, king. It
destroys dharma, wealth and happiness. It kills fame, power and courage, and it
swells the joy of your enemies.

"Hear the truth, lord, about your divine nature, king, and about how your body
was made, and then take heart. Long ago we won you by austerity from the great
lord Maheshvara. The whole upper part of your body was made of heaps of diamond.
Neither missiles nor weapons can pierce it, sinless one. The lower part of your
body the Goddess made of flowers, so that it charms women with its beauty. So
your body was made by the Lord and by the Goddess, best of kings, tiger among
kings. You are divine, not human.

"Kshatriyas of great courage, with Bhagadatta at their head, heroes who know the
divine weapons, will destroy your enemies. So enough of this despair. You have
nothing to fear. Heroic danavas have been born on earth to help you. Other
asuras will enter Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the others. Possessed by them, they
will put away pity and fight your enemies. When they strike in battle, best of
the Kurus, they will spare neither sons nor brothers, fathers nor kin, pupils
nor kinsmen, neither the young nor the old. Their inner selves will be overcome
and they will be possessed by danavas, without love. They will cast off their
love far away and strike at their kin, the tigers among men, gladly, their minds
clouded, bewildered by ignorance, by the fate ordained by the Maker. Calling out
to one another, 'You will not escape me alive,' standing on their manhood,
loosing every weapon and missile, boasting, best of the Kurus, they will make a
slaughter of the people.

"The great Pandavas too will fight back with all their power, and, joined to
fate, the mighty ones will kill them. Hosts of daityas and rakshasas, born in
the wombs of kshatriyas, will fight your enemies in battle with courage, king,
with maces and clubs and swords, and weapons high and low.

"As for the fear of Arjuna that is in you, hero, we have arranged a way to kill
Arjuna too. The soul of Naraka, who was killed, has taken the form of Karna.
Remembering that old enmity, hero, he will fight Keshava and Arjuna. That Karna,
proud in his courage, best of those who strike, great chariot fighter, will beat
Partha in battle for you, and all your enemies. The thunderbolt-wielder knows
this, and to protect Savyasachin he will take Karna's earrings and armour by a
trick. So we too have set daityas and rakshasas to this, by the hundred
thousand, those who are known as the samshaptakas. They will kill the hero
Arjuna. Do not grieve.

"You shall enjoy this earth without a rival, king. Do not drive us to despair.
It does not become you. If you are destroyed, Kaurava, our side will lose. Go,
hero, and never think another thought. You are always our refuge, as the
Pandavas are the gods'."

When they had said this, the daityas embraced that elephant among kings. The
bulls among the danavas comforted him, whom none could overcome, like a son.
They made his mind firm and spoke kind words to him, Bharata, and gave him
leave, saying, "Go, and win victory."

When they had let the strong-armed one go, the same spirit brought him back to
the very place where he had sat down to fast. The spirit set the hero down,
honoured him, took leave of the king, and vanished there and then.

---

When she had gone, King Duryodhana thought that all of it had been a dream,
Bharata. His thought was, "I shall beat the sons of Pandu in battle." Suyodhana
believed that Karna and the samshaptakas were set on killing Partha, the slayer
of enemies, and able to do it. So the hope of the wicked son of Dhritarashtra
that he would conquer the Pandavas grew firm, bull among the Bharatas.

Karna too, his mind and self possessed by the inner soul of Naraka, then set his
cruel mind on killing Arjuna. The heroic samshaptakas, their minds possessed by
rakshasas, overcome by passion and darkness, wanted Phalguna's death. And
Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the others, their minds overcome by danavas, lord of
the people, did not love the sons of Pandu as they had. King Suyodhana told no
one of this.

At the end of the night Karna Vaikartana joined his palms and, as if smiling,
spoke reasoned words to King Duryodhana. "A dead man does not conquer his
enemies. It is the living who see good things. Where are good things for the
dead, Kauraveya, and where is victory? This is no time for despair, or fear, or
death."

The strong-armed one embraced him with his arms and said, "Get up, king. Why are
you lying there? Why are you grieving, slayer of enemies? You have scorched your
enemies with your courage. How can you want to die? Or has fear come on you from
seeing Arjuna's might? I promise you truly, I will kill Arjuna in battle. When
the thirteenth year has passed, I take up my weapon by truth. I will bring the
Parthas into your power, lord of the people."

At these words of Karna, and because of what the daityas had said, and because
the others bowed before him, Suyodhana got up. He had heard the daityas' words,
and he had fixed his mind firmly in his heart.

Then the tiger among men made ready his army, crowded with chariots, elephants
and horses and thronged with foot soldiers. That great host set out like the
flood of the Ganga, king. With white parasols, banners and pure white
fly-whisks, with chariots, elephants and foot soldiers, the crowded army shone
like the sky in autumn when the clouds have cleared. Praised by the chief
brahmins with blessings for victory, like an emperor, taking the salutes of
joined palms, King Duryodhana son of Dhritarashtra went in front, blazing with
the highest splendour, with Karna and with the son of Subala, the gambler, king
of kings. All his brothers, with Duhshasana at their head, and Bhurishravas,
Somadatta and great King Bahlika, the upholders of the Kurus, followed that lion
among kings as he went, with chariots of many shapes, with horses and the best
of elephants. In a short time, king, they entered their own city.

**Divergences.**

* **Gandhari's gaze.** The popular story that Gandhari made Duryodhana's body invulnerable with her gaze is not in the CE (L-46). The CE's only account of how his body was made is the danavas' boast here (3.240.6–8): the upper body of diamond from Maheshvara, the lower of flowers from the Goddess.
* **The spirit's teeth.** A rejected line (\*1151) describes the spirit with sharp teeth and blazing hair. The CE only says she rose yawning (3.239.22). Not narrated.
* Other rejected lines (\*1147–\*1150, \*1152–\*1159) add small details, such as the danavas embracing him when he arrives and the city coming out to meet him. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.239.3 *pātram āmam ivāmbhasi*: "like an unfired pot in water".
* 3.239.10 *prītyājighrata mūrdhani*: "smelt the crown of his head", the old gesture of love.
* 3.239.17 *manasopacitiṃ kṛtvā nirasya ca bahiṣkriyāḥ*: "He made his offerings in his mind, and put away all outward rites." The sense is uncertain.
* 3.239.19 *karma vaitānasaṃbhavam*: "a rite of the sacred fires".
* 3.239.22 *kṛtyā*: "a spirit", a being raised by a rite to do a task. The text calls her *kṛtyā* throughout.
* 3.239.25 *rasātalam*: the underworld, the same as Patala in 3.239.18.
* 3.240.6 *vajrasaṃcayaiḥ*: "of heaps of diamond". *Vajra* can also be the thunderbolt; the novel follows the sense of hardness.
* 3.240.19 *hatasya narakasyātmā*: "the soul of Naraka, who was killed", the asura killed by Krishna.
* 3.240.22 *saṃśaptakāḥ*: "the samshaptakas", warriors sworn to kill or die. They appear in the war.
* 3.240.34: "King Suyodhana told no one of this." The narrator's statement is kept.
* 3.240.39 *satyenāyudham ālabhe*: "I take up my weapon by truth", an oath.
