# Rishyashringa

Then the son of Kunti set out, bull of the Bharatas, and came in turn to the two
rivers Nanda and Aparananda, which take away the fear of sin. When the king
reached the healthy mountain Hemakuta, he saw many strange and wonderful things
there, past all thought. There speaking brought clouds and showers of stones in
thousands, and people could not climb it and lost heart. The wind always blew
there, the god always rained, and the blessed Fire was seen there at evening and
at dawn. When the Pandava saw these many wonderful things, he asked Lomasha
about that wonder.

"Hear with a single mind, tamer of enemies, as I tell you what I have heard of
it before," said Lomasha. "On this peak of Rishabha there lived an ascetic named
Rishabha, who lived many hundreds of years, an ascetic, very quick to anger.
When others spoke to him, in his anger he said to the mountain, 'If anyone
speaks here, throw stones at him.' And the ascetic called the wind and said, 'No
sound.' So a man who speaks is stopped by a cloud. So that great seer did some
things in anger and forbade others, king.

"Long ago, king, so it is said, when the gods had gone to the Nanda, men who
wanted to see the gods suddenly followed them. The gods with Shakra at their
head did not want to be seen, and they made this place hard to reach, with the
mountain as a barrier. From that time, son of Kunti, men have never been able to
see this mountain, let alone climb it. No one who has not done austerity can see
this great mountain or climb it, son of Kunti. So hold your tongue. Here all the
gods always performed the finest sacrifices. Their signs are still seen today,
Bharata. This dūrvā grass looks like kusha grass, and the ground looks as if
strewn for a sacrifice, and these many trees are shaped like sacrificial posts,
lord of the people. Gods and seers still live here, Bharata, and their fire is
seen at evening and at dawn. For those who bathe here, son of Kunti, sin is
struck down at once. So bathe here, best of the Kurus, with your younger
brothers. Then, when you have bathed in the Nanda, you will go to the Kaushiki,
where Vishvamitra practised the fiercest and finest austerity."

Then the king and his company bathed there, and went to the Kaushiki, the holy,
lovely river of auspicious water.

---

"This is the Kaushiki, the holy river of the gods, bull of the Bharatas," said
Lomasha. "And here is Vishvamitra's lovely hermitage, shining. And here is the
holy hermitage of great Kashyapa, whose son was the ascetic Rishyashringa,
master of his senses. By the power of his austerity he made Vasava rain. In a
drought, the killer of Bala and Vritra rained out of fear of him. The splendid
lord, Kashyapa's son, was born of a doe. He did a great wonder in the land of
Lomapada. When the crops had come back, King Lomapada gave him his daughter
Shanta, as the Sun gave Savitri."

"How was Rishyashringa, Kashyapa's son, born of a doe?" said Yudhishthira. "How
could he have austerity, from so strange a union? Why did Shakra, the killer of
Bala and Vritra, rain out of fear of that wise boy when drought had come? What
was Shanta like, the princess of firm vows, who beguiled the mind of that
deer-born boy? And Lomapada, the royal seer, was known to be righteous. How was
it that the chastiser of Paka did not rain in his land? Tell me all this in
full, blessed one, just as it was, and the doings of Rishyashringa. I want to
hear it."

"Hear how Rishyashringa, the mighty son of the brahmin seer Vibhandaka, was
born," said Lomasha. "Vibhandaka's self was made by austerity, his seed was
never wasted, and he had the splendour of Prajapati. The splendid child was born
in a great lake, a boy honoured as an elder. Kashyapa, the seer honoured by the
divine seers, came to the great lake and practised austerity, and after a long
time he was worn out. When he saw the apsaras Urvashi, his seed fell as he was
touching the water, king, and a doe drank it then. Drinking it with the water in
her thirst, she became pregnant, king, because the rule cannot fail and because
what the gods have made must be. From that doe his son was born, the great seer
Rishyashringa, always given to austerity, and he grew up in the forest. The
great-hearted one had a deer's horn on his head, king, and so he became known as
Rishyashringa, 'Deer Horn'. He had never seen a human being except his father,
and so his mind was always on the chaste life, king.

"At this very time a friend of Dasharatha, known as Lomapada, was the lord of
the Angas. We have heard that he broke faith with the brahmins, and the lord of
the world was abandoned by the brahmins. Because the king had wronged his family
priest, the thousand-eyed god did not rain, as chance would have it, and his
people suffered. The lord of the earth asked brahmins rich in austerity and
wise, able to make the king of the gods rain, 'How may Parjanya rain? Find a
way.' When he urged them, the wise men told him their views.

"Then one fine sage said to the king, 'The brahmins are angry with you, king of
kings. Make amends. And bring Rishyashringa, the sage's son, lord of the earth,
a forest dweller who knows nothing of women and delights in simplicity. If that
great ascetic comes down into your land, king, Parjanya will rain at once. I
have no doubt of it.' When he heard these words, king, he made amends for
himself. He went and came back, when the brahmins were appeased, and when the
people saw the king return, they welcomed him.

"Then the lord of the Angas called his ministers, skilled in counsel, and set
about the bringing of Rishyashringa, when they had settled their plan. With
those ministers, learned in the texts, wise in affairs and grounded in policy,
the unshakable king found a way. The lord of the earth sent for the chief
courtesans, skilled in every art, and spoke to them. 'Bring Rishyashringa, the
seer's son, into my land by some means, lovely ones, by tempting him and winning
his trust.' Those women, afraid of the king and afraid of a curse, turned pale
and lost heart, and said it could not be done.

"But one old woman among them said to the king, 'I will try to bring that
ascetic, great king. But you must grant me the things I want. Then I will be
able to tempt Rishyashringa, the seer's son.' The king granted all she asked and
gave her much wealth and jewels of every kind. Then she took some women full of
beauty and youth, lord of the earth, and went straight to the forest.

---

"To do the king's work, at the king's bidding and by her own wit, she made a
hermitage on a boat, Bharata. It was adorned with artificial trees, with flowers
and fruit of every kind, and bushes and creepers of every kind that gave sweet
fruit at will. She made that boat hermitage very lovely and charming, wonderful
to see. Then she moored the boat not far from Kashyapa's hermitage and had men
spy out the sage's comings and goings. Then the courtesan told her clever
daughter what she must do, and when she saw a chance, while Kashyapa was away,
she sent her. The skilled girl went there, to the one who was always given to
austerity, and coming to that hermitage she saw the seer's son.

"'Is all well with the ascetics, sage?' said the courtesan's daughter. 'Have you
plenty of roots and fruit? Are you happy in this hermitage? I have come to see
you now. Is the austerity of the ascetics growing? Is your father's splendour
undiminished? Is he pleased with you, brahmin? Do you keep up your recitation,
Rishyashringa?'

"'You shine like a light, rich as you are,' said Rishyashringa. 'I think I
should greet you. I will gladly give you water for your feet, and fruit and
roots, as dharma requires. Sit as you please on a comfortable mat of kusha grass
covered with a black deerskin. Where is your hermitage? And what is this vow you
keep, brahmin? For you are like a god.'

"'My hermitage is lovely, son of Kashyapa,' said the courtesan's daughter,
'three leagues beyond this mountain. There it is our own dharma not to be
greeted, and we do not touch water for our feet.'

"'I will give you ripe fruit,' said Rishyashringa, 'bhallataka and amalaka,
parushaka, ingudi and dhanvana. Have your fill of priyala.'

"She put all of them aside and gave him costly foods instead. They were full of
flavour and very fine to look at, and Rishyashringa liked them. She gave him
fragrant garlands and bright, shining clothes and the finest drinks. Then he was
glad, and played, and laughed. She played with a ball at his side, bending like
a creeper heavy with fruit. Touching his limbs with hers, she embraced
Rishyashringa again and again. She bent and broke the flowering sarja, ashoka
and tilaka trees, and as if shy, overcome with passion, she tempted the great
seer's son. When she saw Rishyashringa changed, she pressed his body again and
again, and then, looking back, she slowly went away, giving the fire offering as
her excuse.

"When she had gone, Rishyashringa was mad with love and out of his mind. His
heart went with her, and he felt empty, and sighed, and looked stricken. Then, a
moment later, Vibhandaka Kashyapa appeared, his eyes tawny like a lion's,
covered with hair to the tips of his nails, given to recitation, steady in his
conduct and his meditation. He came and saw his son sitting alone, brooding, his
mind turned upside down, sighing again and again, looking upwards, and
Vibhandaka said to his wretched son, 'Why is no firewood gathered, my son? Have
you made the fire offering today? Have you cleaned the ladles, and brought the
cow for the offering to her calf today? You are not as you were before, son. You
are lost in thought and out of your mind, and very wretched. What is it today? I
ask you, who came here today?'

---

"'A young ascetic came here, with matted hair,' said Rishyashringa, 'neither
short nor too tall, with a fine mind, the colour of gold, with long lotus eyes,
shining like a son of the gods. He was rich in beauty, bright as the sun, his
eyes very white and very black, finer than a chakora's. His matted hair was
dark, clear and fragrant, very long, tied with golden strings. On his neck there
was something like a support, and it shone like lightning in the sky. Below his
neck were two lovely round things, without hair. His waist was slender at the
navel, and his hips were very wide. Under his bark garment a golden girdle
shone, like this belt of mine.

"'And there was another wonderful thing about him. Something jingled at his
feet. And on his wrists were strings that jingled too, like this rosary of mine.
When he moved, they sang like geese on a lake, mad with joy. His bark garments
were wonderful to see, not like these of mine. His face was wonderful, and when
he spoke it gladdened my heart. His voice was like a male cuckoo's, and when I
heard it my inmost self was shaken. As a forest in the middle of spring, stirred
by the breeze, breathes out its scent, so he breathes out the finest holy scent
when the wind blows on him, father.

"'His matted hair was well tied, parted in two, lying even on his forehead. His
ears were covered, as it were, with bright, lovely rings. He struck a round,
many-coloured fruit with his right hand, and it touched the ground and sprang up
high again and again, wonderful to see. He struck it and turned about like a
tree shaken by the wind. When I saw him, father, like a son of the immortals, I
felt the greatest love and delight. He embraced my body again and again, took
hold of my hair, bent down my face, put his mouth on my mouth and made a sound,
and that filled me with joy.

"'He did not think much of water for his feet, nor of these fruits I brought
him. "This is my vow," he told me, and he gave me other fruits, new ones. I ate
those fruits, and these are not their equal in taste. Their skin is not like the
skin of these, and these have no kernels like those. That noble one gave me very
tasty water to drink. As soon as I drank it I felt still greater joy, and the
ground seemed to rock. These bright, fragrant garlands tied with silk are his.
He scattered them here and went back to his own hermitage, shining with
austerity. His going has left me out of my mind, and my body seems to burn. I
want to go to him quickly, and have him here always, moving about. I am going to
him, father. What is that vow of his called? I want to practise it with him, as
he practises his fierce austerity.'

"'Those are rakshasas, son, going about in that wonderful form,' said
Vibhandaka. 'They are beyond compare in beauty, and very terrible, and they
always plan to hinder austerity. They take lovely shapes, my son, and tempt with
every kind of trick. Those fierce ones throw sages in the forest down from
happiness and from the worlds. A sage who controls himself, who seeks the worlds
of the good, should never go near them. When they have hindered ascetics, the
evil ones enjoy themselves, sinless one. Those drinks, son, are evil spirits,
not to be drunk, the drink of bad men. And these bright, shining, fragrant
garlands are not for sages.'

---

"Saying 'They are rakshasas,' Vibhandaka kept his son back and searched for her.
When he had not found her in three days, he came back to his hermitage. When
Kashyapa went out again to gather fruit, as ascetics do, the courtesan went
again to tempt the sage Rishyashringa. As soon as Rishyashringa saw her, he was
glad, and ran to her in a flurry and said, 'Let us go to your hermitage before
my father comes.'

"Then, king, by that trick they put Kashyapa's only son on board, untied the
boat, and, tempting him in every way, came to the lord of the Angas. He set that
lovely boat, which had been brought over, within sight of a hermitage, took it
from the bank, and made a wonderful forest there called the King's Hermitage.
The king settled Vibhandaka's only son in the women's quarters, and then he saw
the god suddenly rain, and the world fill with water.

"His wish fulfilled, Lomapada gave his daughter Shanta to Rishyashringa. And he
took steps to answer the anger of the father, and had cattle set out and fields
ploughed along the roads. For Vibhandaka's coming, the king had many beasts and
brave herdsmen set there, and gave them orders. 'When the great seer Vibhandaka,
longing for his son, asks you, you must say to him with joined hands, "These
cattle and fields are your son's. What can we do to please you, great seer? We
are all your servants, bound by your word."'

"Then the sage, fierce in his anger, came to his hermitage with the roots and
fruit he had gathered. He searched there and did not see his son, and he was
furious. Torn with anger, suspecting the king's doing, he went to Champa,
wanting to burn up the king of the Angas and his land. Tired and hungry,
Kashyapa came to those rich herdsmen's camps. The herdsmen honoured him as the
rules require, and he spent the night there like a king. Having been treated
with great honour by them, he said, 'Whose men are you, good people?' Then they
all came up and said, 'This wealth has been set aside for your son.'

"Honoured in place after place and hearing those sweet words, his passion mostly
calmed, he came glad to the lord of the Angas in his city. Honoured by that bull
among men, he saw his son like Indra in heaven, and saw his daughter-in-law
Shanta there, like lightning flashing out. When he saw the villages and the
camps and his son, and Shanta, his great anger was calmed, and Vibhandaka showed
the lord of the earth the greatest favour, king of men.

"The great seer left his son there, who was like the sun and fire in power, and
said to him, 'When a son is born to you, come to the forest, when you have done
all that pleases this king.' Rishyashringa did as he said and went to where his
father was. And Shanta served him as she should, as Rohini in the sky serves the
moon. She was to him as fortunate Arundhati was to Vasishtha, or Lopamudra to
Agastya, as Damayanti was to Nala, and as Shachi to the wielder of the
thunderbolt. She was as Nadayani Indrasena, always obedient to Mudgala,
descendant of Ajamidha. So Shanta served Rishyashringa in the forest with love,
king of men.

"This is his holy hermitage, shining, adorning the great lake, of that man of
holy fame. Bathe here, your purpose achieved and purified, and then go on to the
other fords, king."

**Divergences.**

* **Shanta's father.** In the familiar version Shanta is Dasharatha's daughter, adopted by Lomapada,
  and Rishyashringa later performs Dasharatha's sacrifice for sons. In the CE Lomapada is only called
  Dasharatha's friend (3.110.19); Shanta is Lomapada's daughter (3.110.5), and the sacrifice is not told.
* **The doe's curse.** A rejected passage (\*543) says the doe was a divine maiden under Brahma's curse,
  to be freed when she bore the sage's son. Not in the CE.
* **Sita and Draupadi.** Rejected lines (\*558) add Sita and Draupadi to the list of devoted wives. Not in
  the CE.

**Choices.**

* 3.109.9: Rishabha's orders to the mountain and the wind are as the text gives them.
* 3.109.11 *sahasā*: "suddenly".
* 3.110.2: Lomasha calls Vibhandaka "Kashyapa", of Kashyapa's line; both names are kept.
* 3.110.17 *ṛśyaśṛṅgam*: "a deer's horn"; the name is explained in the text.
* 3.110.23 *parjanyaḥ*: Parjanya, the rain god.
* 3.111.12: the fruits are named as the text names them.
* 3.112: Rishyashringa, who has never seen a woman, describes her as a young ascetic in his own terms; kept
  close to the text, including the "two round things" of 3.112.3.

  for Yudhishthira, of Ajamidha's line.
