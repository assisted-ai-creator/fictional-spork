# Dharma's Boons

At the yaksha's word the Pandavas rose, and at once the hunger and thirst of all
of them went away.

"You stand on one foot in the lake, never beaten," said Yudhishthira. "I ask
you, what god are you? I do not think you are a yaksha. Are you one of the
Vasus, or one of the Rudras, or the best of the Maruts, or the
thunderbolt-wielder, lord of the thirty gods? For these brothers of mine could
fight a hundred thousand warriors, and I see no means by which they could have
been struck down. I see their senses as those of men who have woken from a good
sleep. Are you our friend, or are you our father?"

"I am your father, my son," said the yaksha, "Dharma, you of gentle valour. Know
that I have come here wanting to see you, bull of the Bharatas. Fame, truth,
self-control, purity, straightness, modesty, steadiness, giving, austerity and
chastity: these are my bodies. Non-violence, equality, calm, austerity, purity
and freedom from envy: know that these are my doors. For you are always dear to
me. By good fortune you delight in the five. By good fortune you have conquered
the six-footed one: two at the beginning, two in the middle, and two at the end,
that lead to the other world. I am Dharma, bless you, and I came here wanting to
test you. I am pleased with your non-cruelty, and I shall give you a boon,
sinless one. Choose a boon, king of kings, for I will give it, sinless one.
Those men who are devoted to me never come to a bad end."

"The man whose fire-sticks the deer took and went off with," said Yudhishthira,
"may his fires not be lost. Let that be my first boon."

"It was I who took that brahmin's fire-sticks, son of Kunti," said Dharma, "in
the form of a deer, to test you, lord."

"I give it," the blessed one answered. "Choose another boon, bless you, you who
are like an immortal."

"Twelve years in the forest are over, and the thirteenth has come," said
Yudhishthira. "May no men anywhere recognise us while we live through it."

"I give it," the blessed one answered, and comforted Kunti's son, whose valour
was truth, again. "Even if you roam this earth in your own forms, no one in the
three worlds will recognise you, Bharata. By my grace, upholders of the Kurus,
you will pass this thirteenth year hidden and unrecognised in Virata's city.
Whatever form each of you imagines in his mind, each of you will take as he
wishes. And give these fire-sticks to the brahmin, for I took them in the form
of a deer to test you. Take a third boon, my son, matchless and great. For you
were born of me, king, and Vidura shares a portion of me."

"I have seen you, the god of gods, eternal, with my own eyes," said
Yudhishthira. "Whatever boon you give me in your pleasure, I shall take, father.
May I always conquer greed, delusion and anger, lord. May my mind always be set
on giving, austerity and truth."

"You have all these virtues by your nature, Pandava," said Dharma. "You are
Dharma again yourself, and it will be as you have said."

When he had said this, the blessed Dharma, who brings the worlds into being,
vanished. The high-minded Pandavas, who had come together again after a good
sleep, went back to the hermitage, all their weariness gone, and gave the
fire-sticks to the ascetic brahmin.

A man who recites this great story of the rising again and the reunion, which
adds to the fame of father and son, becomes master of his senses and
self-controlled, and will live a hundred years with sons and grandsons. And the
mind of men who know this story is never drawn to what is against dharma, to
setting friends at odds, to taking what belongs to others, to touching another's
wife, or to meanness.

---

Given leave by Dharma, the Pandavas, whose valour was truth, were to live the
thirteenth year in hiding, unrecognised. The wise brothers, strict in their
vows, sat down together near the ascetics who had lived with them out of
devotion during their forest life. The great, cultured brothers, firm in their
vows, joined their palms and spoke to them, wanting to take their leave of that
dwelling.

"You all know how the sons of Dhritarashtra took our kingdom by a trick, and
made us poor many times," they said. "We have lived hard in the forest for
twelve years. The remaining time, the thirteenth year, is to be spent in hiding.
We shall live it hidden. You should give us your leave. Wicked Suyodhana, and
Karna with the son of Subala, if they knew, would do us some wrong, being our
bitter enemies. They are ready in their conduct and ready with men among the
townspeople and their own people. Will it be ours again, to be settled in our
own kingdoms together with the brahmins, in our own kingship?"

When he had said this, pure Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, overcome by grief
and sorrow, fainted, his throat choked with tears. Then all the brahmins with
his brothers comforted him, and Dhaumya spoke to the king words of great
meaning.

"King, you are learned, self-controlled, true to your promises and master of
your senses. Men like you are not bewildered in any misfortune. Even the gods
have met misfortunes many times, and have lived in hiding here and there, great
as they are, to hold down their enemies. Indra went to the Nishadhas and lived
hidden in a hermitage on a mountain slope, and so did his work of holding down
the strength of his enemies. Vishnu took a horse's head, and before that lived
long unrecognised in Aditi's womb, to kill the daityas. You have heard how, in
the hidden form of a dwarf, in the form of a brahmin, he took Bali's kingdom
from him with his strides. You have heard all that the brahmin seer Aurva did in
the worlds, my son, living hidden in a thigh. You have heard, you who know
dharma, what Hari did in hiding when Vritra was held down, entering Shakra's
thunderbolt. You have heard all that the Fire did for the gods when he entered
the waters and lived there hidden. In the same way Vivasvat, of the highest
energy, living hidden on the earth, burned up all his enemies everywhere, my
son. Vishnu, of terrible deeds, living hidden in Dasharatha's house, killed
ten-necked Ravana in battle. So these great ones, hidden here and there,
conquered their enemies in battle. So you too will conquer."

Comforted by Dhaumya's words, Yudhishthira, who knew dharma, did not waver, by
his learning in the treatises and his own understanding. Then mighty Bhimasena,
strong-armed, the best of the strong, spoke to the king, cheering him with his
words.

"Out of regard for you, great king, the bearer of Gandiva has done nothing rash,
with his mind following dharma. I have always held back Sahadeva and Nakula,
slayers of enemies, of terrible valour, able to destroy them. We shall not
abandon whatever you set us to. You arrange it all, and we shall quickly conquer
our enemies."

When Bhimasena had said this, the brahmins gave the highest blessings, took
leave of the Bharatas, and went each to his own home. All the chief men learned
in the Veda, the ascetics and the sages, spoke their blessings as was proper,
longing to see them again. The five learned, heroic Pandavas rose with Dhaumya
and set out, taking Krishna with them, Bharata. Because of an omen, they went a
krosha from that place. There the tigers among men, ready the next day to begin
their life in hiding, sat down to take counsel. All of them were skilled in the
separate treatises, all were masters of counsel, and all knew the times for
peace and war.

**Divergences.**

* **The six-footed one.** A rejected line (\*1387) explains the "six-footed one" as desire and anger, greed and delusion, fear and despair. The CE says only "two at the beginning, two in the middle, and two at the end" (3.298.9). Not narrated.
* **The yaksha as Dharma.** The yaksha is Dharma, Yudhishthira's father, testing him (3.298.6–10). The deer that took the fire-sticks was Dharma too (3.298.13, 3.298.20). See L-28.
* Other rejected lines (\*1388–\*1389) add small phrases. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.298.2 *sarasy ekena pādena tiṣṭhantam*: "You stand on one foot in the lake".
* 3.298.7–8: Dharma's "bodies" and "doors" are listed as the text lists them.
* 3.298.9 *pañcasu raktaḥ*: "you delight in the five". The text does not name them.
* 3.298.21: "Vidura shares a portion of me", as told in Book 1.
* 3.298.27–28: the verses on the merit of hearing the story are the CE's own, kept in the narrator's voice.
* 3.299.5: "They are ready in their conduct and ready with men among the townspeople and their own people." The sense is uncertain.
* 3.299.11–18: Dhaumya's examples are kept in the text's order. The "horse's head" (3.299.12) refers to Vishnu's form as Hayagriva.
* 3.299.28 *nimittataḥ*: "because of an omen". The text does not say what omen.
