# Hanuman

The heroes, tigers among men, kept themselves very pure and lived there six
nights, longing to see Dhananjaya. The Pandavas took their pleasure there,
enjoying themselves in that fine and lovely forest, which delighted every
creature. Its trees were open in flower and bent low with fruit. It was lovely
on every side, loud with flocks of cuckoos, and its trees had thick, glossy
leaves and cool, pleasant shade. The Pandavas were glad to look at the bright
lakes with their clear water, shining all over with lotuses and water lilies. A
fragrant wind blew there, pleasant to the touch, delighting all the Pandavas,
with Krishna and the bulls among brahmins.

Then, as it happened, a wind from the north-east carried a divine lotus, with a
thousand petals, bright as the sun. The Panchali saw it, a pure water flower of
divine scent, lovely, fallen on the ground where the wind had brought it. The
lovely woman came to that lovely saugandhika lotus, the finest of all. She was
overjoyed, king, and said to Bhimasena, "Look, Bhima, at this divine, beautiful
flower, the finest of all, full of scent and perfect in shape. It delights my
heart. I will give this one to the Dharma King, scorcher of enemies. Bring me
more for my pleasure, in the Kamyaka hermitage again. If I am dear to you,
Partha, bring me many of these. I want to take them back to the Kamyaka
hermitage." When she had said this to Bhimasena, the blameless Panchali took the
flower and went to the Dharma King.

Bhima understood the wish of the queen he loved. Bhima of terrible courage, the
bull among men, wanted to please her. Longing to fetch more flowers, he set off
quickly, facing into the wind, the way the flower had come. He took his
gold-backed bow and his arrows like venomous snakes. Like an angry lion, like an
elephant in rut, seeking to please Draupadi, the strong one rushed at the
mountain, trusting in the strength of his arms, free of fear and bewilderment.

The slayer of enemies ranged that lovely mountain where kinnaras go. Its rocks
were dark blue and covered with trees, creepers and bushes. It was bright with
ores, trees, beasts and birds of many colours, full of every ornament, like an
arm of the earth raised high. His eyes were fixed on the slopes of Gandhamadana,
lovely in every season, and in his heart he thought over her wish. He went on,
of boundless courage, his ears, mind and eyes held by the calls of the cuckoos
and the humming of the bees. Full of splendour, he smelled the strong scent that
rose from the flowers of every season, and he was as unbridled as a rutting
elephant in the forest. His father took away his weariness with the wind of
Gandhamadana, cool with his father's touch, and the hair of his body stood up
with joy. For the sake of the flower the tamer of enemies stirred up the
mountain, where yakshas, gandharvas, gods and hosts of brahmin seers go.

The mountain seemed painted with fingers by clean streaks of ore, gold, black
and silver, laid on in uneven lines. With clouds clinging to its sides it seemed
to dance, winged. The water falling from its springs seemed to hang it with
strings of pearls. It had lovely rivers, bowers and waterfalls, and caves. Many
peacocks danced there to the sound of the apsarases' anklets. Its rocks were
rubbed by the tusk tips of the elephants that guard the quarters. The unstirred
waters running out of its rivers made it seem that its garment had slipped.
Deer, grazing calmly nearby with mouthfuls of grass, not knowing what fear is,
watched him with curiosity.

Shaking the tangles of creepers again and again with the speed of his thighs,
the glorious son of Kunti, son of the Wind, went on as if at play. Fine-eyed, he
was set on doing what his beloved wished. He was tall, like a golden palm, built
like a lion, young. He had the stride of a rutting elephant and the speed of a
rutting elephant. His eyes were coppery like a rutting elephant's, and he could
hold off a rutting elephant. The unseen wives of the yakshas and gandharvas,
sitting beside their lovers, turned and stirred to watch him. The Pandava ranged
the lovely slopes of Gandhamadana as if he were offering a new form of beauty
for sale.

He remembered the many hardships Duryodhana had caused, and he was set on
pleasing Draupadi, who lived in the forest. He thought, "With Arjuna gone to
heaven and me gone too, what will noble Yudhishthira do about the flowers? Out
of love, that best of men will surely not let Nakula and Sahadeva go, for he
does not trust the forest." Thinking, "How can I find the flowers quickly?", the
tiger among men went on as fast as the king of the birds.

Vrikodara shook the earth with his feet like a thunderclap at the turn of the
moon, and terrified the herds of elephants, as fast as the wind. The mighty one
crushed hosts of lions and tigers, and uprooted great trees and smashed them
with his chest. The joy of Pandu dragged the creepers and vines along by his
speed, like an elephant wanting to climb higher and higher to the mountain top,
roaring very loudly like a cloud with lightning. At that terrible sound, and the
sound of his bow, lord, the herds of deer fled in fear on every side.

Then the strong-armed one saw, on the slopes of Gandhamadana, a lovely grove of
plantains, many leagues wide. The mighty one went at it fast to churn it up,
breaking trees of every kind like a great elephant with temples running. Bhima,
the best of the strong, tore up plantain trunks as tall as many palm trees and
flung them about with force. Then many great creatures came at him, herds of
ruru deer and elephants, and buffalo that live in the water. Lions and tigers
rushed at Bhimasena in anger, jaws wide, most terrible, roaring very fiercely.
Then the son of the Wind, in anger, trusting in the strength of his arms, killed
elephant with elephant and lion with lion, and mighty Pandava killed the others
with blows of his palm. Struck down by Bhima, the lions, tigers and hyenas all
crept away in fear, and dropped dung and urine.

The glorious, mighty son of Pandu brushed them aside and quickly entered the
forest, filling the quarters with his noise. At that fierce sound, and at
Bhimasena's roar, all the beasts and birds deep in the forest were terrified.
Hearing that sound, raised suddenly by the beasts and birds, water birds rose in
thousands, their wings wet. Seeing those flocks of water birds, the bull of the
Bharatas followed them and saw a lovely, very great lake. Golden plantain
groves, stirred by a gentle breeze, seemed to fan the unruffled lake as they
spread along its shores. The mighty one quickly went down into the lake, full of
lotuses and water lilies, and played there hard, like a great unbridled
elephant. When he had played in it a long time, he came out, of boundless
splendour.

Then he plunged quickly into that forest of many trees, and the Pandava blew his
conch loudly with all his breath. At the sound of the conch, at Bhimasena's roar
and the fierce slapping of his arms, the mountain caves seemed to roar. That
slapping was loud as the crash of a thunderbolt. Hearing it, the lions asleep in
the mountain caves let out a great roar. The elephants, terrified by the lions'
roaring, let out a very great cry too, Bharata, and it filled the mountain.

---

Hearing that noise, the huge-bodied monkey named Hanuman, a bull among monkeys,
who was asleep, yawned. He was lying in the middle of the plantain grove, in the
power of sleep. Yawning, he lashed his long tail, raised like Indra's banner,
with a sound like Indra's thunderbolt. From the mouths of its caves the mountain
sent out the sound of his tail all round, as a cow lets out a bellow. The sound
of his tail drowned the cry of the rutting elephants and went on over the bright
mountain slopes.

When Bhimasena heard it, the hair of his body stood on end, and he went through
the plantain grove looking for where the sound came from. Then the strong-armed
one saw the lord of the monkeys on a broad rock in the middle of the plantain
grove. He was hard to look at, like a mass of lightning, tawny as a mass of
lightning, flickering like a mass of lightning. His short, thick neck was set on
his crossed arms. Most of his body was in his shoulders, so that he was slender
at the waist. He shone with his tail, thick with long hair, its tip a little
bent, raised like a banner. His face had red lips, a copper-red tongue and
mouth, red ears and moving brows, and his fangs were round at the tips. It was
like the moon with its rays. Adorned with the bright white inside his mouth, and
with his mane, it was like a heap of ashoka flowers. Of great splendour among
the golden plantains, blazing with his own body like a fire with its flames, he
looked about unafraid with honey-coloured eyes. He was the finest of monkeys, a
hero, huge in body and very strong.

Bhima of terrible courage went up to him quickly and roared a lion's roar to
wake the monkey. The beasts and birds were terrified at Bhima's roar. Hanuman, a
being of great spirit, opened his eyes a little and looked at him with contempt,
with his honey-coloured eyes. Then the monkey spoke to the man with a smile.

"I was ill and sleeping happily," he said. "Why have you woken me? You know
better. You should be kind to creatures. We do not know dharma, for we are born
as animals. Men have understanding and are kind to living things. How can wise
men like you give themselves to cruel acts that harm body, speech and mind, and
destroy dharma? You do not know dharma. You have not waited on the old. In your
little wisdom you destroy the forest beasts. Tell me who you are, and why you
have come to this forest, which has nothing to do with men or with human ways.
Beyond here the mountain cannot be crossed. It is very hard to climb. There is
no road here, hero, except the road of the siddhas. I stop you out of pity and
friendship, mighty one. You cannot go further. Be calm, lord. Eat these roots
and fruits, like the drink of immortality, and turn back, if you will take my
advice."

When Bhimasena, the hero, the grinder of enemies, heard these words of the wise
lord of monkeys, he said, "Who are you, and why have you taken a monkey's body?
It is a kshatriya who asks you, of the class next after the brahmins. I am a
Kaurava, of the line of the Moon, carried in Kunti's womb, a Pandava, son of the
Wind, known as Bhimasena."

Hanuman, son of the Wind, took Bhimasena's words with a smile, and said to the
son of the Wind, "I am a monkey. I will not give you the road you want. Better
turn back. Do not come to harm."

"Harm or anything else," said Bhima, "I am not asking you, monkey. Give me the
road. Get up. Do not come to harm."

"I have no strength to get up," said Hanuman. "I am troubled by sickness. If you
must go on, jump over me and go."

"The Supreme Self, without qualities, stands filling your body," said Bhima. "He
is to be known by knowledge. I will not insult him or leap over him. If I had
not come to know him, the maker of beings, from the scriptures, I would leap
over you and this mountain as Hanuman leapt the ocean."

"Who is this Hanuman who leapt the ocean?" said Hanuman. "I ask you, best of the
Kurus. Tell me, if you can."

"He is my brother," said Bhima, "praised for his virtues, gifted with wisdom,
spirit and strength, the hero, the bull among monkeys, very famous in the
Ramayana. For Rama's wife that lord of the monkeys leapt the ocean, a hundred
leagues wide, in a single bound. That mighty hero is my brother. I am his equal
in splendour, in strength, in courage and in battle, and I am able to punish
you. Get up. Give me the road, or see my manhood today. If you do not do as I
say, I will send you to the house of Yama."

Hanuman saw that he was drunk with strength and proud of the might of his arms.
Laughing at him in his heart, he said, "Be kind. I have no strength to get up,
for I am old, sinless one. Out of pity for me, move this tail aside and go."

Then Bhima, smiling with contempt, took hold of the great monkey's tail with his
left hand, and he could not move it. Then he tried to heave it up with both
arms, the tail raised like Indra's bow. Mighty Bhima could not lift it even with
both arms. His brows went up, his eyes rolled, his face knotted into a frown and
his body ran with sweat, and still he could not lift it. For all his effort,
glorious Bhima, shaken by trying to lift the tail, stood by the monkey's side
with his face down in shame.

The son of Kunti bowed low and said with joined hands, "Be kind, tiger among
monkeys. Forgive my harsh words. Are you a siddha, or a god, or a gandharva, or
a guhyaka? I ask you: tell me, if you will, who you are, wearing a monkey's
shape."

"Since you are curious to know me, tamer of enemies," said Hanuman, "hear all of
it, joy of the Pandavas. I was born by the Wind, the life of the world, of
Kesarin's wife, lotus-eyed one. I am the monkey named Hanuman. All the leaders
of the monkey troops, of great courage, waited on Sugriva, son of the Sun, and
Valin, son of Shakra, both kings of all the monkeys, and on me, grinder of
enemies. I had a friendship with Sugriva, as the wind has with fire. Then for
some reason he was wronged by his brother, and Sugriva lived a long time with me
on Rishyamuka.

"Then a hero, a son of Dasharatha, mighty, named Rama, walked this earth, Vishnu
in human form. Seeking to please his father, with his wife and his younger
brother, bow in hand, the best of bowmen went to live in the Dandaka forest.
Ravana carried off his wife by force from Janasthana, when he had tricked the
wise Raghava with a creature in the shape of a deer. Robbed of his wife, Raghava
was searching for her with his brother when he saw Sugriva, bull among monkeys,
on a mountain top. The great Raghava made friends with him. He killed Valin and
set Sugriva in the kingdom, and Sugriva sent the monkeys out to search for Sita.

"We set out with millions of monkeys in the direction we were sent, and there a
vulture gave us news of Sita. Then, to win success for Rama, whose deeds never
tire, I suddenly leapt the ocean, a hundred leagues wide. I saw the queen in
Ravana's house, and I came back again after making my name known there. Then the
hero Rama killed all those rakshasas and won back his wife, as the lost Vedas
were won back. When Rama was established, hero, I asked him this: 'As long as
the story of Rama lasts in the worlds, slayer of enemies, so long may I live.'
And he said, 'So be it.' Rama ruled the kingdom for ten thousand years and ten
hundred years, and then he went to heaven.

"So here, my son, the apsarases and gandharvas always delight me, sinless one,
singing the deeds of that hero. This road cannot be travelled by mortals, joy of
the Kurus. So I have blocked this road of yours, which the gods use, so that no
one should insult you or curse you, Bharata. This is the divine road of the
gods. Men do not go here. And the lake you came for is close by."

**Divergences.**

* **Hanuman knows his brother.** A rejected passage (\*708) has Hanuman recognise Bhima and block the road on
  purpose; another (\*707) calls him "Rama's beloved friend". In the CE he is woken by the noise and asks who Bhima
  is (3.146.78). See L-132.
* **Bhima's plan to kill him.** A rejected passage (\*722) has Bhima think of dragging the weak monkey by the tail to
  Yama's world. Not narrated.
* **Lanka and the bridge.** In Hanuman's account the CE says only that he saw Sita and came back after making his name
  known (3.147.35). The burning of Lanka, the bridge, Vibhishana and the return to Ayodhya are in rejected passages
  (\*733–\*736). See L-134.
* **Sita's gift.** A rejected line (\*738) says that by Sita's favour divine pleasures always come to him. In the CE it is
  the apsarases and gandharvas who delight him with Rama's story (3.147.39).
* **Sampati.** The vulture who gives news of Sita is unnamed here (3.147.33); the name Sampati is only in rejected lines
  (\*730, \*731).
