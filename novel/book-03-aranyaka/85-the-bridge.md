# The Bridge

"Then, while Rama sat there with them, the best of the monkeys gathered at
Sugriva's command," said Markandeya. "Valin's glorious father-in-law Sushena
came to Rama with a thousand crores of swift monkeys. Gaja and Gavaya, lords of
the monkeys, of great valour, were each seen with a hundred crores. Gavaksha, a
golangula of terrible appearance, was seen leading sixty thousand crores, great
king. Gandhamadana, famous and living on Gandhamadana, led a thousand crores of
fierce monkeys. The wise and mighty monkey named Panasa led ten, twelve, thirty
and five crores. Glorious Dadhimukha, an old monkey but still valiant, led a
great army of monkeys of terrible energy. Jambavan was seen with a hundred
thousand crores of black bears with marks on their faces, terrible in their
deeds. These and many others, leaders of the leaders of monkey troops, beyond
counting, great king, gathered for Rama's sake.

"There was a wild noise everywhere of monkeys running here and there, the colour
of shirisha flowers, roaring like lions. Some were like mountain peaks, some
like buffaloes, some like autumn clouds, their faces red as ground vermilion.
Leaping up, dropping down, bounding along, others shaking up the dust, the
monkeys gathered from every side. That great host of monkeys, like the full
ocean, made its camp there with Sugriva's leave.

"When the lords of the monkeys had all come together, on a fine day, under a
favourable constellation, at an auspicious hour, glorious Raghava set out with
Sugriva, with that army drawn up as if to turn the worlds upside down. Hanuman,
son of the wind, was at the head of the army, and fearless Saumitri guarded the
rear. The two Raghavas, with their lizard-skin finger guards bound on, shone
there surrounded by the great monkey chiefs, like the moon and the sun among the
planets. That monkey army, armed with sal and palm trees and rocks, shone like a
great field of rice at sunrise. Guarded by Nala, Nila, Angada, Kratha, Mainda
and Dvivida, the great army went to accomplish Raghava's purpose.

"Camping without trouble in fine places rich in roots and fruits, with plenty of
honey and meat, with water, and pleasant, and on the mountain slopes too, that
monkey army came to the salt ocean. That force, with its many banners, like a
second ocean, reached the forest on the shore and made its camp.

"Then glorious Dasharathi said to Sugriva, in the midst of the chief monkeys,
words that fitted the moment. 'What way do you think there is of crossing the
ocean? This army is great, and the ocean is hard to cross.'

"Then some of the monkeys, who thought themselves clever, said that the monkeys
could leap the sea, but not the whole army. Some decided on boats, and some on
rafts of various kinds. But Rama calmed them all and said, 'No. Not all the
monkeys, brave as they are, can cross an ocean a hundred yojanas wide. That is
not your final thought. There are not boats enough to carry the army across. And
how could one like me do harm to the merchants? The enemy would strike at our
army, spread out as it is, where it was weak. I do not like crossing on rafts
and floats either. I shall approach the ocean by a proper means. I shall lie
down fasting, and then he will show himself to me. If he does not show me the
way, then I shall burn him with great weapons that cannot be stopped, blazing
more fiercely than fire and wind.'

"When he had said this, Raghava, with Saumitri, touched water and lay down on a
bed of kusha grass to ask the Ocean, as the rite prescribes. Then the Ocean, the
god, the lord of rivers and streams, showed himself to Raghava in a dream,
glorious, surrounded by hosts of water creatures, and by the mines of jewels in
their hundreds. Speaking sweetly, calling him 'Son of Kausalya', he said, 'Tell
me what help I can give you here, bull among men.'

"'I am an Ikshvaku, your kinsman,' Rama said to him. 'I want you to give my army
a road, lord of rivers and streams, by which I can go and kill Dashagriva, the
shame of the Paulastyas. If you will not give me a road when I ask like this, I
shall dry you up with arrows charged by the spells of divine weapons.'

"When the home of Varuna heard Rama say this, he stood with joined palms in
distress and said, 'I do not wish to stand against you. I am no hindrance to
you. But hear what I say, Rama, and when you have heard it, do what must be
done. If I give you a road for your army to march by your command, others too
will command me in the same way by the power of the bow. But there is a monkey
here named Nala, honoured by craftsmen, the mighty son of Tvashtri, the god
Vishvakarman. Whatever wood, or grass, or rock he throws into me, I shall bear
it all up. That will be your bridge.'

"When he had said this, the Ocean vanished, and Rama said to Nala, 'Build a
bridge across the ocean. I think you can do it.' By this means Kakutstha had a
bridge built, ten yojanas wide and a hundred long. It is known as Nala's bridge,
famous on earth to this day, and it stands like a mountain, held up in honour of
Rama's command.

"While he was there, Vibhishana, whose self was dharma, the brother of the lord
of the rakshasas, came to him with four counsellors. Great-hearted Rama received
him with a welcome, but Sugriva suspected that he might be a spy. When Raghava
was truly satisfied by his actions, his conduct and his gestures, he honoured
him. He consecrated Vibhishana as king of all the rakshasas, and made him his
counsellor and companion, and a friend of Lakshmana.

"Guided by Vibhishana, he crossed the great ocean with his army by that bridge
in just a month, lord of men. Then he went on and came to the many gardens of
Lanka, and had the monkeys break up many great ones. There were two counsellors
of Ravana there, the rakshasas Shuka and Sarana, spying in the form of monkeys,
and Vibhishana seized them. When the two night-rovers had taken their own
rakshasa form again, Rama showed them the army and then let them go. When he had
camped his army in the gardens, the brave one sent Angada, a wise monkey, as
envoy to Ravana.

---

"In that forest, rich in food, water, roots and fruits, Kakutstha camped his
army and guarded it as was proper. Ravana, for his part, made the arrangements
in Lanka laid down in the treatises. By its nature it was hard to attack, with
strong walls and gateways. It had seven moats, very hard to cross, of deep water
full of fish and crocodiles, set with stakes of khadira wood. The walls were
made hard to assault with catapults on the battlements and with heaps of stones.
They had pots of venomous snakes for fighters, and resin and dust. They had
clubs, firebrands, iron arrows, lances, swords and axes, and shataghnis, and
clubs covered with wax. At all the city gates there were posts, fixed and
moving, with many foot soldiers and many elephants and horses.

"Then Angada came to the gate of Lanka, and when the lord of the rakshasas had
been told, he went in without fear. In the midst of many crores of rakshasas the
mighty one shone like the sun surrounded by banks of cloud. He went up to the
Paulastya, surrounded by his counsellors, and, speaking well, began to give
Rama's message.

"'Raghava, the glorious lord of Kosala, says this to you, king. It is a message
that suits the time. Take it and act on it. When they come under a king who has
not mastered himself and delights in misrule, countries and cities are infected
by misrule and perish. You alone have wronged me by carrying off Sita by force.
It will bring death to others who have done no wrong. The seers who live in the
forest, whom you harmed before in your pride of strength, the gods you scorned,
the royal seers you killed, and the women you carried off weeping: now the fruit
of that misrule has come to you. I shall kill you with your counsellors. Fight,
and be a man. See the power of my bow, night-rover, a man's bow. Let Janaki Sita
go. You will never escape me. I shall rid this world of rakshasas with my sharp
arrows.'

"When he heard these harsh words from the envoy, King Ravana, beside himself
with anger, could not bear it. Four night-rovers who could read their master's
signs seized him by his four limbs, as birds seize a tiger. Angada, with the
night-rovers clinging to his limbs, simply flew up into the sky with them and
landed on the roof of the palace. As he leapt up with such force, the
night-rovers fell, and hit the ground with their hearts burst, crushed by the
blow. Freed, he jumped down again from the top of the palace, leapt over the
city of Lanka and landed near his own army. Angada came to the lord of Kosala,
told him everything, and, praised by Raghava, the splendid monkey rested.

"Then the joy of the Raghus, with an attack by all the monkeys, swift as the
wind, broke the wall of Lanka. Lakshmana, putting Vibhishana and the lord of the
bears in front, crushed the southern city gate, hard as it was to approach. He
fell on Lanka with a hundred thousand crores of monkeys, their bodies tawny as
young camels, skilled in war. With the monkeys leaping up, dropping down and
falling, the sun could not be seen, its light blotted out by the dust. The
rakshasas, with their women and old people, were amazed, king, to see the wall
turned tawny all over by the monkeys, some like rice flowers, some bright as
shirisha blossoms, some like the young sun, some pale as reeds.

"They broke the jewelled pillars and the tops of the battlements, and hurled
down the engines with their force crushed and broken. Seizing shataghnis with
their wheels and stones, the mighty ones hurled them into the midst of Lanka
with the force of their arms. The hosts of night-rovers on the wall fled in
their hundreds, attacked by the monkeys.

"Then, at the king's command, rakshasas who could take any form they pleased
came out in hundreds of thousands, of hideous shapes. Raining showers of
weapons, driving off the forest-dwellers, they cleared the wall, showing the
greatest courage. The wall was emptied of monkeys again by the night-rovers,
terrible to look at, like heaps of black beans. Many bulls among the monkeys
fell, their limbs pierced by spears, and night-rovers fell there too, crushed by
pillars and arches. The rakshasas and monkeys fought hair to hair, heroes eating
each other with nails and teeth. Rakshasas and monkeys on both sides roared, and
even when they had fallen dead on the ground they would not let go of each
other.

"Rama poured nets of arrows like a raincloud, and when they reached Lanka they
killed the night-rovers. Saumitri too, firm-bowed and tireless, pointed out the
rakshasas in the fortress one by one and brought them down with iron arrows.
Then, at Raghava's command, the armies withdrew. The work of destruction had
been done in Lanka, the target had been hit, and victory was ahead.

---

"Then, as the soldiers were making camp, some of Ravana's followers, bands of
pishachas and lesser rakshasas, came at them: Parvana, Putana, Jambha, Khara,
Krodhavasha, Hari, Praruja, Aruja, Praghasa and others. As those wicked ones
came on, invisible, Vibhishana, who knew the art, destroyed their power to
vanish. Seen by the mighty monkeys, who could leap far, they were all killed,
king, and fell lifeless to the earth.

"Ravana could not bear it, and came out with his army. He drew up the array of
Ushanas and had all the monkeys struck down. Raghava came out against ten-faced
Ravana's drawn-up army, followed the rule of Brihaspati, and drew up his own
array against the night-rover. Ravana met Rama there and fought him, and
Lakshmana fought Indrajit. Sugriva fought Virupaksha, Nikharvata fought Tara,
Nala fought Tunda, and Patusha fought Panasa. Each met the one he thought he
could take on, and fought in the hour of battle, relying on the strength of his
own arms.

"The clash grew, adding to the fear of the timid, terrible and hair-raising,
like the battle of the gods and asuras long ago. Ravana tormented Rama with
showers of spears, lances and swords, and Raghava tormented Ravana with sharp,
keen iron arrows. In the same way Lakshmana pierced the watchful Indrajit with
arrows that split the vitals, and Indrajit pierced Saumitri with many arrows.
Vibhishana and Prahasta rained sharp arrows feathered with birds' feathers on
each other, without fear. There was a clash of great weapons between those
mighty ones, and the whole of the three worlds, moving and unmoving, trembled at
it."

**Divergences.**

* **The bridge.** In the CE the Ocean himself offers to bear up whatever Nala throws in, and Nala builds the bridge, ten yojanas wide and a hundred long (3.267.40–45). The popular details of stones marked with Rama's name, and of the squirrel who helps, are not in the Mahabharata. Nor is Rama's worship of Shiva at Rameshvaram; the name occurs only in a rejected Sabha passage (2.@17). See L-157.
* **Vibhishana.** He is consecrated king of the rakshasas as soon as Rama trusts him, before the crossing (3.267.49).
* Other rejected lines (\*1274–\*1279) add Kumuda among the monkey chiefs, a count of bears, and a comparison of Ravana to Ushanas. Not narrated.

**Choices.**

* 3.267.4 *golāṅgūlaḥ*: "a golangula", a kind of monkey with a cow's tail.
* 3.267.6: Panasa's numbers are the text's: "ten, twelve, thirty and five crores".
* 3.267.28: "how could one like me do harm to the merchants?", by seizing their boats.
* 3.267.34 *kausalyāmātaḥ*: "Son of Kausalya", literally "you whose mother is Kausalya".
* 3.267.35: "I am an Ikshvaku, your kinsman." Sagara, an Ikshvaku king (3.104.6), took the ocean as his son (3.106.33).
* 3.268.4–5: the defences are listed as the text lists them. *śataghnī*, "a hundred-killer", is kept as "shataghni".
* 3.268.28 *kapilīkṛtam*: "turned tawny all over by the monkeys".
* 3.268.34 *māṣarāśisadṛśaiḥ*: "like heaps of black beans".
* 3.268.36 *keśākeśi*: "hair to hair", in close combat.
* 3.269.5–6: the "array of Ushanas" and the "rule of Brihaspati" are the battle arrays of the teachers of the asuras and the gods.
* 3.269.8: the pairs are as the text gives them. Tara here is a monkey, not Valin's wife.
