# Shalva's Attack

"You would not have come to this trouble, lord of the earth," said Vasudeva, "if
I had been in Dvaraka, near at hand, king. I would have come to the game even
uninvited by the Kauravas, by the son of Ambika and by King Duryodhana,
unconquerable one. I would have stopped the game by showing its many evils. I
would have called Bhishma and Drona, Kripa and Bahlika, and said to King
Dhritarashtra, son of Vichitravirya, 'Enough of dice, Kaurava, for your sons'
sake, king of kings, and for your own, my lord.' I would have told of the evils
that brought you down, and those that long ago threw the son of Virasena out of
his kingdom. I would have described, lord of the people, how gaming destroys
wealth before it can even be enjoyed, and how the craving never stops, just as
it is.

"Women, dice, hunting and drink: these four vices, born of desire, are the ones
by which a king loses his fortune, king. Those who know the teachings think they
should be spoken against in every case, and those who know them see that dice
above all should be spoken against. In a single day a man's wealth is destroyed,
and disaster is sure. Wealth is lost before it is enjoyed, and there is nothing
but harsh words. This, and more that comes of it with bitter fruit, I would have
told the son of Ambika about dice, strong-armed Kauravya, when I met him. If he
had taken my advice when I said this, there would have been no harm to dharma or
to the Kurus, joy of the Kurus. If he had not taken my sweet and wholesome
words, king of kings, best of the Bharatas, I would have restrained him by
force. And if those who call themselves friends and are enemies had stood by
him, I would have struck down all those gamblers in the hall.

"But I was away from the Anartas then, Kauravya, and so this disaster, brought
on by the dice, came upon you. When I came to Dvaraka, best of the Kurus, joy of
the Pandavas, I heard from Yuyudhana exactly how you had come to grief. As soon
as I heard it, king of kings, I came to you quickly, deeply troubled, wanting to
see you, lord of the people. Alas, what hardship we have all come to, bull of
the Bharatas, that we should see you in misfortune with your brothers."

"Why were you away, Krishna, joy of the Vrishnis?" said Yudhishthira. "Where
were you, away from home, and what did you do while you were gone?"

"I had gone to Saubha, the city of Shalva, bull of the Bharatas, to destroy it,"
said Krishna. "Hear why, best of men. The strong-armed, glorious King
Shishupala, Damaghosha's son, a hero of great splendour, was killed by me at
your sacrifice, best of the Bharatas, at the Rajasuya, over the guest gift. The
wicked man, in the grip of anger, could not bear it. When Shalva heard that he
had been killed, he was filled with fierce anger, and he came to Dvaraka, which
was empty, while I was here, Bharata. He fought there, king, against the young
bulls of the Vrishnis. The cruel man had come riding in Saubha, which goes
wherever its master wishes. Then the evil-minded man killed many of those young
Vrishni heroes, and broke down all the gardens of the city.

"And he said, strong-armed one, 'Where is that lowest of the Vrishni family,
Vasudeva, the dull-witted son of Vasudeva? I will crush in battle the pride of
that man who is so eager for war. Tell me truly, Anartas, and I will go where he
is. When I have killed that killer of Kamsa and Keshin I will turn back. Until I
have killed him I will not turn back. I swear it on my weapon.' 'Where is he?
Where is he?' So the king of Saubha ran about, here and there, eager, they say,
to fight me in battle. 'Today, in my anger at the killing of Shishupala, I will
send that wicked, low traitor to the house of Yama. He struck down my brother,
King Shishupala, out of his own wicked nature. I will kill him on the earth. My
brother was a boy and a king, and he was not at the head of a battle. The hero
was killed off his guard. I will kill Janardana.'

"When he had raved like this, great king, and slandered me, joy of the Kurus, he
rose into the sky in Saubha, which goes where it wills. When I came back, I
heard how the wicked, evil-minded king of Marttikavata had behaved towards me,
Kauravya. Then I too, Kauravya, my eyes rolling with anger, made up my mind to
kill him, king. For I saw the havoc among the Anartas, and the insults to me,
and the growing arrogance of that doer of evil deeds. So I set out to destroy
Saubha, lord of the earth. Searching for him, I found him at the whirlpool in
the ocean. Then I blew the conch Panchajanya, king, challenged Shalva to battle,
and stood ready to fight. There was a fight there for a good while between me
and the danavas, and I brought them all under my power and struck them to the
ground. That is what I was doing, strong-armed one, and why I did not come then.
As soon as I heard of the game at Hastinapura, which came of bad conduct, I
came."

---

"Vasudeva, strong-armed and wise," said Yudhishthira, "tell me at length of the
destruction of Saubha. I can never hear enough of it."

"When he heard that I had killed King Shishupala, the son of Shrutashravas,
strong-armed one," said Vasudeva, "Shalva came to the city of Dvaravati, best of
the Bharatas. The wicked man besieged it on every side, son of Pandu. Shalva
drew up his forces and stood over the city, in the sky as well. From there the
king fought the city, and there was a fight with attacks from every side.

"The city was armed all round, with banners and arches, with wheels and hudas,
with engines and miners, with watch-towers and gateways, with towers and turrets
and gate-houses. It had hooks for seizing hair, and throwers of firebrands and
blazing torches. It had ushtrikas, best of the Bharatas, and drums great and
small, fuel, grass and kusha grass, king, and shataghnis and ploughshares. It
had bhushundis, balls of stone and clubs, weapons and battle-axes, iron shields,
fire, and huda-horns. It was fitted out in the way the teachings lay down, bull
of the Bharatas, with many things of every kind. It was manned by Gada, Samba,
Uddhava and the rest, men able to resist, tiger of the Kurus, heroes of famous
families, of courage proved in battle. It was guarded by the middle force and
the one called the Sara, with forward bands of horse and foot.

"It was proclaimed in the city, 'No one is to drink wine,' by Ugrasena, Uddhava
and the others, who were guarding against carelessness. 'For King Shalva would
strike at us if we were off our guard.' So thinking, the Vrishnis and Andhakas
all stood on guard. All the Anartas, the actors, dancers and singers, were sent
out by those who guarded the stores of wealth. All the bridges were broken down,
and the boats forbidden. The moats were thickly planted with stakes, joy of the
Kurus. Wells, best of the Kurus, and pits for roasting were dug all round for a
krosha, and the ground was made rough.

"The fortress is rough by nature, guarded by nature, armed by nature, sinless
one, and then above all. Well guarded, well hidden, supplied with every weapon,
the city was like Indra's palace, best of the Bharatas. No one went out without
a seal, and no one was let in without a seal, in the city of the Vrishnis and
Andhakas, king, when Saubha came. In every street and square, Kaurava, king of
kings, there was an army, full of elephants and horses. The army had been paid
its wages and its food, king, given its weapons and gear, and rewarded for its
deeds, strong-armed one. No one was paid in base metal, and no one's pay was
overdue. No one served as a favour, and there was no one whose courage had not
been seen. So Dvaraka was well ordered, king, by those who gave rich fees, and
well guarded by King Ahuka, lotus-eyed one.

---

"Shalva, lord of Saubha, came up to the city, king of kings, and camped there
with a great army of men and elephants. The army, of four branches, guarded by
King Shalva, camped on level ground where there was plenty of water. It avoided
the burning grounds, the temples of the gods, the anthills and the sacred trees,
and there the army camped. Six roads were made through the camp, dividing the
troops, and there were nine slopes in Shalva's camp, king. It had every kind of
weapon and men skilled in all of them. It was crowded with chariots, elephants
and horses, and thronged with foot soldiers and banners. Its men were well fed
and content, and bore the marks of heroes. Its banners and armour were bright,
and its chariots and bows. When he had camped at Dvaraka, Kauravya, bull among
men, he sent it against the city at speed, like the king of birds.

"When they saw the army of the lord of Shalva coming, the young princes, the
joys of the Vrishnis, went out and fought. They could not bear the attack of
King Shalva, Kaurava: Charudeshna, Samba, and Pradyumna, the great chariot
fighter. In their armour, in their chariots, with bright ornaments and banners,
they all closed with many of the foremost warriors of King Shalva.

"Samba took up his bow and gladly fought Shalva's counsellor Kshemavriddhi, the
commander of his army, in battle. The son of Jambavati let loose a great rain of
arrows on him, best of the Bharatas, as the thousand-eyed god lets loose the
rain. The commander Kshemavriddhi bore that tumultuous rain of arrows, great
king, as unmoving as the Himalaya. Then Kshemavriddhi, king of kings, let loose
on Samba a still greater net of arrows, made by magic. Samba tore the net of
magic apart with magic, and showered his chariot with a thousand arrows. Pierced
by Samba, the commander Kshemavriddhi, hurt by Samba's arrows, fled on his swift
horses.

"When Shalva's cruel commander had fled, a mighty daitya named Vegavat rushed at
my son. Samba, the pride of the Vrishni family, was attacked, king of kings, and
the hero stood his ground, holding off the rush of Vegavat. Samba, of true
courage, son of Kunti, whirled his swift mace and hurled it hard at Vegavat.
Struck by it, king, Vegavat fell to the ground, like an old tree with rotten
roots, broken by the wind. When that great asura had fallen, struck by the mace,
my son went into the great army and fought on.

"A danava named Vivindhya, a famous great chariot fighter with a great bow,
great king, closed with Charudeshna. Then there was a tumultuous fight between
Charudeshna and Vivindhya, king, like that of Vritra and Vasava long ago.
Furious with each other, they struck each other with arrows, roaring, great
king, like two mighty lions. Then the son of Rukmini charged an arrow, bright as
fire or the sun, that destroys enemies, with a great weapon-spell. My son, the
great chariot fighter, challenged Vivindhya in fury and loosed it at him, king,
and he fell dead.

"When he saw Vivindhya killed and his army thrown into confusion, Shalva came
again in Saubha, which goes where it wills. Then the whole army that lived in
Dvaraka was thrown into disorder, strong-armed one, when they saw Shalva in
Saubha come down to the earth. Then Pradyumna went out, son of Kunti, and
rallied that army. He said to the Anartas, great king, 'Stay, all of you, and
all of you watch me in battle, as I hold off Saubha by force, with its king.
Today, Yadavas, with iron arrows like snakes, loosed from the bow in my arms, I
will destroy the army of the lord of Saubha. Take heart. There is nothing to
fear. The king of Saubha dies today. Now that I have met him, the wicked man
will perish, Saubha and all.'

"As Pradyumna spoke so gladly, son of Pandu, that army stood firm, hero, and
fought as it pleased."

**Divergences.**

* **Why Krishna was absent.** In the CE Krishna says he was fighting Shalva of Saubha, who had
  attacked Dvaraka in revenge for Shishupala (3.15.2–22). Stories that Krishna saved Draupadi at
  the dicing are not in the CE; in the CE he was not there (3.14.1, 3.14.14). See ledger L-19.
* The vulgate adds that Shalva was long-lived by Brahma's favour (\*74). Not in the CE.
