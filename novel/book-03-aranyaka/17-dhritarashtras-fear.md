# Dhritarashtra's Fear

"This deed of Partha of boundless splendour is a great wonder," said Janamejaya.
"When Dhritarashtra, in his great splendour, heard of it, brahmin, what did he
say?"

When King Dhritarashtra, Ambika's son, heard from Dvaipayana, the best of seers,
that Partha had gone to Shakra's world, he said this to Sanjaya.

"Suta, I have heard in full what wise Partha has done. Do you know it too,
charioteer, just as it happened? My son, careless, lost in low pleasures,
dull-witted, bent on evil, the fool, will have the earth destroyed. The great
man whose words are always true, even in jest, and who has Dhananjaya to fight
for him, could win the three worlds. Who could stand before Arjuna as he looses
his barbed arrows and iron shafts, sharp-pointed and whetted on stone, even
Death, who is beyond old age? My wicked sons have all fallen into the power of
death, since they face war with the unassailable Pandavas. Think as I may, day
and night, I see no chariot fighter who could stand up to the bearer of Gandiva
in battle. If Drona and Karna went against him, or even Bhishma, there would be
great doubt in the world. But I do not see victory for us.

"Karna is soft-hearted and careless. The teacher is old, and our guru. Partha is
unforgiving and mighty, passionate and firm in his courage. There would be a
terrible fight, and no side beaten at all. For all of them know weapons, all are
brave, all have won great fame. Beaten, they would not want even lordship over
everything. Surely there would be peace only with their deaths, or with
Phalguna's. But there is no one who could kill Arjuna, and no one who could
conquer him. How will his fury be calmed, now that it has risen against these
fools?

"The hero, the equal of the lord of the thirty gods, satisfied Agni at Khandava.
He conquered all the kings at the great sacrifice of royal consecration. The
thunderbolt, falling on a mountain's head, might leave something behind,
Sanjaya. But the arrows Kiritin shoots, my son, will leave nothing. As the rays
of the sun burn all that moves and all that is still, so the arrows loosed by
Partha's arms will burn my sons. Already the army of the Bharatas seems to me to
be broken on every side, stricken with fear by the thunder of Savyasachin's
chariot. When Kiritin stands in battle with his bow drawn, scattering his arrows
and sowing them, he will be as hard to get past as the Ender, the taker of all,
created by the Ordainer."

"All that you have said about Duryodhana, king, is just as you say," said
Sanjaya. "None of it is false, lord of the earth. For the Pandavas of boundless
power are filled with anger. They saw Krishna, their lawful, glorious wife,
brought into the hall. They heard those words of Duhshasana's, and of Karna's,
great king, words that will bear a terrible fruit. I do not think they will
sleep.

"You have heard, great king, how Partha pleased Sthanu of the eleven bodies with
his bow in battle. The lord of all the gods himself, Kapardin, the blessed one,
took the dress of a Kirata and fought Phalguna, to test him. There the guardians
of the world showed themselves to Arjuna, the bull of the Kauravas, who had
striven through austerity for weapons. No man on earth but Phalguna could hope
to see these lords with his own eyes. What man could hope to wear down in battle
the hero whom Maheshvara, in bodily form, could not wear down?

"This terrible, hair-raising uproar has been brought on by those who dragged
Draupadi and enraged the Pandavas. There Bhima, his lips trembling, spoke a
great word when he saw Duryodhana show Draupadi both his thighs. 'I will break
your thighs, you wicked man, with a mace like the thunderbolt, at the end of the
thirteen years, you cheat at dice!' All of them are the best of fighters, all of
boundless splendour, all masters of every weapon, hard to conquer even for the
gods. I think the Parthas, roused by anger, full of courage and fury, will make
an end of your sons in battle."

"What did Karna do, suta, by speaking harsh words?" said Dhritarashtra. "It was
enmity enough that Krishna was brought into the hall. Will my foolish sons stand
now, when their eldest brother, their guide, does not keep to good conduct? The
wretch will not listen to my words either, suta, seeing me without eyes,
helpless and senseless. And his foolish counsellors, Karna, Saubala and the
rest, witless, only add to his many faults. The arrows of Partha, of boundless
splendour, even loosed in play, would burn up my sons. How much more when anger
drives them? Loosed by the strength of Partha's arms, flying from his great bow,
charged with the mantras of divine weapons, they could bring down even the gods.
He has Janardana for his counsellor, his guard and his friend, Hari, the lord of
the three worlds. What has he not conquered? And this is a very great wonder of
Arjuna's, Sanjaya, as we hear: that he met the great god arm to arm. The whole
world saw what Phalguna and Damodara did long ago at Khandava, to help Fire.
When Partha and Bhima and Vasudeva the Satvata are angry, I have no sons left at
all, nor ministers, nor kin."

---

"All this lamenting of King Dhritarashtra's, sage, after he had banished the
heroic Pandavas, is for nothing," said Janamejaya. "How could the king disregard
his own witless son Duryodhana, when he was angering the sons of Pandu, those
great chariot fighters? But tell me, what did the sons of Pandu eat in the
forest? Was it forest food, or grown in the fields?"

"Forest food, and deer brought down with clean arrows," said Vaishampayana. "The
bulls among men offered the best to the brahmins, and then they ate.

"While those brave, great archers lived in the forest, king, brahmins followed
them, some keeping the sacred fires and some not. Yudhishthira supported ten
thousand brahmins, great-hearted snatakas, and as many again who knew the way to
freedom. He killed ruru deer and black antelopes and other clean forest beasts
with his arrows, as is proper, and gave them to the brahmins. No one there
looked sickly or ill, or thin or weak, or wretched or afraid. The Dharma King
Yudhishthira, the best of the Kauravas, cared for his kinsmen as if they were
his own dear sons, and for his brothers as if they had been born of his own
mother. And glorious Draupadi, like a mother, fed her husbands and the
twice-born first, and then ate what was left.

"The king went east, Bhimasena south, and the twins west or north, bows in hand,
and every day they killed deer for meat. So they lived in Kamyaka, missing
Arjuna, longing for him, and five years went by while they studied, prayed and
made offerings."

---

Dhritarashtra, Ambika's son, heaved a long, hot sigh, bull of the Bharatas, and
called Sanjaya the suta.

"Nakula and Sahadeva, the two sons of Pandu, sons of gods, blessed, bright as
the king of the gods, are wild in battle. Their weapons are firm, they strike
from far off, they are set on fighting, quick of hand, fixed in their anger,
always ready and full of force. When those two stand at the head of the battle
with Bhima and Arjuna before them, brave as lions, as hard to bear as the
Ashvins, I see nothing of our army left, Sanjaya. For those two great chariot
fighters, sons of gods, have no match in battle. Impatient as they are, they
will not forgive Draupadi's torment. The Vrishnis, great archers, or the mighty
Panchalas, guarded in battle by Vasudeva, whose promises are true, will burn my
sons' army in battle, with the Parthas. Not even mountains could withstand the
charge of the Vrishnis when Rama and Krishna lead them, son of a suta. In their
midst the great archer Bhima, of terrible courage, will range with his iron
mace, the killer of heroes. The kings will not be able to bear the roar of
Gandiva, like the crash of a thunderbolt, nor the force of Bhima's mace. Then,
as Duryodhana's follower, I shall remember the words of my friends, which I
should have remembered, and did not act on before."

"This is a very great wrong that you have let pass, king," said Sanjaya. "You
could have stopped your son, and out of folly you did not. When Madhusudana
heard that the Pandavas had been beaten at the dice, Achyuta came quickly to the
Parthas in Kamyaka to comfort them. So did the sons of Drupada, led by
Dhrishtadyumna, and Virata, and Dhrishtaketu, and the Kekayas, those great
chariot fighters. What they said there, when they saw the Parthas beaten, I
learned through a spy, and I have told it to you. Madhusudana met the Pandavas
there, surrounded by them, and Hari told them, 'So be it,' when they asked him
to be Phalguna's charioteer in battle. For Krishna himself was angry when he saw
the Parthas come to this, wearing black antelope skins as their upper garments.
This is what he said to Yudhishthira.

"'The wealth the Parthas had at Indraprastha, which I saw at the royal
consecration, which other kings could hardly win! There I saw all the kings,
stricken with fear of your weapons' might, the Vangas and Angas, Paundras and
Odras, Cholas, Dravidas and Andhakas. I saw those who live on the shore of the
sea and in the ports, the Simhalas, the Barbaras, the mlecchas, and those who
live in the dry lands. I saw western kingdoms by the hundred, down to the sea,
all the Pahlavas and Daradas, Kiratas, Yavanas and Shakas. There were Harahunas
and Chinas, Tukharas and Saindhavas, Jagudas, Ramathas, Mundas, the kingdom of
women and the Tanganas. These and many others, bull of the Bharatas, I saw come
to your sacrifice to serve at the meal. That wealth of yours, fickle, gone over
to others: I will take the lives of those who took it, and bring it back.

"'With Rama, Kauravya, with Bhima, Arjuna and the twins, with Akrura, Gada and
Samba, with Pradyumna and Ahuka, with the hero Dhrishtadyumna and the son of
Shishupala, I will kill Duryodhana in battle at once, Bharata. I will kill
Karna, Duhshasana, Saubala and any other who fights against us. Then you, living
in Hastinapura with your brothers, will take the wealth of Dhritarashtra's sons
and rule this earth.'

"Then the king said to him, in that gathering of heroes, as all of them
listened, Dhrishtadyumna and the rest: 'I accept this word of yours as true,
Janardana. You will kill my enemies, strong-armed one, with all their followers.
Make me true after the thirteenth year, Keshava. For I promised this life in the
forest in the midst of kings.'

"The men in that gathering, led by Dhrishtadyumna, agreed to the Dharma King's
words, and at once calmed the angry Keshava with sweet words that suited the
time. And they said to the Panchali, who had done no wrong, as Vasudeva
listened, 'Duryodhana will lose his life through your anger, queen. We promise
you this truly. Do not grieve, lovely one. Krishna, those who laughed then when
they saw you angry, beasts and birds will laugh at as they eat their flesh.
Vultures and jackals will drink their blood and drag at the heads of those who
dragged you across the floor of the hall. Panchali, you will see their bodies on
the ground, dragged at and eaten, again and again, by the eaters of flesh. The
earth will drink the blood of those who tormented you there, and of those who
looked on, when their heads are cut off.'

"Those bulls among men said many such words then, all of them full of splendour,
brave, all bearing the marks of unbroken fortune. After the thirteenth year
those great chariot fighters, with the Dharma King, will come with Vasudeva at
their head. Rama and Krishna and Dhananjaya, Pradyumna and Samba, Yuyudhana and
Bhima, the sons of Madri, the sons of the Kekaya king, the sons of the Panchala,
with the Dharma King. These are unconquerable heroes of the world, great men
with their followers and their armies. Who that wants to live would go out to
meet them all in battle, angry as maned lions?"

"At the time of the dicing Vidura said to me," said Dhritarashtra, "'If you beat
the Pandavas, king of men, surely this is the time of the Kurus' end, a great
terror, a flood of blood.' I think it will be just so, suta, as the steward told
me long ago. Beyond doubt this war will come when the time is up, as was
foretold."

---

"When the great Partha had gone to Shakra's world for weapons," said Janamejaya,
"what did the Pandavas do, with Yudhishthira at their head?"

When the great Partha had gone to Shakra's world for weapons, the bulls among
men lived in Kamyaka with Krishna. Then one day, in a lonely spot, on a patch of
grass, the best of the Bharatas sat down with Krishna, stricken with sorrow,
grieving for Dhananjaya, their throats thick with tears. Grief overwhelmed them
all, parted from him. They were in pain at being parted from Dhananjaya, and at
the loss of their kingdom.

Then strong-armed Bhima spoke to Yudhishthira.

"Great king, at your command that bull among men has gone, Arjuna, in whom the
lives of the sons of Pandu rest. If he were lost, the Panchalas with their sons,
and we, and Satyaki and Vasudeva would all be lost, beyond doubt. What could be
more painful than this, that the splendid Bibhatsu has gone at your command,
thinking nothing of his many hardships? Relying on his arms, we great-hearted
men all think our enemies beaten in battle and the earth won. It was only
because of the power of that archer that I did not send all the sons of
Dhritarashtra, with Saubala, to the other world in the middle of the hall. We
have strength in our arms, and we bear the anger that has risen in us because of
you, guarded as we are by Vasudeva. For with Krishna we could kill our enemies,
with Karna at their head, and rule the whole earth won by our own arms. Through
your fault at the dice we have all been overwhelmed, king, though we lack
nothing in manhood and are the strongest of the strong.

"You should look to a kshatriya's dharma, great king. The forest is no dharma
for a kshatriya, great king. The wise know that kingship is the highest dharma
of a kshatriya. You know a kshatriya's dharma, king. Do not stray from the path
of dharma. Let us kill the sons of Dhritarashtra before the twelve years are
out, king. I will bring Partha back from the forest and send for Janardana. Then
in a great battle, with the armies drawn up, I will send the sons of
Dhritarashtra to the other world at a stroke, lord of the people. I will kill
all the sons of Dhritarashtra, with Saubala, and Duryodhana and Karna, and
anyone else who fights. When I have made peace, you can come back from the
forest afterwards. If it is done that way, no fault will be yours, lord of the
people. And with sacrifices of every kind, my lord, tamer of enemies, we will
shake off whatever sin has been done, great king, and go to the highest heaven.

"It could be so, king, if our king were not a child, and slow, set on dharma. It
is settled that the deceitful must be killed by deceit. To kill a cheat by
deceit is not called a sin. So too, Bharata, those who know dharma see in the
rules of dharma that a day and a night are equal to a year, great king. So too a
saying of the Veda is always heard, lord: a year is fulfilled by a hard vow,
great king. If the Vedas are your authority, unshakable one, then let the time
of thirteen years be taken as passed after a single day. It is time to kill
Duryodhana and all his followers, tamer of enemies, before he makes the whole
earth one with him, king."

As Bhima spoke like this, King Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, kissed the Pandava
on the head and comforted him.

"Beyond doubt, strong-armed one, you will kill Suyodhana after the thirteenth
year, with the bearer of Gandiva. As for your saying to me, Partha, lord, that
the time has come, I cannot bring myself to speak a lie. That is not in me. Even
without deceit, son of Kunti, you will kill Suyodhana, the unassailable, bent on
evil, with all his followers."

As Yudhishthira, the Dharma King, was saying this to Bhima, the great and
blessed seer Brihadashva came. When the righteous Dharma King saw him come,
walking in dharma, he honoured him with the honey offering, as the texts lay
down. When the seer was rested and seated, strong-armed Yudhishthira sat by him,
looked at him, and said much, in a pitiful way.

"Blessed one, my wealth and kingdom were taken from me by a game of dice. I was
challenged by cheats, skilled at dice, masters of deceit. I did not know the
dice, and they were bent on evil and used deceit. My wife, who is dearer to me
than life, was brought into the hall. Is there any king on earth more unlucky
than I am? Have you seen one before, or heard of one? I think there is no man
more wretched than I am."

"You say, great king," said Brihadashva, "that nowhere is there a man more
unlucky than you, Pandava. I will tell you of a king who was more wretched than
you, lord of the earth, if you wish to hear it, sinless one."

Then the king said to him, "Tell it, blessed one. I want to hear of a king who
came to such a state."

"Listen closely, king, with your brothers, unshakable one," said Brihadashva,
"to the story of a king who was more wretched than you, lord of the earth. Among
the Nishadhas there was a lord of the earth called Virasena. He had a son named
Nala, who saw clearly into dharma and wealth. We have heard that this king was
beaten by Pushkara through deceit, and though he did not deserve sorrow, he
lived in the forest with his wife. He had no horse, no chariot, no brother and
no kin left to him as he lived in the forest, king. But you are surrounded by
heroic brothers, like gods, and by the best of the twice-born, like Brahma
himself. So you should not grieve."

"I want to hear in full, best of speakers, the story of the great Nala," said
Yudhishthira. "Please tell it to me."

**The CE's own doublets.**

* **Krishna's visit.** Sanjaya's account of Krishna's visit to Kamyaka (3.48.12–35) retells
  what the narrator told in 3.13 (chapter 5), with differences. Here the king's answer and the
  others' vow to Draupadi come after a list of the peoples at the royal consecration, and the
  visitors ask Krishna to be Arjuna's charioteer (3.48.15). Both are kept as the text gives them.
* **Bhima's loophole.** Bhima proposes, as at 3.36.31–33, to count the time as already served.
  Here it is "a day and a night equal to a year" (3.49.21–23).
* **Duryodhana's thigh.** Bhima's vow to break Duryodhana's thighs, first made at 2.63.14, is
  recalled by Sanjaya (3.46.28–29).

**Divergences.**

* **Bhima's fear of the year in hiding.** A passage in which Bhima says Duryodhana's spies will
  find them in the thirteenth year (@7, after 3.49.24) is an appendix passage and not narrated.
