# Ashtavakra

"See the holy hermitage, lord of men," said Lomasha, "full of trees always in
fruit, of him who is spoken of on earth as the son of Uddalaka, Shvetaketu,
master of the mantras, of the highest mind. Here Shvetaketu saw Sarasvati
herself in human form, and spoke to Sarasvati as she came, saying, 'I will know
speech.' At that time, king, there were two of the best of those who know
brahman, an uncle and his sister's son: Ashtavakra, son of Kahoda, and
Shvetaketu, son of Uddalaka. Those two brahmins, uncle and nephew, went into the
place of sacrifice of the lord of the earth, the king of Videha, and beat the
boundless Bandin in debate."

"What was the power of that brahmin who beat Bandin like that?" said
Yudhishthira. "And how did he come to be Ashtavakra, bent in eight places? Tell
me all this truly, Lomasha."

"Uddalaka had a single devoted pupil, king, named Kahoda," said Lomasha. "He
served his teacher, obeyed him, and studied with him for a long time. The
brahmins, his fellow pupils, looked down on him. When the teacher knew of that
slight, he gave him at once his learning and his own well-born daughter Sujata
for a wife. She conceived a child as bright as fire, and it spoke to its father
as he recited. 'You spend the whole night reciting, father, and it is not quite
right.' Rebuked like that among his pupils, the great seer cursed the child in
the womb in his anger. 'Because you speak from the womb, you will be born bent
in eight places.'

"He was born bent like that, the great seer famous as Ashtavakra, 'bent in eight
places'. Shvetaketu was his uncle, and they were the same age. When Sujata was
troubled by the son growing in her womb, she went to her husband in private,
wanting money, and asked the poor man's favour. 'How shall I manage without
money, great seer? This is my tenth month, and you have no money at all to help
me through this trouble when I give birth.' When his wife said this, Kahoda went
to Janaka for money. There Bandin, a master of debate, beat the brahmin and had
him drowned in the water.

"When Uddalaka heard that he had been beaten in debate by the suta and drowned
in the water, he said to Sujata, 'This must be hidden from Ashtavakra.' She kept
that counsel well. Even when he was born, the brahmin did not hear of it. He
thought of Uddalaka as his father, and of Shvetaketu as his brother. Then, in
his twelfth year, Shvetaketu dragged Ashtavakra by the hand, crying, from his
grandfather's lap, where he sat, and said, 'This is not your father's lap.' That
cruel word stayed in his heart and gave him great pain. He went home weeping and
asked his mother, 'Where is my father?' Then Sujata, in great distress and
afraid of a curse, told him everything. When he had learned the whole truth from
his mother, the brahmin said to Shvetaketu, 'Let us go to King Janaka's
sacrifice. We hear his sacrifice is full of wonders. We will hear the brahmins
debate there, and eat the finest food, and our judgement will grow.'

"The uncle and nephew went to King Janaka's rich sacrifice. On the road
Ashtavakra met the king, and when he was told to make way, he said this.

---

"'The road belongs to the blind man, the road belongs to the deaf, to the woman,
to the man carrying a load. The road belongs to the king if he does not meet a
brahmin. If he meets one, the road belongs to the brahmin.'

"'I give you the road today,' said the king. 'Go by whatever way you like. No
fire is small, and even Indra always bows to brahmins.'

"'We have come to see the sacrifice, sir,' said Ashtavakra to the gatekeeper.
'We are very eager. We two guests have come and want to go in. We ask your
leave, keeper of the gate. We are here to see the sacrifice of Indradyumna's
son, and we want to speak and to see the lord of Janaka's line. Do not strike
us, keeper of the gate, with anger, the worst of sicknesses. Let us in at once.'

"'We carry out Bandin's orders,' said the gatekeeper. 'Listen to what I tell
you. Young brahmins do not go in here. Old, learned, excellent brahmins go in.'

"'If entry here is given to the old,' said Ashtavakra, 'then it is right for me
to go in, gatekeeper. We are old, we have kept our vows, and we have the power
of the Veda to earn us entry. We are obedient and masters of our senses, and we
have reached the end of knowledge and learning. They say one should not despise
someone for being a boy. Even a small fire burns when it is touched.'

"'Recite Sarasvati, the one syllable loved by the Veda, of many forms, shining,'
said the gatekeeper. 'Look at yourself, a boy. Why do you boast? Success in
debate is hard to win.'

"'Growth is not known by the growth of the body,' said Ashtavakra, 'as the knot
of the silk-cotton tree grows great. A small, short thing that bears fruit is
grown. What bears no fruit has no growth.'

"'Boys take wisdom from the old,' said the gatekeeper, 'and in time they become
old. Knowledge cannot be won in a short time. Why do you, a boy, talk like an
old man?'

"'A man is not old because his hair is grey,' said Ashtavakra. 'The gods know
that even a boy who understands is old. The seers made it the law: not by years,
not by grey hairs, not by wealth, not by kinsmen, but the one who has learned
the Veda is great among us. I have come wanting to see Bandin in the king's
assembly. Announce me to the king who wears the lotus garland, gatekeeper. Today
you will see me speak, gatekeeper, when the debate with the wise has grown hot.
You will see who is high and who is low, when all the others have fallen silent
today.'

"'How can you, a boy of ten, enter the sacrifice, where only the learned and
trained may enter?' said the gatekeeper. 'I will try to find a way to let you
in. You do your best.'

"'King, best of Janaka's line,' said Ashtavakra, 'you deserve honour, and
everything is rich in you. You are the performer of the rites of sacrifice, as
King Yayati alone was before. We have heard that the learned Bandin beats those
who know the Veda in debate, and then without a qualm has them all drowned in
the water by men you have sent, who carry out his orders. When I heard this from
the brahmins, I came to speak of brahman. Where is Bandin? When I meet him, I
will put him out as the sun puts out the stars.'

"'You hope to beat Bandin without knowing your opponent's power of speech,' said
the king. 'Only those whose strength is known can speak like this. He has been
seen by brahmins who are devoted to debate.'

"'He has not debated with the likes of me,' said Ashtavakra. 'That is why he has
become a lion and speaks without fear. Meeting me today he will be struck down
and lie like a broken cart on the road, with its axle gone.'

"'The man who knows the meaning of the thing with six naves, twelve axles,
twenty-four joints and three hundred and sixty spokes is the highest poet,' said
the king.

"'May that ever-moving wheel of twenty-four joints, six naves, twelve rims and
three hundred and sixty spokes protect you,' said Ashtavakra.

"'What does the one who falls on the gods like a hawk bear, joined like two
mares?' said the king. 'What is their child, and what do they bring forth?'

"'May that never be in your house, king,' said Ashtavakra, 'not even in your
enemies'. The one whose charioteer is the wind bears a child in them, and they
bring it forth.'

"'What does not close its eyes when it sleeps?' said the king. 'What does not
move when it is born? What has no heart? What grows with speed?'

"'A fish does not close its eyes when it sleeps,' said Ashtavakra. 'An egg does
not move when it is born. A stone has no heart. A river grows with speed.'

"'I do not think you are human,' said the king. 'You have the nature of a god.
You are not a boy. I think you are old. No one is your equal at speech. So I
open the door. There is Bandin.'

---

"'Here among these matchless kings, gathered and ruled by Ugrasena, king,' said
Ashtavakra, 'there is no room among the debaters for one who wants to know, as
among geese crying over a great lake. You will not speak against me today, you
who pride yourself on out-talking others. You have met your match, like a river
meeting the sea. Stand firm against me today, Bandin, like a fire blazing with
fuel.'

"'Do not wake a sleeping tiger,' said Bandin, 'or a poisonous snake licking the
corners of its mouth. If you strike its head as if you had kicked it, you will
not get away unbitten. Know that. A weak man who, out of pride in his build,
strikes a mountain, his own hand is torn, nails and all, and no wound is seen on
the mountain. All the kings are brought low before the king of Mithila, as the
mountains are before Mainaka, as calves are before an ox.'

"Ashtavakra, roaring in the assembly, grew angry and said to Bandin, king,
'Answer me what I say, and I will answer what you say.'

"'Only one fire is kindled in many forms,' said Bandin. 'One sun lights up all
this. One hero, the king of the gods, kills enemies. And Yama alone is lord of
the Fathers.'

"'Two friends go together, Indra and Agni,' said Ashtavakra. 'Two divine seers,
Narada and Parvata. Two are the Ashvins, and two the wheels of a chariot. Wife
and husband are two, as the Ordainer made them.'

"'Three times this creature is born by its deeds,' said Bandin. 'Three together
carry the vajapeya. The adhvaryus spread out the three pressings. There are
three worlds, and they speak of three lights.'

"'Fourfold is the home of the brahmins,' said Ashtavakra. 'Four together carry
this sacrifice. There are four quarters and four classes, and the cow is always
called four-footed.'

"'There are five fires, and the pankti metre has five feet,' said Bandin. 'There
are five sacrifices, and five senses. In the Veda five with five locks are seen,
and in the world the holy Panchanada is famous.'

"'Some say the fee at the setting up of the fire is six,' said Ashtavakra.
'These six seasons are the wheel of time. There are six senses and six
Krittikas, and six sadyaska rites are seen in all the Vedas.'

"'There are seven tame beasts and seven wild ones,' said Bandin. 'Seven metres
carry one rite. There are seven seers, and seven forms of honour, and the vina
is famed for its seven strings.'

"'Eight shanas make a shatamana,' said Ashtavakra. 'The sharabha, killer of
lions, has eight feet. We have heard of eight Vasus among the gods, and in every
sacrifice the post is made with eight corners.'

"'Nine kindling verses are spoken for the Fathers,' said Bandin. 'They speak of
creation as ninefold. The brihati metre is said to have nine syllables, and the
count always comes to nine.'

"'Ten are the stages of a man in the world,' said Ashtavakra. 'They say ten
hundreds make a thousand. Women with child carry for ten months. There are the
Dasherakas, the Dashas and the Dasharnas.'

"'Eleven are the eleven kinds of beasts for sacrifice,' said Bandin. 'Here there
are eleven posts. The changes of living beings are eleven, and eleven are the
Rudras among the gods in heaven.'

"'They call the year twelve months,' said Ashtavakra. 'A foot of the jagati has
twelve syllables. The twelve-day rite is called the common sacrifice, and the
brahmins here speak of twelve Adityas.'

"'The thirteenth day of the moon is said to be the most fierce,' said Bandin,
'and the earth has thirteen islands.'

"When he had said this much, Bandin stopped. Ashtavakra spoke the other half of
the verse. 'Keshin ran for thirteen days, and they speak of the atichhandas
metres, which begin with thirteen.'

"Then a great uproar rose, when they saw the suta's son silent, his head down,
lost in thought, and Ashtavakra still speaking. In that tumult at King Janaka's
rich sacrifice all the brahmins came up to Ashtavakra with joined hands, glad,
and honoured him.

"'This man had brahmins who had heard the Veda beaten in debate and drowned in
the water, they say,' said Ashtavakra. 'Let this Bandin meet the same fate
today. Take him and drown him in the water.'

"'I am the son of King Varuna,' said Bandin. 'A sacrificial session of twelve
years was being held there, at the same time as your sacrifice, Janaka. It was
for that that I sent the best of brahmins there. All of them went to see
Varuna's sacrifice, and they are coming back. I honour Ashtavakra, who deserves
honour, because of whom I shall meet my father again.'

"'The brahmins were drowned in the water of the sea,' said Ashtavakra, 'beaten
by your speech and cleverness. By cleverness I have now lifted up that speech,
as good men sift speech. Fire, the knower of beings, when it burns the houses of
the good, does not burn them, sparing them by its power. So good men sift speech
when children and sons speak pitifully. Are you listening, Janaka, dull as the
fruit of the shleshmataka? Or do praises go to your head? Like an elephant being
goaded, you do not listen to what I say.'

"'I hear your speech, divine in form, more than human,' said Janaka. 'You
yourself are divine in form. Since you have beaten Bandin in debate, Bandin is
handed over to you today, as you wish.'

"'I have no use for this Bandin alive, king,' said Ashtavakra. 'If Varuna is his
father, drown him in the lake.'

"'I am the son of King Varuna,' said Bandin. 'I have no fear of being drowned in
the water. In a moment Ashtavakra will see his father Kahoda, long lost.'

"Then all those brahmins, honoured by great Varuna, rose up near Janaka.

"'This is why men want sons by their deeds, Janaka,' said Kahoda. 'What I could
not do, my son has done. A strong son is born to the weak, a wise son to the
foolish, a learned son to the unlearned, Janaka.'

"'May Death himself carry off the heads of your enemies in battle with his sharp
axe, king,' said Bandin. 'Bless you. The great ukthya is sung here, and the
finest sama, and the soma is drunk properly in this session. The gods themselves
have gladly taken their pure shares in Janaka's sacrifice here.'

"When all the brahmins had risen, shining more brightly than before, Bandin,
with King Janaka's leave, went into the water of the sea. Ashtavakra honoured
his father, and was honoured by those brahmins as was right, and when he had
beaten Bandin he went back to that fine hermitage with his uncle.

"Here, son of Kunti, you will stay happily with your brothers and the brahmins,
content. Then, devoted to pure deeds, you will go with me to other holy places,
Ajamidha."

**Divergences.**

* **The Samanga.** In the familiar version Ashtavakra's father tells him to bathe in the river Samanga, and his
  body is made straight. That is a rejected passage (\*662, after 3.134.38). In the CE he goes home with his uncle
  after the debate, and nothing is said of his body being straightened. See L-131.
