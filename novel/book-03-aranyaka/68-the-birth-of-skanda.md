# The Birth of Skanda

"I have told you the many lines of the fires, sinless one," said Markandeya.
"Now hear, Kauravya, of the birth of wise Karttikeya. I will tell you of the
wonderful son of Adbhuta, of boundless power, born of the wives of the seven
seers, devoted to brahmins, the increaser of fame.

"Long ago the gods and asuras fought, killing each other, and the danavas of
terrible form always defeated the gods. Purandara saw his army struck down again
and again by them, and he thought hard about a leader for his army. 'I must find
a man of great strength who, trusting in his courage, could protect the army of
the gods when I see it broken by the danavas.'

"He went to the mountain Manasa, thinking hard about this, and then he heard a
terrible cry of pain from a woman. 'Let some man run to me and save me! Let him
point out a husband for me, or be my husband himself!' Purandara said to her,
'Do not be afraid. There is no danger for you.' When he had said this, he saw
Keshin standing in front of him, wearing a diadem, mace in hand, like a mountain
veined with ores, holding the girl by the hand. Vasava said to him, 'You of
ignoble deeds, why do you want to carry off this girl? Know that I am the
wielder of the thunderbolt. Stop harming her.'

"'Let her go yourself, Shakra,' said Keshin. 'I have asked for her. It would be
best for you to go home to your city alive, chastiser of Paka.'

"When he had said this, Keshin hurled his mace to kill Indra. As it came on,
Vasava cut it in the middle with his thunderbolt. Then Keshin, in anger, hurled
a mountain peak at him. Seeing the mountain peak coming, Shatakratu split it
with his thunderbolt, king, and it fell to the ground. As it fell, the peak
struck Keshin. Badly hurt, he let go of the blessed girl and ran away.

"When the asura had gone, Vasava said to the girl, 'Who are you? Whose are you?
And what are you doing here, fair-faced one?'

"'I am Prajapati's daughter, known as Devasena,' said the girl. 'My sister,
Daityasena, was carried off by Keshin before. We two sisters used to come here
to Manasa with our friends for pleasure, with Prajapati's leave. The great asura
Keshin always wanted to carry us off. Daityasena wanted him, but I did not,
chastiser of Paka. She has been carried off by him, blessed one, and I have been
freed by your strength. I want a husband, lord of the gods, one who cannot be
conquered, whom you point out.'

"'You are my mother's sister's daughter,' said Indra, 'and my mother is a
daughter of Daksha. I want you to tell me of your own strength.'

"'I am weak, strong-armed one,' said the girl, 'but my husband will be strong.
By my father's boon, he will be honoured by gods and asuras.'

"'What kind of strength will your husband have, goddess?' said Indra. 'I want to
hear you tell it, blameless one.'

"'He has been seen to be the conqueror of the wicked,' said the girl, 'of gods,
danavas and yakshas, kinnaras, snakes and rakshasas, of great courage and great
strength. The one who will conquer all creatures with you will be my husband,
devoted to brahmins, the increaser of fame.'

"When Indra heard her words he was troubled and thought hard. 'There is no
husband for this goddess such as she speaks of.' Then he, bright as the sun, saw
the sun at its rising, and the greatly blessed Soma entering the sun. It was the
new moon, and the terrible hour had begun, and on the mountain of sunrise he saw
the battle of the gods and asuras. Shatakratu saw the eastern twilight joined
with red clouds, and the blessed one saw the home of Varuna with red water. He
saw the fire taking the offering poured with many mantras by the Bhrigus and
Angirases and entering the sun. He saw the twenty-fourth joint of the moon
standing at the sun, and the terrible Soma, gone to dharma, gone into the sun.
Seeing the moon and the sun become one, and seeing that terrible meeting, Shakra
thought, 'This terrible conjunction is great and full of splendour. This meeting
of Soma with the fire and the sun is wonderful. The son Soma begets would be
this goddess's husband. And Agni has all these qualities, and Agni is a god. If
he begot a child, it would be this goddess's husband.'

"Having thought this, the blessed one went to the world of Brahma, taking
Devasena with him, saluted the Grandfather and said, 'Point out a good, brave
husband for this goddess.'

"'As you have thought this thing out, slayer of danavas,' said Brahma, 'so it
will be. That child will be strong and of wide courage. He will be your general,
with you, Shatakratu, and he will be this goddess's husband, full of courage.'

---

"When he heard this, the lord of the gods bowed to him and went with the girl to
where the divine seers were, the chief brahmins of great vows, led by Vasishtha.
Thirsting for their share of the Soma that the seers had won by austerity at
their sacrifice, the gods, led by Shatakratu, went there. When they had
performed the rite as was right, the great ones offered the oblations of all the
dwellers in heaven into the well-kindled fire. Called by them, the wonderful
carrier of offerings came out of the disc of the sun, silent, as is proper, the
lord, and came to the offering fire where the brahmins had made the offering
with mantras. There the eater of offerings took the many offerings and gave them
to the dwellers in heaven for the seers, best of the Bharatas.

"As he came out, he saw the wives of those great ones, sitting in their own
hermitages and bathing at their ease. They were like golden altars, spotless as
the crescent moon, like the flames of the fire, all wonderful as stars. With his
mind gone to them, his senses were stirred. Seeing the wives of the lords of
brahmins, the fire fell into the power of desire. Then he thought again, 'It is
not right that I am stirred. I desire the good wives of the lords of brahmins,
who do not desire me. I cannot see them or touch them without cause. So I will
enter the householder's fire and look at them again and again.' Entering the
householder's fire, as if touching them all with his flames, bright as gold, he
rejoiced as he watched them. Staying there a long time, in their power, his mind
set on them, desiring those fine women, his heart burning with desire, and set
on leaving his body because he could not have the brahmins' wives, Agni went to
the forest.

"Svaha, Daksha's daughter, had long loved him. The passionate one had long
looked for a weakness in him, but the god was always watchful and the blameless
one could find none. She knew that the fire had gone to the forest, truly
burning with desire. The passionate one thought, 'I will take the forms of the
wives of the seven seers and love the fire, tormented by desire and bewildered
by their forms. If I do this, he will have his pleasure, and I will have my
desire.'

"Shiva, the wife of Angiras, was full of good conduct, beauty and virtue. The
goddess first took her form, lord of the people, and went to the fire, and the
fine woman said to him, 'Agni, you should love me, for I am burning with desire.
If you will not, know that I am dead. I am the wife of Angiras, named Shiva,
eater of offerings. I have come, sent by my friends, when we had taken counsel
and decided.'

"'How do you know that I am tormented by desire?' said Agni. 'And how do the
others, all those beloved wives of the seven seers you speak of?'

"'You have always been dear to us,' said Shiva, 'but we are afraid of you.
Knowing your mind by its signs, they have sent me to you. I have come here to
lie with you. Take your pleasure quickly. The mothers are waiting for me. I will
go, eater of offerings.'

"Then Agni, full of love and joy, took Shiva, and the goddess, joined with him
in love, took the seed in her hand. She thought, 'Those who see this form of
mine in the forest will tell a false tale of fault against the brahmin women
with the fire. So, to guard against it, I will become a Garuda bird. Then I will
leave the forest easily.' She became a Suparna bird and went out of the great
forest. She saw the White Mountain, thick with clumps of reeds, guarded by
wonderful seven-headed serpents whose look is poison, by rakshasas, pishachas
and fierce hosts of spirits, and full of rakshasa women and many beasts and
birds. She suddenly went to the mountain top, very hard to reach, and quickly,
though she was a good woman, threw the seed into a golden pit.

"Taking the forms of the wives of the other great seven seers, the goddess loved
the fire. But she could not take the divine form of Arundhati, because of the
power of her austerity and her service to her husband. Six times, best of the
Kurus, on the first day of the fortnight, the passionate Svaha threw Agni's seed
into that pit. There that fallen seed, gathered by its splendour, begot a son.
Honoured by the seers, it made the fallen one, skanna, into Skanda. The boy had
six heads, twice as many ears, twelve eyes and twelve arms, one neck and one
body. On the second day he appeared, on the third he was a child, and on the
fourth he was Guha, with all his limbs.

"Wrapped in a great red cloud with lightning, he shone in that great red cloud
like the risen sun. He took up the great bow, which makes the hair stand on end,
laid down there by the destroyer of Tripura, the killer of the enemies of the
gods. Taking that best of bows, the mighty one roared, as if bewildering the
three worlds with all that moves and does not move. Hearing his roar, like the
sound of masses of great clouds, the two great elephants Chitra and Airavata
rushed up. Seeing them coming, the one bright as the young sun, the heir of
Agni, took them in two hands, and his spear in another hand. With another arm he
took a great cock, the best of the strong, with a copper-red crest, clinging to
him. Holding it, the mighty one roared terribly and played. With two arms the
strong one took the finest conch and blew it, terrifying even the strong among
creatures. With two arms he struck the sky again and again. Playing, the great
general shone, as if drinking the three worlds with his mouths, beyond measure,
on the mountain top, like the rayed sun at its rising.

"Seated on the top of that mountain, of wonderful courage, beyond measure, he
looked at the quarters with his many faces, and seeing many things, he roared
again. Hearing his roar, people fell down in many ways. Frightened and troubled,
they went to him for refuge. The people of many colours who took refuge with
that god the brahmins call his companions, very mighty. The strong-armed one got
up, comforted those people, drew his bow and shot arrows at the great White
Mountain. With his arrows he split the mountain Krauncha, son of the Himalaya,
and through it the geese and vultures go to Mount Meru. The mountain, split,
fell, crying out in great pain, and when it had fallen, the other mountains
cried out in great fear. The best of the strong, beyond measure, heard the cry
of those in great pain and was not troubled. He raised his spear and roared.
That great spear, hurled by the great one, split the terrible peak of the White
Mountain with force. Struck by it, the White Mountain, in distress, left the
earth with the other mountains and flew up in fear of the great one. Then the
earth, deeply shaken, split on every side. In pain she came to Skanda, and she
was strong again. The mountains bowed to him and went back to the earth. And
then the world began to worship Skanda on the fifth day of the bright fortnight.

---

"The seers, seeing terrible portents of many kinds, were alarmed and performed
rites of peace for the worlds, they who give life to the worlds. The people who
lived in that forest, Chaitraratha, said, 'This great disaster has been brought
on us by the fire, who has lain with six wives of the seven seers.' Others said
to the Garuda bird, 'You have brought this disaster on us.' They had seen the
goddess going in her form. But people did not know that this was Svaha's doing.
When the Suparna bird heard it, she thought, 'He is my son,' and went slowly to
Skanda and said, 'I am your mother.'

"Then the seven seers heard of the son of great might who had been born, and
they put away six of their wives, all but the goddess Arundhati. The forest
dwellers said he was born of the six. Svaha said again and again to the seven
seers, 'This is my son. I know it is not as you think,' king. The great sage
Vishvamitra, when he had performed the seers' sacrifice, had followed the fire,
burning with desire, unseen behind him, and he knew all of it truly. Vishvamitra
was the first to go to Kumara for refuge, and he made a divine hymn to the great
general. The great sage performed for him all the thirteen auspicious rites of
childhood, beginning with the birth rite. Vishvamitra did this work for the good
of the world: the greatness of the six-faced one, the winning of the cock, the
winning of the goddess by the spear, and of the companions too. So the seer
Vishvamitra became dear to Kumara. The great sage knew that Svaha had changed
her form, and he said to all the sages, 'The women have done no wrong.' But,
having heard the truth from him, they put away their wives all the same.

"When the gods heard of Skanda, they said together to Vasava, 'Skanda's strength
is unbearable. Kill him, Shakra, quickly, without delay. If you do not kill him
today, he will become Indra, mighty one, taking the three worlds, and us, and
you, Shakra, under his power.' He said to them, troubled, 'This child is very
strong. He could show his courage in battle and destroy even the creator of the
worlds. Let all the Mothers of the world go to Skanda today. They have the
courage they wish. Let them kill him.' 'So be it,' they said, and they went.

"When they saw that he had no match in strength, they lost heart. Thinking, 'He
cannot be overcome,' they went to him for refuge. They said, 'You are our son.
The world is upheld by us. Greet us all with joy, for our milk flows for you and
we are weak with love.' The great general honoured them and granted their
wishes. Then the strongest of the strong saw his father Agni coming. Honoured by
him, Agni stood firm with the host of the Mothers, surrounding the great general
and guarding him. Of all the Mothers, the woman born of anger, the nurse,
guarded Skanda with a spear in her hand, like a son. The cruel daughter of the
red ocean, who feeds on blood, embraced the great general and guarded him like a
son. Agni became Naigameya, goat-faced, with many children, and amused the child
on the mountain as if with toys.

---

"The Grahas with the Upagrahas, the seers and the Mothers, and the blazing hosts
of companions led by the eater of offerings, these and many other terrible
dwellers in heaven stood round the great general with the hosts of the Mothers.
Seeing the victory in doubt, the lord of the gods, eager to win, mounted the
shoulders of Airavata and set out with the gods. Wanting to kill the great
general, Indra went quickly. That army of the gods was fierce, of great speed
and great brilliance, with bright banners and armour, mounts and bows of many
kinds, dressed in the finest clothes, full of glory and adorned.

"Kumara went to meet Shakra as he came to kill him. Mighty Shakra went quickly
on the road, roaring, cheering on the army of the gods, wanting to kill the son
of Pavaka. Honoured by the thirty gods and by the great seers, Vasava came near
Karttikeya. Then the lord of the gods roared a lion's roar with the gods, and
Guha, hearing that sound, roared like the ocean. At his great sound the army of
the gods, like a stirred-up ocean, reeled here and there, senseless.

"Seeing the gods come to kill him, the son of Pavaka in anger loosed great
flames of fire from his mouth. They burned the armies of the gods as they
writhed on the ground. With their heads and bodies ablaze, their weapons and
mounts ablaze, they fell suddenly and shone like bright hosts of stars. Burning,
the gods left the wielder of the thunderbolt and went for refuge to the son of
Pavaka, and then they found peace.

"Abandoned by the gods, Shakra loosed his thunderbolt at Skanda. Loosed, it
struck Skanda quickly in the right side, and it split the side of the great one,
great king. From the blow of the thunderbolt another being was born from Skanda,
a young man in golden armour, holding a spear, with divine earrings. Because he
was born of the thunderbolt's entering, he became Vishakha. Seeing this second
one born, bright as the fire at the end of time, Indra in fear joined his hands
and went to Skanda for refuge. Skanda gave him and his army freedom from fear,
good man. Then the thirty gods, overjoyed, sounded their instruments.

**Divergences.**

* **Whose son.** In the familiar story Skanda is the son of Shiva and Parvati, nursed by the six Krittikas. In this CE telling he is born of Agni's seed, laid by Svaha in the forms
  of six seers' wives in a golden pit on the White Mountain (3.214.1–16); Rudra's part, and the Krittikas', are told later (3.218.27–29). See L-145.
* **The portents.** Rejected passages (\*1057, \*1063) describe the omens at his birth at length. Not narrated.
* **Indra's reluctance.** A rejected passage (\*1065) has Indra say he cannot kill a child and the gods mock him. Not narrated.
