summary: At Raibhya's hermitage Lomasha tells of Yavakri, son of Bharadvaja, who tried to win the Vedas by austerity alone until Indra, piling sand into the Ganga, shamed him and granted the boon; how, proud of it, he insulted Raibhya's daughter-in-law and was killed by a demon Raibhya made; how Bharadvaja cursed Raibhya and burned himself; how Raibhya's son Paravasu killed his father by mistake and blamed his brother Arvavasu; and how the gods, pleased with Arvavasu, brought them all back to life.
---

# Yavakri

"Here shines the Samanga, the Madhuvila, king," said Lomasha. "This is
Kardamila, where Bharata was consecrated. When the husband of Shachi had killed
Vritra and was joined with ill fortune, they say, he bathed in the Samanga and
was freed from every sin. This is Vinashana, bull among men, in the side of
Mainaka, where Aditi long ago cooked that food for the sake of sons. Climb this
king of mountains, bull among men, and you will drive away ill fortune, which is
inglorious and not to be spoken of. These are the Kanakhala hills, king, dear to
the seers, and here shines the great river Ganga, Yudhishthira. Here the blessed
Sanatkumara reached the highest perfection. Bathe in her, Ajamidha, and you will
be freed from every sin. Touch in silence, son of Kunti, with your ministers,
the lake of waters called Punya, the mountain Bhrigutunga and the Ganga. Here
shines the lovely hermitage of Sthulashiras. Here, son of Kunti, put away pride
and anger. Here shines the splendid hermitage of Raibhya, Pandava, where
Yavakri, the poet, son of Bharadvaja, perished."

"What was the mighty seer Bharadvaja like?" said Yudhishthira. "And why did
Yavakri, the seer's son, perish? I want to hear all of it, Lomasha, as it
happened. I take great delight in the deeds of men like gods as they are told."

"Bharadvaja and Raibhya were friends," said Lomasha. "They lived here always in
the forest, delighting in each other. Raibhya had two sons, Arvavasu and
Paravasu, and Bharadvaja had a son, Yavakri, Bharata. Raibhya was learned, with
his sons, and the other was an ascetic. The two of them had a love beyond
compare from childhood, Bharata.

"When Yavakri saw his father, an ascetic, not honoured, and Raibhya honoured by
the brahmins with his sons, sinless one, the splendid one burned with anger and
was overcome by it, Pandava. He took up a terrible austerity to win knowledge of
the Veda. Heating his body in a great blazing fire, the great ascetic made Indra
burn. Then Indra came to Yavakri, Yudhishthira, and said, 'Why have you taken up
this highest austerity?'

"'I practise this supreme austerity, you whom the hosts of the gods honour,'
said Yavakri, 'so that the Vedas, which the twice-born have not studied, may
come to me. My effort is for the sake of learning, chastiser of Paka. By
austerity I want to know all knowledge, Kaushika. The Vedas can only be won from
a teacher's mouth in a long time, lord, and so I have taken up this supreme
effort.'

"'This is not the road by which you want to go, brahmin seer,' said Indra. 'Why
destroy yourself, brahmin? Go and learn from a teacher's mouth.'

"When he had said this, Shakra went away, Bharata, and Yavakri, of boundless
courage, strove at his austerity all the more. Practising terrible austerity,
king, the great ascetic made the lord of the gods burn fiercely, so we have
heard. When he saw the great sage practising such fierce austerity, the god who
split Bala came again and tried to stop him. 'You have set out on a thing that
cannot be done. This is not wise of you. The Vedas will come to you and to your
father.'

"'If this is not done as I wish, king of the gods,' said Yavakri, 'I will
practise a still more terrible austerity with great discipline. Know this,
Maghavan: I will cut off my limbs one by one and offer them into the blazing
fire, if you do not grant me all I wish here, king of the gods.'

"When the wise one saw the great sage's resolve, he thought hard how to turn him
back. Then Indra took the form of a brahmin ascetic many hundreds of years old,
weak and sick with consumption. At the ford where Yavakri used to go to purify
himself, he made a dam of sand in the Bhagirathi. When the best of the
twice-born would not do as he said, Shakra began to fill the Ganga with sand. He
threw handfuls of sand into the Bhagirathi without rest and began a dam, to show
Yavakri. Yavakri saw him working hard at the damming, and the bull among sages
laughed and said, 'What is this, brahmin? What are you trying to do? This great
effort is quite useless.'

"'I will dam the Ganga,' said Indra. 'It will be an easy road. People suffer, my
son, crossing again and again.'

"'You can never dam this great flood,' said Yavakri. 'Turn back from what cannot
be done and set about what can.'

"'Just as you have set about this austerity for the Vedas,' said Indra, 'which
cannot be done, so we have taken up this burden.'

"'If you think my undertaking useless, as yours is, lord of the thirty gods,
chastiser of Paka,' said Yavakri, 'then do what I can be given, lord of the
hosts of the gods. Give me other boons, by which I shall surpass others.'

"Indra gave him the boons the great ascetic had asked. 'The Vedas will come to
you, with your father, as you wish. And whatever else you want, Yavakri, you may
go.' His wish fulfilled, he went to his father and spoke to him.

"'The Vedas will come to me and to my father, both of us. We will surpass
others. I have won those boons.'

"'Pride will come to you, my son, now that you have won the boons you wanted,'
said Bharadvaja. 'Full of pride and mean, you will soon perish. They tell these
verses about it, which the gods spoke. There was once a mighty seer named
Baladhi, my son. Tormented by grief for a son, he practised very hard austerity.
"Let my son be immortal," and he won that. The gods granted him favour, but not
to be equal to the immortals. "No mortal is immortal. His life will depend on a
cause." "As these mountains stand for ever, best of the gods, unwasting, let
them be the cause on which my son's life depends," said Baladhi.

"'Then his son was born, Medhavin, always angry. When he heard this, he grew
proud and despised the seers. Wandering over this earth, harassing the sages, he
came to the mighty, wise Dhanushaksha. Medhavin wronged him, and the mighty one
cursed him: "Become ashes!" But he did not become ashes. When Dhanushaksha saw
Medhavin unharmed, the mighty one had the mountains, the cause of his life,
split by buffaloes. When the cause was destroyed, the boy suddenly died. His
father took up his dead son and lamented. Seeing him lamenting, the sages
recited to him, as if in pain, a verse spoken in the Veda long ago. Hear it from
me: "No mortal can ever pass beyond what is ordained. Dhanushaksha had the
mountains split by buffaloes."

"'So boys who win boons, bold and full of pride, soon perish. Do not be like
that. Here is Raibhya, very mighty, and his two sons are like him. Take care, my
son, never to go near him. For when he is angry he can hurt you, son, in his
wrath. He is learned, an ascetic, and a great seer quick to anger.'

"'I will do so,' said Yavakri. 'Do not grieve at all, father. I honour you, and
I honour Raibhya as a father too.'

"When he had spoken gently to his father, Yavakri, fearing nothing, went about
wronging other seers, full of joy.

---

"Wandering about fearlessly, Yavakri went in the month of Madhava to Raibhya's
hermitage. In the holy hermitage, adorned with flowering trees, he saw Raibhya's
daughter-in-law walking, like a kinnari, Bharata. Shameless, robbed of his
senses by desire, Yavakri said to that modest woman, 'Come to me.' She knew his
character, and she was afraid of a curse, and of Raibhya's power. 'Yes,' she
said, and went. Then she led him aside and held him off, Bharata. Then Raibhya
came to his hermitage, tamer of enemies. Seeing his daughter-in-law, Paravasu's
wife, weeping and in distress, he comforted her with gentle words and asked her,
Yudhishthira. The good woman told him all that Yavakri had said, and how she had
answered Yavakri with forethought.

"As Raibhya listened to what Yavakri had done, a great anger rose in him, as if
burning his heart. Then, in the grip of fury, the ascetic, quick to anger, tore
out one of his matted locks and offered it into the well-prepared fire. From it
arose a woman equal to his daughter-in-law in beauty. He tore out another matted
lock and offered it into the fire. From it arose a rakshasa with terrible eyes,
fearful to see. The two said to Raibhya, 'What shall we do?' The seer said to
them in anger, 'Kill Yavakri.' 'So be it,' they said, and went, meaning to kill
Yavakri.

"The creature of magic made by the great one came to him, beguiled him and took
away his water pot, Bharata. Then the rakshasa, spear raised, ran at Yavakri,
unclean, his water pot taken. When Yavakri saw him coming, spear in hand, to
kill him, he suddenly rose and ran to a lake. Seeing the lake without water,
Yavakri ran quickly to all the rivers, and they too were dried up. Driven by the
terrible rakshasa with the spear in his hand, he ran in terror, suddenly, to his
father's fire house. As he was entering, the blind shudra who guarded it held
him back by force at the door, and he stood still, king. Then the rakshasa
struck Yavakri with his spear as the shudra held him, and he fell with his heart
split. When he had killed Yavakri, the rakshasa went back to Raibhya, and with
Raibhya's leave he went off with that woman.

---

"Bharadvaja, son of Kunti, having done his daily recitation, took up his bundle
of fuel and came into his hermitage. Before, all the fires used to rise up when
they saw him, but now, his son killed, the fires did not come to greet him. The
great ascetic noticed the change in the fire offering and said to the blind
shudra sitting there as keeper of the house, 'Why do my fires not welcome the
sight of me, shudra? And you are not as you used to be. Is all well in the
hermitage? I hope my foolish son has not gone to Raibhya. Tell me quickly, for
my mind is uneasy.'

"'Surely your foolish son went to Raibhya,' said the shudra, 'for he lies here
killed by a strong rakshasa. Driven by that rakshasa with a spear in his hand,
he came to the door of the fire house, and I held him back with my arms. So he
was killed here, wanting water, unclean no doubt, struck down swiftly by the
rakshasa with the spear in his hand.'

"When Bharadvaja heard the shudra's painful words, he took up his lifeless son
and lamented in great sorrow. 'It was for the sake of brahmins, surely, that you
practised austerity: "Let the Vedas that the twice-born have not studied come to
me." You were so good to great brahmins, innocent towards all creatures, and yet
you fell into harshness. I forbade you, my son, to go near Raibhya's dwelling.
Yet you went to that low man, who is like Time, Death and Yama. He knew I was
old and that you were my only son, and yet the evil-minded, splendid man gave
way to anger. Grieving for my son through Raibhya's deed, I will give up without
you, my son, the life I love most on earth. As I give up my body in grief for my
son, guilty, so Raibhya's eldest son will soon kill Raibhya, though he is
innocent.

"'Happy are the men who by birth have no son. They go as they please, without
knowing grief for a son. But those who, their minds confused by grief for a son,
curse their dear friends in their pain, who could be more wicked than they? I
have seen my son dead, and I have cursed my dear friend. Who else will suffer a
calamity like this?' When he had lamented like this in many ways, Bharadvaja
burned his son, and then he entered the blazing fire himself.

---

"At that very time the great and mighty King Brihaddyumna, for whom Raibhya
performed sacrifices, was holding a session of sacrifice. Wise Brihaddyumna
chose Raibhya's two sons, Arvavasu and Paravasu, as helpers for the session.
With their father's leave they went there, son of Kunti, and Raibhya stayed in
the hermitage with Paravasu's wife.

"Then Paravasu came home alone to look in, and saw his father in the forest,
wrapped in a black antelope skin. In the last part of the night, blind with
sleep, while darkness still lingered, he thought his father, moving in the thick
forest, was a deer. Thinking him a deer, he killed his father, not meaning to,
wanting to protect himself. When he had performed all the rites for the dead for
him, Bharata, he came back to the session and said to his brother, 'You cannot
carry this work alone at all. I killed our father, thinking he was a deer. Keep
the vow for the killing of a brahmin for my sake, sage. I can do this work
alone.'

"'You carry on with wise Brihaddyumna's session,' said Arvavasu. 'I will keep
the vow for the killing of a brahmin for you, with my senses restrained.'

"When he had come to the end of the atonement for killing a brahmin,
Yudhishthira, the sage Arvavasu came back to the session. When Paravasu saw his
brother there, he said to Brihaddyumna in the assembly, 'This man is a killer of
a brahmin. Do not let him come in to see your sacrifice. A brahmin killer could
harm you even with a look, no doubt of it.' As the servants drove him away,
king, Arvavasu said again and again, 'I did not do this killing of a brahmin.'
Though the servants called him 'Brahmin killer!' again and again, Bharata, he
would not admit that he had done the killing himself. 'My brother did this, and
I took the atonement on myself for him.'

"The gods were pleased with Arvavasu for what he did, king. They had him chosen
and had Paravasu cast out. Then the gods with Agni at their head gave him a
boon. He chose that his father should rise, that his brother should be free of
guilt, that his father should not remember being killed, and that Bharadvaja and
Yavakri should both rise too. Then all of them appeared, Yudhishthira. Then
Yavakri said to the gods with Agni at their head, 'I studied the Veda and kept
the vows. How could Raibhya kill me, a student and an ascetic, in such a way,
best of the immortals?'

"'Do not speak so, Yavakri,' said the gods, 'as you are speaking, sage. You
learned the Vedas easily, without a teacher. But he, by his own work, pleased
his teachers through hardship, and over a long time and with toil he won the
highest brahman.'

"When the gods with Agni at their head had said this to Yavakri and brought them
all back to life, they went back to heaven. This is his holy hermitage, with
trees always in flower and fruit. Stay here, tiger among kings, and you will be
freed from every sin."

**Divergences.**

* **The woman's likeness.** A rejected line (\*666) says the woman Raibhya made took his daughter-in-law's shape
  to deceive Yavakri. In the CE she is made "equal to her in beauty" (3.137.10) and beguiles him (3.137.13).
* **Yavakri's later life.** A rejected line (\*674) says that after coming back to life Yavakri kept the vow of a
  student for many years. Not in the CE.

**Choices.**

* 3.135.3: Aditi's cooking of "that food for the sake of sons" is named and not told.
* 3.135.9 *kavir yavakrītaḥ*: "Yavakri, the poet"; the text uses both Yavakri and Yavakrita.
* 3.135.30 *sayakṣmaṇaḥ*: "sick with consumption".
* 3.136.3–13: Bharadvaja's tale of Baladhi and Medhavin is kept as a tale within the tale.
