# The Hunter

When all those great ascetics had gone, the blessed Hara, who holds Pinaka in
his hand and takes away all sin, took on the dress of a Kirata, a mountain
hunter. He shone like a golden tree, with a body as bright as a second Mount
Meru. He took up a splendid bow and arrows like poisonous snakes, and came down
in a great blaze, like fire burning a thicket. With him came the goddess Uma,
keeping the same vow and wearing the same dress, and a crowd of joyful spirits
in dress of every kind. Hidden in the guise of a Kirata, followed by women in
their thousands, the god shone brightly then, king, Bharata.

In an instant the whole forest fell silent. The sound of the springs and of the
birds died away.

As he came near Partha, whose deeds are never strained, he saw a son of Diti
called Muka, wonderful to look at. The demon had taken the form of a boar and
was scheming how to kill Arjuna, the wicked creature. Phalguna took up the bow
Gandiva and arrows like poisonous snakes. He strung the fine bow and made it
ring with the twang of its string, and said to the boar, "You want to kill me,
when I have come here and done you no wrong. So I will send you first to the
house of Yama."

When Shankara, in the form of a Kirata, saw strong-bowed Phalguna about to
shoot, he suddenly stopped him. "I marked him first, this beast as dark as a
blue cloud."

Phalguna paid no heed to his words and shot. At the same moment the brilliant
Kirata loosed an arrow like a thunderbolt, like a tongue of fire, at the same
target. The two arrows fell together on Muka's broad body, as hard as rock. The
two shafts struck with a sound like lightning crashing, like the thunderbolt
striking a mountain. Pierced by many arrows, like snakes with blazing mouths,
the demon died, taking again his terrible rakshasa shape.

Then Jishnu, the killer of enemies, saw a man as bright as gold, hidden in the
guise of a Kirata, with women about him. The son of Kunti, glad at heart, spoke
to him as if laughing.

"Who are you, wandering in the empty forest with a crowd of women around you?
Are you not afraid in this terrible forest, you who shine like gold? Why did you
shoot this beast, which was mine? This rakshasa came here and I took him first.
Whether you did it from desire or in contempt of me, you will not escape me with
your life. What you have done to me today is not the law of the hunt. For that,
mountain dweller, I will take away your life."

When the son of Pandu said this, the Kirata laughed, and spoke gently to
Savyasachin.

"This beast was my target first, and mine to take. It was my blow that took his
life. You should not lay your own faults on another, swollen with pride in your
strength. I have been wronged, fool. You will not escape me with your life.
Stand firm. I will shoot arrows like thunderbolts. Strive with all your power,
and you shoot your arrows too."

---

Then the two of them grew furious there. Roaring again and again, they cut at
each other with arrows like poisonous snakes. Arjuna loosed a rain of arrows on
the Kirata, and Shankara took it with a calm mind. The bearer of Pinaka took
that rain of arrows for a while and stood with his body unhurt, unmoved like a
mountain.

When Dhananjaya saw his rain of arrows come to nothing, he was greatly amazed,
and he said, "Well done! Well done! Ah, this man of tender limbs, who lives on
the peak of the Himalaya, takes the iron shafts that Gandiva looses and does not
flinch! Who can he be? Rudra himself, a god, a yaksha, the lord of the gods? For
the thirty gods do gather on this greatest of mountains. No one but the god who
bears Pinaka could bear the force of the nets of arrows I have loosed in
thousands. God or yaksha, if he is anyone but Rudra, I will send him to the
house of Yama with my sharp arrows."

Then Jishnu, glad at heart, loosed iron shafts that pierce the vitals, in
hundreds, king, like the sun sending out its rays. The blessed lord who brings
the worlds into being, the trident in his hand, took them with a calm mind, as a
mountain takes a rain of stones. In an instant Phalguna's arrows were spent. He
grew afraid when he saw his arrows gone.

Jishnu thought of the blessed Fire, who had given him the two quivers that never
run out, long ago at Khandava. "What can I shoot from my bow, now that my arrows
are spent? And this man, whoever he is, swallows all my arrows. I will strike
him with the tip of my bow, as an elephant is struck with the point of a spear,
and send him to the house of Yama, who holds the rod."

The son of Kunti, killer of enemy heroes, fought on with the tip of his bow. But
the mountain dweller swallowed his divine bow too. Arjuna stood with his bow
swallowed and his sword in his hand. Wanting to end the fight, he rushed at the
hunter. The joy of the Kurus stepped forward and brought the sharp sword down on
his head with all the strength of his arm, a sword that did not stick even on
mountains. But when it reached his head, that fine sword shattered.

Then Phalguna fought him with trees and rocks. The blessed one, in the form of a
Kirata, with his huge body, took the trees, and then the rocks. Then mighty
Partha struck the unassailable one, who had the form of a Kirata, with fists
that fell like thunderbolts, making smoke come from his mouth. The blessed one,
in the form of a Kirata, struck back at Phalguna with fists as hard as Shakra's
thunderbolt, cruel blows. A terrible crackling rose from the fists of the
Pandava and of the Kirata as they fought. That great, hair-raising fight went on
for a good while, blow for blow of their arms, like the fight of Vritra and
Vasava.

Then mighty Jishnu struck the Kirata with his chest. The Kirata, with all his
strength, struck back at the struggling Pandava. From the grinding of their arms
and the rubbing of their chests, a fire with embers and smoke broke out on their
bodies. Then the great god gripped him and crushed him with his limbs, and in
his anger overpowered him with his splendour, and his mind went dark. With his
limbs crushed, Phalguna looked like a ball, held fast in the body of the god of
gods, Bharata. Held so by the great one, he could not breathe. He fell
senseless. Then Bhava was pleased.

"Well done, Phalguna, well done," said the blessed one. "I am satisfied with
your matchless deed. There is no kshatriya equal to you in courage and in
steadiness. Your splendour and your strength are equal to mine today, sinless
one. I am pleased with you, strong-armed one. Look at me, bull among men. I give
you sight, large-eyed one. You were a seer of old. You will conquer all your
enemies in battle, even the dwellers of heaven."

Then Phalguna saw the god there, the great god Girisha, trident in hand, in all
his splendour, with the goddess. Partha, the conqueror of enemy cities, went
down on his knees to the ground, bowed his head, and asked Hara's favour.

"Kapardin," said Arjuna, "lord of all beings, you who put out Bhaga's eyes,
blessed one, forgive me my offence, Shankara. I came to this great mountain
longing to see you, blessed one, to this home of ascetics that you love, lord of
the gods. Blessed one, whom all beings honour, I ask your favour. Great god, let
this not be counted against me, this rash, rash deed. I fought with you without
knowing who you were. I have come to you for refuge. Forgive me, Shankara."

The god of great splendour, whose banner bears a bull, laughed. He took
Phalguna's fine arm and said, "It is forgiven."

---

"In a former body you were Nara," said the blessed one, "with Narayana for your
companion. At Badari you practised fierce austerity for many tens of thousands
of years. The highest splendour is in you, or in Vishnu, the highest of beings.
You two, the first of men, hold up the world by your splendour. At Shakra's
consecration you took up a great bow that thundered like a cloud, lord, and you
and Krishna punished the danavas. This is that same Gandiva, Partha, fit for
your hand. I took on illusion and swallowed it, best of men. And here again are
your two quivers that never run out, Partha, as is right. I am pleased with you,
Partha, whose courage is true. Take from me the boon you long for, bull among
men. There is no man like you among mortals, giver of honour, nor in heaven. The
kshatriya order has you at its head, scourge of enemies."

"Blessed one," said Arjuna, "if you will give me what I wish, out of your
favour, you whose banner is a bull, then, lord, I wish for that terrible divine
weapon, the Pashupata. It is Rudra's own, of terrible power, called
Brahmashiras, which at the fearful end of an age destroys the whole world. With
it I could burn up danavas and rakshasas in battle, spirits and pishachas,
gandharvas and snakes. When it is charged with mantras, thousands of tridents
spring from it, and maces fearful to see, and arrows like poisonous snakes. With
it I could fight Bhishma, Drona and Kripa in battle, and the suta's son, who
always speaks bitter words. This is my first wish, blessed one, you who put out
Bhaga's eyes. By your favour, let me be able to do it."

"I give you the great Pashupata, my own beloved weapon," said the blessed one.
"You are able to hold it, to loose it and to call it back, Pandava. Not even
great Indra knows it, nor Yama, nor the king of the yakshas, nor Varuna, nor
Vayu. How then will men know it? But it must never be loosed rashly at any man,
Partha. If it strikes one of little power, it will burn up the whole world.
There is no one in the three worlds, moving or still, whom it cannot kill. It
can be loosed by the mind, by the eye, by speech and by the bow."

When he heard this, Partha made himself pure at once and gathered his mind. He
clasped the feet of the lord of all and said, "Teach me."

Then Shiva taught him the weapon, with its secrets and the way to call it back.
That weapon, like Death in bodily form, came to wait on the great Pandava as it
waited on the three-eyed husband of Uma, and Arjuna gladly received it. Then the
earth shook, with its mountains, forests and trees, with its seas and woodlands,
its villages, towns and mines. The sound of conches and drums and of kettledrums
in their thousands rose, and at that moment there was a great crash of thunder.
Then gods and danavas saw the terrible weapon, blazing, standing in bodily form
at the side of the Pandava of boundless power. And wherever the three-eyed god
had touched Phalguna, every ill in his body was destroyed.

The three-eyed god told Arjuna, "Go to heaven." Partha bowed his head, joined
his hands and gazed at the god.

Then Bhava, the master, the lord of those who live in heaven, wise, the mountain
lord, the husband of Uma, Shiva, gave the best of men Gandiva, the great bow
that destroys the sons of Diti and the pishachas. And the lord left that fine
mountain, with its white slopes, its peaks and caves, where birds and great
seers lived, and with Uma he went up into the sky as the best of men watched.

**Divergences.**

* **The clay altar and the garland.** In the familiar version Arjuna, beaten, makes a clay
  altar or image of Shiva and worships it with a garland, then sees the garland on the
  hunter's head and knows him. It is not in the CE. It is a rejected passage (\*171, after
  3.40.51), translated by Ganguli (G 3.39). In the CE Arjuna falls senseless and Shiva, pleased,
  speaks to him (3.40.51–52). See L-120.
* **Muka.** The boar is the demon Muka, a son of Diti (3.40.7). A rejected passage says Shiva
  sent him (\*164). Not in the CE.
* **Who shot first.** The CE has both arrows strike together (3.40.13–14).
