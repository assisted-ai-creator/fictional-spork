# Vidura's Counsel

When the Pandavas had gone into the forest, the son of Ambika, the king whose
eyes were his wisdom, was tormented. As he sat at his ease, he spoke to the
righteous Vidura, whose understanding had no bottom.

"Your wisdom is as clear as that of Bhargava, and you know the subtle, highest
dharma. You are fair, and the Kauravas respect you. Tell me what is good for
them and for me. Now that things have come to this, Vidura, what should be done
today? How can we win over these townspeople? I do not want them to tear us up
by the roots, nor do I want to see them destroyed."

"Dharma is the root of the three aims of life, lord of men," said Vidura, "and
they say this kingdom has its root in dharma. Keep to dharma, king, as far as
you can, and protect all your sons, and the sons of Kunti. That dharma was
destroyed in the hall by wicked men, with the son of Subala at their head. Your
son challenged the son of Kunti, who keeps his word, in the gaming hall, and
beat him. I see a way, king, to repair what remains of this evil policy of
yours, so that your son may be freed from his sins and stand well in the world,
Kauravya. Let the sons of Pandu have back all that you gave away, king. This is
the highest dharma, that a king should be content with what is his own and not
covet what belongs to others. This is what you must do above all: satisfy them,
and disgrace Shakuni. If anything is to be left for your sons, king, do this,
and do it quickly.

"If you do not do this, king, the destruction of the Kurus is certain. For
neither Bhimasena nor Arjuna, once angry, will leave anything of their enemies
on the battlefield. They have Savyasachin, master of weapons, to fight for them,
and Gandiva, the finest bow in the world. They have strong-armed Bhima to fight
for them. What is there in the world that they cannot win? I told you before, as
soon as your son was born, what was good for you then: 'Give up this son, who
will harm the family.' You did not do it, king. Now I have told you what is good
for you, and if you do not do it, king, you will be sorry.

"If your son agrees to this, and is glad to share one kingdom with the Pandavas,
you will have no grief, for there will be friendship. That holds so long as you
do not take your son's part with your helpers. If he will not, restrain him, and
make the son of Pandu ruler. Let Ajatashatru, free from passion, rule this earth
by dharma, king. Then all the kings will wait on us at once, like vaishyas. Let
Duryodhana, Shakuni and the son of the suta serve the sons of Pandu in
friendship. Let Duhshasana beg the pardon of Bhimasena, and of Drupada's
daughter, in the middle of the hall. Comfort Yudhishthira, honour him, and set
him on the throne. You have asked me. What else can I say? When you have done
this, king, you will have done all you need to do."

"What you said in the hall, Vidura, when you had the Pandavas and me there,"
said Dhritarashtra, "and what you say now, is good for them and bad for my sons.
My mind cannot accept any of it. Why have you come to this settled view, that
you speak for the Pandavas' sake? It makes me think you do not wish me well. How
can I give up my son for the Pandavas? No doubt they are my sons too. But
Duryodhana came from my own body. Who that thought of fairness would say, 'Give
up your own body for the sake of another'? Everything you say to me is crooked,
Vidura, though I hold you in high honour. Go, or stay, as you like. An
unfaithful wife leaves, however kindly she is treated."

With that, Dhritarashtra rose suddenly and went into his private rooms, king.
Vidura said, "This is the end," and hurried away to where the Parthas were.

---

The Pandavas, bulls of the Bharatas, meaning to live in the forest, had set out
with their followers from the bank of the Ganga for Kurukshetra. They stayed by
the Sarasvati, the Drishadvati and the Yamuna, and went on from forest to
forest, always westwards. Then, on the bank of the Sarasvati, on the flat desert
lands, they saw the forest called Kamyaka, beloved of the sages. The heroes
lived there, in that forest full of beasts and birds, attended and comforted by
the sages, Bharata.

Vidura, longing to see the Pandavas, went with a single chariot to the rich
Kamyaka forest. He drove to the wood in a chariot drawn by swift horses, and saw
the Dharma King sitting in a lonely place with Draupadi, his brothers and the
brahmins.

That king, who keeps his word, saw Vidura coming quickly from far off, and said
to his brother Bhimasena, "What will the steward say to us when he comes? I hope
he is not coming at the word of the son of Subala, to call us to play again. I
hope that low Shakuni will not win our weapons from us too, in the gaming hall.
If anyone challenges me, saying, 'Come!', I cannot back away, Bhimasena. And if
Gandiva is ever put at risk, our winning back the kingdom will be at risk too."

Then all the Pandavas rose and received Vidura, king. Honoured by them, the
Ajamidha met the sons of Pandu as was proper. When Vidura had rested, those
bulls among men asked him why he had come. He told them at length how
Dhritarashtra, the son of Ambika, had behaved.

"Dhritarashtra asked me, Ajatashatru, when I was under his protection, and
honoured me," said Vidura. "'Now that things have come to this, be fair, and
tell me what is good for them and for me.' So I told him what was fitting for
the Kauravas, and good and wholesome for Dhritarashtra too. His mind would not
accept what was good for him, and I do not think anything else would be fitting.
I told him the highest good, Pandavas, and the son of Ambika did not listen to
me. Like wholesome food to a sick man, what I said did not please him. A fool
cannot be led to his own good, Ajatashatru, any more than a wicked wife in the
house of a learned brahmin. What I said did not please the bull of the Bharatas,
any more than a husband of sixty pleases a young girl. The ruin of the Kauravas
is certain, king. Dhritarashtra will not come to his own good. Good advice does
not stay with him, any more than water sprinkled on a lotus leaf.

"Then Dhritarashtra grew angry and said to me, 'Go where your faith is, Bharata.
I do not want you any more as my helper, to rule this earth or this city.' So I
have been cast off by Dhritarashtra, king, and I have come quickly to advise
you. Hold on to all I said in the hall, and to what I will say to you now. The
man who is afflicted with bitter hardships by his rivals, and waits patiently
for his time, tending himself like a small fire, will one day enjoy the earth
alone. The man whose wealth is not kept from his helpers, king, has helpers who
share in his sorrow too. This is the way to win helpers, and with helpers, they
say, one wins the earth. Truth is best, Pandava, and no idle talk. Share food
equally, eating with your helpers. Do not put yourself before them. The king who
lives like this prospers."

"I will do as you say," said Yudhishthira, "and I will keep to your wise counsel
with care. Whatever else is fitting for the time and place, tell me that too,
and I will do all of it."

---

When Vidura had gone to the Pandavas' hermitage, king, the wise Dhritarashtra
was tormented with remorse, Bharata. He came to the door of the hall, lost in
his longing for Vidura, and fell senseless before the kings. When he came to his
senses again and rose from the ground, the king spoke to Sanjaya, who stood near
him.

"My brother and my friend is dharma itself, in another form. When I think of him
today, my heart is ready to break. Bring me my brother, who knows dharma,
quickly." So the king spoke, and lamented piteously.

Burning with remorse, wasted with longing for Vidura, the king, out of love for
his brother, said this to Sanjaya. "Go, Sanjaya, and find out whether my brother
Vidura is alive, whom I in my wickedness drove away in anger. My wise brother,
of boundless understanding, never did me the least wrong. How has that wisest of
men come to suffer wrong from me? The wise man must not give up his life. Go and
bring him, Sanjaya."

When he heard the king's words, Sanjaya agreed. He said, "Very well," and
hurried to the Kamyaka forest. He soon reached the forest where the Pandavas
were, and saw Yudhishthira dressed in the skin of the ruru deer. He was sitting
with Vidura and with brahmins in their thousands, guarded by his brothers, like
Shatakratu by the gods. Sanjaya went up to Yudhishthira and honoured him, and he
greeted Bhima, Arjuna and the twins as was fitting. When the king had asked
after his health and he was seated at his ease, Sanjaya told why he had come,
and said this.

"King Dhritarashtra, the son of Ambika, is thinking of you, steward. Go quickly
and see him, and bring the king back to life. Take leave of these best of men,
the Pandavas, joys of the Kurus, and go, at the lion among kings' command, you
who give honour to others."

When he was told this, the wise Vidura, who loved his own people, went back to
the city named after the elephant, with Yudhishthira's leave. Mighty
Dhritarashtra said to the wise man, "By good fortune you have come, you who know
dharma. By good fortune you remember me, sinless one. Today, night and day, bull
of the Bharatas, I have lain awake because of you, and seen my body strangely
changed." He took Vidura on his lap and smelled his head. "Forgive me," he said,
"for what I said to you in anger."

"I have forgiven it already, king," said Vidura. "You are our highest elder.
That is why I have come so quickly, eager to see you. Men whose minds are set on
dharma take the side of the wretched, tiger among men. There is no need to think
about it. The sons of Pandu are to me what your sons are, king. But my mind has
turned to them today, because they are in need."

So the two brothers, both of great splendour, Vidura and Dhritarashtra, made
their peace with each other and were very glad.
