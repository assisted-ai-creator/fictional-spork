# Draupadi's Anger

Then one evening the Parthas, who had gone to the forest, sat with Krishna and
talked, sunk in sorrow and grief. Then Krishna, dear, lovely to look at,
learned, and devoted to her husbands, said this to the Dharma King.

"Surely that wicked, cruel, evil-minded son of Dhritarashtra feels no sorrow at
all for us. When he sent you to the forest, king, dressed in deerskins, with me
and with all your brothers, the evil-minded wretch did not say a word to you. He
felt no remorse. The heart of that doer of evil deeds must be made of iron,
since he spoke harsh words to you then, the best of men, devoted to dharma. You
were used to comfort and never deserved sorrow, and the evil-minded, wicked man
has brought this sorrow on you and is enjoying himself with his friends.

"When you went out to the forest dressed in deerskins, Bharata, only four wicked
men shed no tears: Duryodhana, Karna, evil-minded Shakuni, and that fierce, bad
brother, Duhshasana. From the eyes of all the other Kurus, best of the Kurus,
tears fell, for they were overcome with sorrow.

"When I see this bed, and remember the one you had before, I grieve for you,
great king, who never deserved sorrow and were used to comfort. When I see this
mat of kusha grass, and remember the ivory seat set with jewels in the middle of
the hall, grief chokes me. I used to see you in the hall surrounded by kings.
Now I do not see it, king, and what peace can my heart have? I used to see you
anointed with sandal paste, as bright as the sun. Now I see you smeared with mud
and dirt, and I faint, Bharata. I used to see you, king of kings, in fine white
silk clothes of great price. Now I see you in bark.

"Food, well cooked and fit for every taste, used to be carried out of your house
on golden dishes to brahmins by the thousand, king. Food of the finest kind was
given, my lord, to ascetics without homes and to householders alike. Now I do
not see it, king, and what peace can my heart have? Cooks used to feed your
brothers, great king, young men with polished earrings, on dainty food, finely
cooked. Now I see them all living on forest food in the forest. They never
deserved sorrow, lord of men, and my mind finds no rest.

"Do you not feel your anger grow when the time has come, when you see Bhimasena
here, unhappy, living in the forest, brooding? Why does your anger not grow when
you see Bhimasena, who deserves comfort, unhappy, doing his work with his own
hands, unfallen one? He was honoured with carriages of every kind and with fine
clothes of every sort. Why does your anger not grow when you see him in the
forest? Vrikodara here is able to kill all the Kurus, my lord, but he bears it,
waiting for your favour.

"Arjuna, with two arms, is the equal of Arjuna with many arms. In the speed of
his arrows he is like Time, the Ender, and Yama. By the power of his weapons all
the kings bowed down and waited on the brahmins at your sacrifice, great king.
Why does your anger not grow when you see this tiger among men, honoured by gods
and danavas, brooding? I faint, Bharata, when I see Partha, who never deserved
sorrow and was used to comfort, in the forest, and your anger does not grow. He
conquered gods, men and snakes with a single chariot. Why does your anger not
grow when you see him in the forest? The scourge of enemies, surrounded by
carriages of wonderful shape, by horses and elephants, took wealth by force from
the kings. He shoots five hundred arrows at a single stroke. Why does your anger
not grow when you see him in the forest?

"Why does your anger not grow when you see Nakula in the forest, dark, tall and
young, the best of shield-bearers in battle? Why does your anger not grow,
Yudhishthira, when you see the son of Madri, Sahadeva, handsome and brave, in
the forest? Why does your anger not grow when you see me in the forest, born in
Drupada's family, the daughter-in-law of great Pandu?

"Surely you have no anger at all, best of the Bharatas, since your mind is not
troubled when you see your brothers and me. There is no kshatriya in the world
without anger. That is what the word means. Yet today I see in you, a kshatriya,
the opposite. The kshatriya who does not show his fire when the time comes is
always despised by every creature, Partha. So you must never show patience
towards your enemies. They can only be killed by your fire, no doubt of it. And
in the same way, the kshatriya who is not calm when it is time for patience is
disliked by every creature and perishes, here and hereafter.

"On this they tell an old story, the talk between Prahlada and Bali, the son of
Virochana. Bali asked the lord of the asuras, the wise Prahlada, the daitya
king, his father's father, who had learned the traditions of dharma, 'Is
patience better, father, or is force? Tell me the truth of this doubt of mine,
father, for I ask you. Tell me for certain which is better here, you who know
dharma. I will do exactly as you teach me.'

"When he was asked this, the grandfather, wise, who knew every conclusion, told
him all of it, as he had asked.

"'Force is not always better, and patience is not always better, my son,' said
Prahlada. 'Know that both are so, without doubt. The man who is always patient,
my son, suffers many ills. His servants despise him, and so do those who stand
by. No creature ever bows to him. So the wise, my son, speak against being
always patient. His servants scorn him and fall into many faults, and the fools
try to take his wealth. Carriages, clothes, ornaments, beds and seats, food and
drink and all the things of the house, those in charge take for themselves as
they please, the fools. They do not give what they are told to give at their
master's command. They never honour him with the honour due to a master. And to
be scorned in this world is worse even than death.

"'People speak harshly even to such a patient man, my son, his servants, his
sons, his dependants and those who stand by. They despise the patient man and
even want his wives, and his wives, the fools, behave as they please. And those
who are always insolent, if they do not get even a little punishment from their
lord, grow wicked, and the wicked do him harm. These and many others are the
faults that always come to the patient.

"'Now, son of Virochana, learn the faults of the impatient. The man who is
always wrapped in passion, when he is angry, deals out punishments of every kind
by his own force, whether in the right place or the wrong. Wrapped in his fire,
he falls out with his friends, and he is hated by the world and by his own
people. By insulting others he meets with loss of wealth, reproach and
disrespect, suffering, hatred and greed, and enemies. A man who deals out
punishments of every kind among men in anger soon falls from his power, from his
life and from his own people. People fear the man who meets both those who harm
him and those who help him with force alone, as they fear a snake in the house.
How can a man prosper whom the world fears? The world is sure to turn on him the
moment it sees a weak point. So a man should not let loose his fire too much,
nor be always gentle. The man who is gentle when it is time, and fierce when it
is time, finds happiness in the next world and in this.

"'Now I will tell you in full the times for patience, as the wise say. They must
never be neglected. Listen. If a man who has done you a service before commits
an offence that is not grave, his offence should be forgiven because of his
service. Those who have offended out of ignorance should be forgiven, for wisdom
is not easy for everyone to come by. But if men do wrong knowingly and then say
they did it without knowing, those crooked, wicked men should be killed, even
for a small offence. Every creature's first offence should be forgiven. At the
second, a man should be punished, even for a small wrong. If a man commits an
offence without knowing it, they say it should be forgiven, when it has been
carefully tested. The gentle man kills gentleness with gentleness, and he kills
the harsh with gentleness too. There is nothing gentleness cannot do, and so
gentleness is the sharper. A man should look at the time and the place, and at
his own strength and weakness. Nothing succeeds out of its place and time. One
must wait for the place and the time. And a man should forgive an offender out
of fear of the people too. These are said to be the times for patience. When men
behave otherwise, it is called the time for force.'

"So I think, lord of men, that this is the time for your force, against the
greedy sons of Dhritarashtra, who always do us harm. There is no time for
patience today towards the Kurus. Now that the time for force has come, you
should let your force loose. The gentle man is despised, and people fear the
fierce. The man who knows both, when the time comes, is a true ruler of the
earth."

---

"Anger kills men, and anger makes them prosper too," said Yudhishthira. "Know,
wise lady, that rising and ruin both have their root in anger. The man who holds
back his anger prospers, lovely one. But for the man who never bears his anger,
good lady, anger becomes a terrible thing that ruins him. We see here that the
destruction of creatures has its root in anger. So how can a man like me let
loose anger, which destroys the world?

"An angry man may do evil. An angry man may kill even his elders. An angry man
insults even his betters with harsh words. When he is angry, a man does not know
what may be said and what may not. For an angry man there is nothing he may not
do and nothing he may not say. In his anger he may kill those who should not be
killed, and honour those who deserve death. An angry man may even send himself
to the house of Yama. The wise, who see these faults and want the highest good
here and the best hereafter, have conquered anger. How can a man like me give
way to anger, which the steadfast have cast off? Bearing this in mind, Draupadi,
my anger does not grow.

"The man who does not answer anger with anger saves himself and the other from
great danger. He is the healer of them both. If a foolish, weak man, when he is
wronged, grows angry with stronger men, he throws himself away in the end. The
worlds are lost to that man who throws himself away. So, Draupadi, it is taught
that the weak man should restrain his anger. And the wise man who is strong, and
does not grow angry when he is wronged, destroys the one who wrongs him and
rejoices in the next world. So they say that a man who understands should always
be patient, whether he is strong or weak, even in misfortune.

"The good praise the conquest of anger here, Krishna. The good hold that victory
always belongs to the patient, good man. Truth is better than falsehood, and
kindness better than cruelty. How could a man like me let loose anger, which has
so many faults and which the good avoid, even to kill Suyodhana?

"It is settled that the man whom the far-sighted wise call full of fire has no
anger inside him. The wise, who see the truth, think that man full of fire who
holds back the anger that has arisen by his understanding. An angry man does not
see rightly what should be done, lovely-hipped one. An angry man sees neither
what should be done nor the bounds. An angry man kills those who should not be
killed, and wounds his elders with harsh words. So when force must be used,
anger should stand far off. Skill, resolve, courage and speed are the qualities
of force, and a man overcome by anger cannot easily win them. Only when he has
given up anger does a man truly come to force. Force joined with the right time,
wise lady, is very hard for the angry to bear. Fools always call anger force.
But it is passion, made for the ruin of the world and against men. So a man who
acts rightly should always give up anger. It is certain that a man who does not
stray from his own dharma is better than one who is angry.

"If all the fools and the witless go too far, blameless one, how can a man like
me go too far? If there were no men among men as patient as the earth, there
would be no peace among men, for war has its root in anger. The one who is
struck would strike, and the one struck by his elder would strike back. So
creatures would be destroyed, and adharma would spread. Every man who was abused
would abuse back at once. He would strike back when struck, and harm when
harmed. Fathers would kill their sons, and sons their fathers. Husbands would
kill their wives, and wives their husbands. In a world so enraged, Krishna,
there would be no birth. For birth, lovely-faced one, has its root in peace
between creatures. All creatures would soon perish in such a world, Draupadi. So
anger leads to the ruin and the undoing of creatures. But since we see in the
world men as patient as the earth, there is birth and there is thriving for
creatures. A man should be patient here in every misfortune, good lady. For
patience, they say, is the thriving and the birth of creatures.

"The man who is abused and struck and enraged by a stronger man, and still
forgives, and the wise man who has always conquered anger, is the best of men.
Even if he is a man of power, the eternal worlds are his. But the angry man of
little understanding perishes, in this world and the next.

"On this they recite the verses of the patient, always sung by the great and
patient Kashyapa, Krishna.

"'Patience is dharma. Patience is sacrifice. Patience is the Vedas. Patience is
what is heard. The man who knows this can forgive everything. Patience is
brahman. Patience is truth. Patience is what has been and what will be. Patience
is austerity. Patience is purity. By patience the world is held up. The patient
reach worlds beyond the worlds of those who know brahman, beyond those of the
ascetics, beyond those of the knowers of sacrifice. Patience is the fire of the
fiery, the brahman of the ascetics, the truth of the truthful. Patience is
giving. Patience is fame.'

"How can a man like me give up such patience, Krishna, in which brahman and
truth, sacrifices and worlds, all rest? The worlds of those who sacrifice are
enjoyed, and the patient have others. A man who understands should always be
patient. When a man forgives everything, he attains brahman. This world belongs
to the patient, and the next world too. Here they win honour, and hereafter a
good end. Those men whose anger is always struck down by patience have the
higher worlds. So patience is held to be supreme.

"These are the verses of the patient, always sung by Kashyapa. Now that you have
heard the verses of patience, Draupadi, be content. Do not be angry. The
grandfather, the son of Shantanu, will honour peace. The teacher, and Vidura the
steward, will speak for peace. Kripa and Sanjaya will speak for peace. Somadatta
and Yuyutsu, and the son of Drona too, and our grandfather Vyasa always speak
for peace. Urged on towards peace by these, the king will surely give back the
kingdom. That is what I think. If not, he will perish through greed. A terrible
time has come for the ruin of the Bharatas. I have always been certain of this,
even from the first, spirited one. Suyodhana does not deserve patience, and so
he cannot find it. I deserve it, and so patience finds me. This is the way of
the self-possessed. This is the eternal dharma. Patience and kindness: I will
practise them truly."
