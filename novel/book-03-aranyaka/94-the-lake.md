summary: Back at Dvaitavana, a deer carries off a brahmin's fire-sticks and the Pandavas chase it in vain; resting under a banyan, each brother names the wrong that brought them to this; Nakula, Sahadeva, Arjuna and Bhima go in turn to a lake for water, ignore a voice that forbids them to drink before answering its questions, and fall dead; Yudhishthira comes, hears the voice of a crane that says it is a yaksha, and answers its questions one by one; offered the life of one brother, he chooses Nakula, the son of Madri, and the yaksha grants him all four.

# The Lake

"When Krishna had been carried off like this," said Janamejaya, "and they had
suffered that terrible hardship, and had won her back, what did the Pandavas
do?"

When Krishna had been carried off like this, and they had suffered that terrible
hardship, King Yudhishthira, the unfailing, left Kamyaka with his brothers and
came back to lovely Dvaitavana, rich in sweet roots and fruits, to Markandeya's
hermitage. There the Pandavas lived with Krishna, Bharata, all of them eating
little, living on fruits they gathered. As they lived at Dvaitavana, King
Yudhishthira son of Kunti, Bhimasena and Arjuna, and the two Pandavas, sons of
Madri, whose selves were dharma, restrained in their vows, bold for the
brahmins' sake, scorchers of enemies, met with great hardship that would end in
happiness.

A brahmin came quickly to Ajatashatru as he sat in the forest with his brothers,
and said in distress, "My fire-sticks were hanging on a tree, and they caught on
the antlers of a deer that was rubbing itself against it. The great deer has
taken them and gone off quickly from the hermitage, king, leaping along at great
speed. Follow its track quickly, Pandavas, catch the great deer, and bring them
back, so that my agnihotra is not lost."

When he heard the brahmin's words, Yudhishthira son of Kunti was distressed,
took his bow and ran off with his brothers. All the bulls among men, armed, with
their bows, ran, and striving for the brahmin's sake, they quickly followed the
deer. The great chariot fighters loosed barbed arrows, reed arrows and iron
arrows, but the Pandavas could not pierce the deer, though they saw it close by.
As they strove, the great deer vanished from sight. Not seeing the deer, the
high-minded ones were tired and downcast. They came to a banyan tree with cool
shade in the thick forest, and the Pandavas sat down, their limbs overcome with
hunger and thirst.

As they sat there, Nakula, in distress, said to his eldest brother in
resentment, best of the Kurus, "In this family dharma has never sunk, and no
wealth was ever lost through idleness. We are second to none among all beings.
Why have we come into such danger again, king?"

"There is no limit to misfortunes," said Yudhishthira, "no sign and no cause.
Dharma divides here between the two, merit and sin."

"The usher dragged Krishna into the hall like a servant," said Bhima, "and I did
not kill him there. That is why we have come into danger."

"The sharp words that cut to the bone, spoken by the suta's son," said Arjuna,
"very sharp as they were, I bore them. That is why we have come into danger."

"When Shakuni beat you at the dice, Bharata," said Sahadeva, "I did not kill him
there. That is why we have come into danger."

Then King Yudhishthira said to Nakula, "Climb a tree, son of Madri, and look in
all ten directions. Look for water nearby, or for trees that grow near water.
These brothers of yours are tired and thirsty, my friend."

"So be it," said Nakula, and climbed a tree quickly. Looking all round, he said
to his eldest brother, "I see many trees that grow near water, king, and I hear
the cries of cranes. There is water here, no doubt of it."

Then Yudhishthira son of Kunti, firm in truth, said, "Go there quickly, gentle
one, and bring water at once."

"So be it," said Nakula, and at his eldest brother's command he ran to where the
water was, and soon reached it. He saw the clear water surrounded by cranes, and
as he was about to drink, he heard a voice from the sky. "Do not be rash, my
son. This has long been mine. Answer my questions, son of Madri, and then drink
and take it away." Heedless of those words, very thirsty, Nakula drank the cool
water, and when he had drunk, he fell down.

---

When Nakula was a long time coming, Yudhishthira son of Kunti said to his heroic
brother Sahadeva, the tamer of enemies, "Your elder brother is a long time,
Sahadeva, my friend. Bring back your brother, and bring water too."

"So be it," said Sahadeva, and went in that direction. He saw his brother Nakula
lying dead on the ground. Burning with grief for his brother and tormented by
thirst, he ran to the water, and then the voice spoke. "Do not be rash, my son.
This has long been mine. Answer my questions as you will, and then drink and
take it away." Heedless of those words, thirsty, Sahadeva drank the cool water,
and when he had drunk, he fell down.

Then Yudhishthira son of Kunti said to Vijaya, "Your two brothers have been gone
a long time, Bibhatsu, crusher of enemies. Bless you. Bring them back, and bring
water too."

At this Gudakesha took his bow and arrows, bound on his sword, and went to that
lake, the wise one. The one with white horses saw his two brothers, tigers among
men, who had gone to fetch water, lying there dead. Seeing them as if asleep,
the lion among men was deeply grieved. The son of Kunti raised his bow and
looked all over that forest. He saw no creature in that great forest. Then
Savyasachin, who was tired, ran to the water. As he ran, he heard a voice from
the sky. "Why do you come near the water? You cannot take it by force. If you
answer the questions I ask, son of Kunti, then you shall drink the water and
take it away, Bharata."

Stopped like this, Partha said, "Stop me when you can be seen. Pierced by my
arrows, you will not speak like this again." When he had said this, Partha
rained arrows charged with spells over that whole direction, showing his skill
at shooting at sound. He loosed barbed arrows, reed arrows and iron arrows, bull
of the Bharatas, and rained floods of arrows over the sky.

"What use is striking out, Partha?" said the yaksha. "Answer the questions and
then drink. If you drink without answering the questions, you will not live."

But when he had loosed his unfailing arrows, tormented by thirst, he drank
without heeding the questions, and when he had drunk, he fell down.

Then Yudhishthira son of Kunti said to Bhimasena, "Nakula and Sahadeva, and
Bibhatsu, never beaten, have gone a long time for water, and they do not come
back, Bharata. Bring them back, bless you, and bring water too."

"So be it," said Bhimasena, and went in that direction, where those tigers among
men, his brothers, had fallen. When he saw them, Bhima was grieved, and
tormented by thirst. The strong-armed one thought it the work of yakshas or
rakshasas, and thought, "Surely I must fight today. First I shall drink the
water." Then Partha Vrikodara, the bull among men, ran to the water, thirsty.

"Do not be rash, my son," said the yaksha. "This has long been mine. Answer my
questions, son of Kunti, and then drink and take it away."

Though the yaksha of boundless splendour said this to him, Bhima drank without
heeding the questions, and when he had drunk, he fell down.

---

Then the king, son of Kunti, thought it over, and the strong-armed bull among
men rose with a burning heart. He went into the great forest, where no human
voice was heard, haunted by ruru deer, boars and birds, adorned with trees dark
blue and shining, sung to by bees and birds. As he went through that forest, the
glorious one saw the lake, bordered with golden nets, as if made by
Vishvakarman, set with beds of lotuses, with sindhuvara, reeds, ketakas,
karaviras and pippalas all round. Tired, he came to it, and when he saw the lake
he was amazed.

He saw his brothers struck down, like the guardians of the world fallen when the
end of an age has come, each as mighty as Shakra. He saw Arjuna struck down with
his bow and arrows scattered, and Bhimasena and the twins motionless, their
lives gone. He sighed long and hot, drowned in tears of grief, and thought in
his mind, "Who has struck down these heroes? There is no mark of a weapon on
them, and no footprint of anyone here. I think this is some great being by whom
my brothers have been killed. I shall think this through with a single mind, or
I shall drink the water and know.

"Perhaps this is something done in secret by Duryodhana, planned by the king of
Gandhara, whose mind is always crooked. Who that is brave could trust that
wicked one, who has not mastered himself, to whom right and wrong are the same?
Or perhaps this is a plot of the wicked one through hidden agents." So the
strong-armed one thought in many ways. "This water has not been spoiled by
poison, for my brothers' faces are clear in colour," he thought. "Who else could
stand against these best of men, each with the strength of a flood, except Yama,
the Ender at the time of death?" With this resolve he went down into the water.
As he went into the water, he heard a voice from the sky.

"I am a crane that lives on water weed and fish," said the yaksha. "It was I who
brought your younger brothers under the power of death. You will be the fifth,
prince, if you do not answer the questions I ask. Do not be rash, my son. This
has long been mine. Answer my questions, son of Kunti, and then drink and take
it away."

"Are you the chief of the Rudras, or the Vasus, or the Maruts?" said
Yudhishthira. "I ask, what god are you? This was not done by a bird. Who has
struck down to the ground these four mountains, the Himalaya, the Pariyatra, the
Vindhya and the Malaya, by his energy? You have done a very great deed, best of
the mighty. What neither gods, nor gandharvas, nor asuras, nor rakshasas could
bear in a great battle, you have done, a great wonder. I do not know your
purpose, and I do not know what you want. A great curiosity has come over me,
and fear too, by which my heart is troubled and my head burns. So I ask you,
lord, who are you who stand here?"

"I am a yaksha, bless you," said the yaksha. "I am not a bird that lives in the
water. I have struck down all these brothers of yours, great as they were."

When he heard the unlucky, harsh words of the yaksha, king, he went up to where
he stood. The bull of the Bharatas saw the yaksha standing on the bank, with
strange eyes, a great body, as tall as a palm tree, blazing like fire and the
sun, not to be overcome, like a mountain, threatening in a voice as deep as the
clouds, very mighty.

"These brothers of yours, king," said the yaksha, "though I stopped them again
and again, tried to take the water by force, and so I killed them. This water
must not be drunk here, king, by anyone who wants to keep his life. Do not be
rash, Partha. This has long been mine. Answer my questions, son of Kunti, and
then drink and take it away."

"I do not want what has long been yours, yaksha," said Yudhishthira. "The good
never praise that kind of wanting, lord, when a man praises himself. I shall
answer your questions as well as I understand. Ask me."

---

"What makes the sun rise?" said the yaksha. "Who move about it? What makes it
set? In what does it stand firm?"

"Brahman makes the sun rise," said Yudhishthira. "The gods move about it. Dharma
makes it set. And it stands firm in truth."

"By what does one become learned in the Veda? By what does one win the great? By
what does one have a companion, king? And by what is one wise?"

"By learning one becomes learned in the Veda. By austerity one wins the great.
By steadfastness one has a companion. And one is wise by serving the old."

"What is the godhood of brahmins? What is their dharma, as it were of the good?
What is their human state? What is in them as it were of the bad?"

"Study is their godhood. Austerity is their dharma, as of the good. Death is
their human state. Slander is in them as of the bad."

"What is the godhood of kshatriyas? What is their dharma, as it were of the
good? What is their human state? What is in them as it were of the bad?"

"Arrows and weapons are their godhood. Sacrifice is their dharma, as of the
good. Fear is their human state. Abandoning those who seek shelter is in them as
of the bad."

"What is the one sacrificial saman? What is the one sacrificial yajus? What is
the one thing that cuts the sacrifice, which the sacrifice does not go beyond?"

"Breath is the sacrificial saman. Mind is the sacrificial yajus. Speech alone
cuts the sacrifice, and the sacrifice does not go beyond it."

"What is best of the things that fall? What is best of the things that are sown?
What is best of the things that stand firm? What is best of those who speak?"

"Rain is best of the things that fall. Seed is best of the things that are sown.
Cows are best of the things that stand firm. A son is best of those who speak."

"Who, enjoying the objects of the senses, wise, honoured by the world, respected
by all creatures, breathes and yet does not live?"

"He who makes no offering to these five, the gods, guests, dependants, the
ancestors and himself, breathes and yet does not live."

"What is heavier than the earth? What is higher than the sky? What is swifter
than the wind? What is more numerous than men?"

"A mother is heavier than the earth. A father is higher than the sky. The mind
is swifter than the wind. Cares are more numerous than men."

"What sleeps and does not close its eyes? What does not move once it is born?
What has no heart? What grows by its own force?"

"A fish sleeps and does not close its eyes. An egg does not move once it is
born. A stone has no heart. A river grows by its own force."

"What is the friend of the traveller? What is the friend of one at home? What is
the friend of the sick? What is the friend of one about to die?"

"A caravan is the friend of the traveller. A wife is the friend of one at home.
A physician is the friend of the sick. Giving is the friend of one about to
die."

"What moves alone? What is born again? What is the remedy for cold? What is the
great field for sowing?"

"The sun moves alone. The moon is born again. Fire is the remedy for cold. The
earth is the great field for sowing."

"What in a single word is dharma? What in a single word is fame? What in a
single word leads to heaven? What in a single word is happiness?"

"Skill in a single word is dharma. Giving in a single word is fame. Truth in a
single word leads to heaven. Good conduct in a single word is happiness."

"What is a man's self? What is the friend the gods have made for him? What does
he live on? What is his last refuge?"

"A son is a man's self. A wife is the friend the gods have made for him. He
lives on the rain cloud. Giving is his last refuge."

"What is best of the things that are praised? What is best of wealth? What is
best of gains? And what is best of happiness?"

"Skill is best of the things that are praised. Learning is best of wealth.
Health is best of gains. Contentment is best of happiness."

"What is the highest dharma in the world? What dharma always bears fruit? What,
when restrained, leaves men without grief? With whom does an alliance never wear
out?"

"Non-cruelty is the highest dharma. The dharma of the three Vedas always bears
fruit. The mind, when restrained, leaves men without grief. An alliance with the
good never wears out."

"Giving up what does one become loved? Giving up what does one not grieve?
Giving up what does one become rich? Giving up what is one happy?"

"Giving up pride one becomes loved. Giving up anger one does not grieve. Giving
up desire one becomes rich. Giving up greed one is happy."

"How is a man dead? How is a kingdom dead? How is a shraddha dead? How is a
sacrifice dead?"

"A poor man is dead. A kingdom without a king is dead. A shraddha without a
brahmin learned in the Veda is dead. A sacrifice without fees is dead."

"What is the direction? What is called water? What is food, Partha, and what is
poison? Tell me the time for the shraddha, and then drink and take it away."

"The good are the direction. Space is water. The cow is food. Begging is poison.
A brahmin is the time for the shraddha. Or what do you think, yaksha?"

"You have answered my questions truly, scorcher of enemies," said the yaksha.
"Now tell me what a man is, and who is the man who has all wealth."

"The sound of a good deed touches heaven and earth," said Yudhishthira. "As long
as that sound lasts, so long is he called a man. He to whom the dear and the not
dear are equal, and happiness and sorrow, and past and future, is the man who
has all wealth."

"You have told what a man is, king, and who is the man who has all wealth," said
the yaksha. "So let one of your brothers live, whichever you wish."

"This dark one with red eyes, tall as a great sal tree, broad-chested and
strong-armed," said Yudhishthira, "let Nakula live, yaksha."

"This Bhimasena is dear to you," said the yaksha, "and Arjuna is the refuge of
you all. Why, king, do you want the life of Nakula, a son of another mother? You
pass over Bhima, whose strength equals ten thousand elephants, and want Nakula
to live? People say this Bhimasena is dear to you. By what reasoning do you want
the life of a son of another mother? You set aside Arjuna, on the strength of
whose arms all the Pandavas rely, and want Nakula to live?"

"Non-cruelty is the highest dharma," said Yudhishthira, "higher, I think, than
the highest gain. I wish to do what is not cruel. Let Nakula live, yaksha.
People know me as a king always devoted to dharma. I will not move from my own
dharma. Let Nakula live, yaksha. As Kunti is, so is Madri. I make no difference
between them. I want the same for both mothers. Let Nakula live, yaksha."

"Since you hold non-cruelty higher than gain and desire," said the yaksha, "let
all your brothers live, bull of the Bharatas."

**Divergences.**

* **The four famous questions.** The best-known questions of the yaksha, "Who is happy?", "What is the greatest wonder?" ("Every day creatures go to Yama's house, and those who are left want to live for ever"), "What is the path?" ("the path is the one the great have taken") and "What is the news?", are in a rejected appendix passage (@32). They are not in the CE and are not narrated. See L-161.
* **"Dharma protects those who protect it."** A rejected line in Yudhishthira's reply (\*1385) has "dharma, when destroyed, destroys; dharma, when protected, protects". Not narrated here. The second half is in the CE elsewhere, in Draupadi's words (3.31.7). See L-162.
* **Durvasas.** A rejected appendix passage (@30), placed at the start of this section, tells of Durvasas's visit to the Pandavas. Not narrated. See L-24.
* **Yudhishthira's lament.** A rejected appendix passage (@31) has Yudhishthira lament over each brother at length. Not narrated. In the CE he grieves and reasons (3.297.3–10).
* Other rejected lines (\*1356–\*1384, \*1386) add further questions, Nakula's own grievance, and other details. Not narrated.
